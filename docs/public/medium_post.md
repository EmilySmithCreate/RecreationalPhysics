# Did space snap open?

*A hobbyist's test of an idea about where space came from — with the code, the predictions written
down in advance, and the results that didn't go my way.*

Draft for Medium, 2026-09-22. Paste as-is; the links are marked. Keep the disclosure paragraph.

---

I am a software engineer, not a physicist. For the past few weeks I have been testing an idea I
can't shake, using somebody else's published model and an ordinary laptop. This is what came back,
including the parts that went against me.

## The idea

Space isn't the stage everything happens on. It is one settled arrangement of something deeper,
which I call X because nobody knows what it is. X was curled up, stable for now, like a chemical
that will react if something gives it a push. When the push came, it opened out — and the energy it
let go became everything in it.

That's the shape of a chemical reaction, and of water freezing, and of a lot of other things nature
does. The question is whether space could have done the same.

## Things change in two ways

Cool water and nothing much happens until 0 °C. Then it freezes all at once, gives off a burst of
heat, and can even be caught waiting: very clean water stays liquid below zero until something jolts
it. Heat a magnet instead, and its pull just fades away. Nothing is released, nothing waits.

Physicists call these the sharp kind and the smooth kind. My idea needs the sharp kind, because the
burst of energy is what became the matter and light of the universe.

## What I did

You can't run an experiment on the universe, so I used a published model of space as a network of
dots and links, with no positions in it at all. Each dot has exactly four links, short loops earn
points, and a computer reshuffles the links millions of times to see what pattern settles.

Before testing my own idea, I reproduced what the model's authors had already found. Our code lands
on their published curve to within half a percent, and reproduces the sharp change they report at
one setting. At the smallest sizes I checked it against an exhaustive list of every possible
arrangement — about 1.8 trillion of them.

Then I wrote down my predictions, and what would count as failure, before running anything.

## What came back

**The question most researchers ask came back smooth.** Does space condense sharply out of random
chaos? In this model, no: one hump instead of two, and any burst of energy that could be hiding is
small and shrinking as the network grows. That matches the published work.

**The change my idea actually describes came back sharp.** Start the network as a curled-up tube,
and it waits — hundreds to thousands of rounds — then opens out into flat space. It does it by a
seed and a spreading front, and gives off exactly the energy worked out in advance. Halfway through,
99% of the network is either still tube or already flat, with almost nothing in between. That's what
"sharp" means, and all three tests were written before the runs.

**Sealed in a box, it's a bonfire, not slush.** Let the released heat stay inside and the change
either carries through to clean flat space or, with too little room, melts the space it just made.
Of 420 runs: 179 clean, 154 melted, 87 in between, and none stuck half-and-half. Energy balanced to
the last unit in every run.

**And a prediction of mine failed.** I predicted the leftover — a scrap of the old arrangement that
the front sweeps past — would grow with the size of the space. It doesn't. One small scrap per run,
however big the box. I'm reporting that as loudly as the rest, because that was the deal.

## What this does and doesn't show

It shows the mechanism is possible: a curled-up arrangement really can open out sharply and release
a definite lump of energy, in a model somebody else published, with nothing added to make it happen.

It does not show that this is how our universe began. The networks are small, at most a few hundred
points. The stand-in for X is my own choice. Nothing here touches time, quantum behaviour or
relativity. And no physicist has reviewed any of it.

There's an older warning, too. In 1981 Alan Guth proposed almost exactly this shape — a sharp change
whose released energy became the matter of the universe — and it was abandoned, because the bubbles
of new space never merged into one smooth universe. Whether my version escapes that problem is the
first thing I'd want a physicist to tell me.

## How it was kept honest

Every prediction was committed to a file before the runs that tested it. Result files are never
edited. Every setting that was run is reported, including the ones with unwelcome answers. Two of my
own test rules turned out to be badly written and had to be corrected after the fact — that's
recorded too, right next to the verdicts they changed.

All the code and the write-ups were drafted with an AI assistant (Claude), in conversation with me.
I checked what I could against the original papers and against exact answers at small sizes, but
this is unreviewed work by a hobbyist, and it should be read that way.

## Where it goes next

The tests are already written down: plant several seeds and see whether each leaves a scrap; find
where the curled arrangement stops being stable; measure whether what it opens into really behaves
like space; look for a speed limit; see whether the leftover scraps attract one another, which would
be the first hint of gravity. And ask the model's authors a couple of questions I can't answer from
their papers.
