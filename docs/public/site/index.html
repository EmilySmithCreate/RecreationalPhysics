Did space snap open?

An idea about where space came from: that it snapped open out of something curled up, and the energy it let go became everything in it. What the idea says, what a computer model showed, and what it would mean.

Emily Smith, with Claude (Anthropic) · Version 1, 22 September 2026 · Not yet reviewed by a physicist

The code and every result file → Full methods and results (paper) — coming soon

The short answer

  • In a computer model, a curled-up arrangement opened out suddenly into a flat, space-like one. It gave off exactly the burst of energy worked out in advance, and the energy books balanced to the last unit. That is the change the idea describes, working.
  • It passed every test of sharpness we wrote down before the runs. One prediction failed: the leftover scrap doesn't grow as the space grows, so in this model it stays tiny. The idea doesn't need it to: the lump is what carries the energy.
  • What's ahead are the bigger tests: much larger networks, getting known physics out of it, and a physicist's review. Those are what would turn a working mechanism into an account of our universe.

Background

Things change in two ways

Settled science Cool water and nothing much happens until it reaches 0 °C. Then it freezes all at once, giving off heat as it goes, with ice and water side by side. Very clean water can even stay liquid below zero until something jolts it. It is stuck behind a hill it can't get over on its own.

Heat a fridge magnet instead and its pull just fades away to nothing. There is no hill, no burst of heat and no moment when half of it has changed. Physicists call the first kind of change first-order (sharp) and the second continuous (smooth).

How do you tell which kind you have? Settled science Let the system jiggle for a long time and count how often it turns up at each energy. That count is the statistical view of the same landscape, seen from underneath.

Settled science Two things make this hard to measure. Small systems hide the hill: with only a few pieces, everything can flip at once, so even a sharp change can look like one broad hump. The real test is whether the valley deepens as the system gets bigger. And not every change is either kind. The best-understood changes in the early universe were gradual handovers with no sharp moment at all. So the kind of change has to be measured, not assumed.

The idea

Space as a change of arrangement

The idea Space isn't the stage everything happens on. It is one settled arrangement of something deeper, which we call X because nobody knows what it is. X isn't random chaos. It is a specific arrangement that is stable for now, like a chemical that will react if something gives it a push. In this picture X is curled up, with fewer large directions than space has, and the change uncurls it.

The idea has six parts, called claims. They are numbered because each can hold or fail on its own. The icons come back later, wherever a result speaks to that claim.

  1. Claim 1

    Space is one settled arrangement of something deeper, X.

  2. Claim 2

    Whatever X is, known physics (relativity, quantum mechanics) has to come back out of it.

  3. Claim 3

    X and space are two arrangements of the same stuff. One can turn into the other.

  4. Claim 4

    The change was sharp (two humps, not one) and gave off a lump of energy that became matter and light.

  5. Claim 5

    Not everything converted. A leftover remains: either leftover X, or a different arrangement, called X2.

  6. Claim 6

    The energy books balance across the change.

This is the author's proposal. It is not established, not published, and not tested except as described below.

The test

A toy universe made of links

You can't test this on the universe, so we tested it on a published model by the physicists Carlo Trugenberger, Christy Kelly and Fabio Biancalana. The model is a network of dots and links, and it has no positions at all. Each dot is linked to exactly four others. Small closed loops of links earn points. A computer reshuffles the links millions of times and we watch what pattern settles. Nothing in the model knows about space, so if something space-like appears, it appeared by itself.

Counting loops tells you the network's shape from the inside. It can settle as a flat sheet, a tube (one direction curled up), or a closed knot. The tube is our stand-in for X: a specific curled-up arrangement, stable for now, and higher in energy than the sheet. It is a stand-in, not X.

Why the sizes are small, and why these ones. The networks here have 36 to 288 points. A tube is always 4 points around, the tightest curl the network can make, and its length sets the size: 16, 24, 36, 48 or 72 points long gives 64, 96, 144, 192 or 288 points. Each experiment's sizes were fixed in its written plan before it ran, and every size we ran is shown. The smaller ones repeat earlier experiments so results can be compared; the largest is as far as run time on one laptop allows.

Before testing the idea's own change, we repeated the published work: the same model, the same question, and the same kind of measurement other researchers use (the two-humps test above). If our tools couldn't reproduce their answers, nothing new from them would be worth trusting.

Matches a published curve

Our simulation lands on the model's published 2019 curve at the same size, typically within 0.005.

Reproduces a published sharp change

Matches exact answers

At the smallest sizes we listed every possible arrangement, about 1.8 trillion, and the simulation matches the exact averages.

One figure we can't match

A 2025 figure from the same group differs from ours in one middle range. It also disagrees with their 2019 curve, so that's a question for the authors.

The question the published researchers ask is whether space forms suddenly out of a random tangle of links. Here is the lump we measured at several sizes.

Our result Other researchers We got the same answer they did. With one penalty switched off, the change is sharp, with a lump that grows with size, but the network ends up as separate knots, not space. With the full rule, which does make space, the change is smooth. We added one thing: a comparison across several sizes, which the original paper said was out of reach. The smooth result held.

What this means for the idea

Space doesn't condense sharply out of random chaos in this model, at these sizes. That isn't the idea's question, since X is a specific arrangement rather than chaos, so it neither supports nor rules out the idea. What it does show is that our tools give the same answers as the published work, which is why the results that follow are worth taking seriously.

Result 1

Speaks toClaim 3Claim 4

One arrangement into another: sharp

This is the change the idea actually describes. We start the network as a perfect tube and hold it at a fixed temperature. The tube sits 1 energy unit per point above a flat sheet, and at first it stays put.

Each run waits, then opens out into a flat sheet within a few hundred rounds. It gives off exactly the energy worked out beforehand by arithmetic, 1 unit per point. Some runs pause part-way on a small leftover (the dotted line) before they finish. Before running the main test, we wrote down three things "sharp" would have to mean:

Held

Starts by chance

The waiting time is random, like a radioactive atom, not a timer running down.

Held

99% one or the other

Halfway through, 99% of points are either still tube or already sheet. Almost nothing is in between.

Held

One front

The new space grows as one connected region spreading along the tube, not as scattered patches.

Our result All three held at four sizes (64 to 192 points), on two independent sets of 30 runs each.

The formal verdict: a sharp change, in two steps. That is the pre-registered verdict at 64, 96 and 192 points. Twelve runs had come to rest on rarer leftovers, so we replayed them, saved the final network and read what each one is. Eleven matched their energy exactly. One, at 144 points, changed state in the very last stretch of its run, so that size is set aside, though the three tests above hold there too.

What this means for the idea

This is the core of what we were looking for. A curled-up arrangement opens out, one direction becoming large, and gives off a definite lump of energy. It happens sharply, with every piece claim 4 asks for: stable for now, a push, a seed, a spreading front and an exact lump. And it happens in a published model, with nothing added to make it happen.

One thing it isn't: energy from nowhere. The energy was stored in the curled shape and let go, and the books balance.

Result 2

Speaks toClaim 4Claim 6

In a sealed box: a bonfire, not slush

In the runs above, a heat bath carried the lump away instantly. The idea is about what happens when it can't. So we sealed the box: no energy gets in or out, and the lump has to go somewhere inside. Would it feed the change like a bonfire, or smother it into half-and-half slush?

None ended as slush: part tube and part sheet, sitting still, with nothing melting. Our reasoning said slush was impossible, because it needs a temperature at which tube and sheet are equally happy and no such temperature exists. A single case would have shown that reasoning wrong.

Our result A bonfire, with a threshold. With enough room for the heat, the tube converts completely into a clean sheet. With too little, the heat melts the new space it has just made — melts meaning the points lose their neat two directions and go back to being connected any old way, which is the disordered state space came out of. The change undoes itself. At every size the switch-over falls between 1 place per 4 points and 1 per 2, where we had predicted 1 per 3.5. It moves in step with size, as predicted. Energy balanced to the last unit in every run, which is claim 6 holding in the model.

Result 3

Speaks toClaim 5

The leftover: one small scrap, however big

Almost every sealed sheet kept a leftover: four points of tube that the spreading front swept past without uncurling.

Why we expected it to grow. Freeze water quickly and air bubbles get trapped in the ice, and a bigger block traps more bubbles. If the spreading front left scraps behind at a steady rate as it swept along, a longer tube would end with more of them. The leftover would then be a fixed share of the space, like bubbles in ice, and could add up to a real part of a universe's contents. We wrote that prediction down before measuring.

Our result It didn't grow. There was about one scrap per box at every size from 64 to 288 points, like a single bubble however big the block. Our prediction failed, and we report it as prominently as the ones that held. A rarer second kind of leftover also turned up: a small twist, in 9 of 80 boxes. Why there is exactly one scrap is still unknown.

What this means for the idea

What "one scrap" means at scale. A scrap is 4 points. In a 288-point box that is 1.4% of the points. In a box ten times bigger it would be 0.14%. If a whole universe opened from a single seed, its leftover would be one grain of sand on a beach. The idea doesn't need more: what matters is that the lump can carry the energy. A leftover too small to notice also fits what we see, since no relic defects from the early universe have been found.

What it doesn't undo. The energy given off and the opening of a direction both do grow with size: a bigger tube gives a bigger lump. That is Result 1, and it stands. Only the leftover stays small.

Our reading, unreviewed One way the leftover could still matter: if a real conversion started from many separate seeds, and each left a scrap. Surprisingly few might be needed for dark matter; see "Is the leftover dark matter?" below. Our tubes almost always opened from a single seed, so this is untested.

Putting it together

What the model says about X, X2 and the lump

The idea names three things: X, the leftover (X2), and the lump of energy released between them. The model has a working stand-in for each: the tube for X, the four-point scrap for the leftover, and the measured burst for the lump. Here is how they behave, and how they fit together.

X

The tube sits a fixed amount above space: 1 unit per point at the setting we used. It is stable for now only inside a window. At rule setting 1.25 it waits hundreds to thousands of rounds. At 1.5, where the lump per point would be twice as big, it falls apart within 50 rounds. So in this model, a bigger lump comes with a less stable X. Exactly where the window closes hasn't been measured yet.

The lump

The lump is exactly the energy gap between X and space. We worked it out by arithmetic before running, and then measured it. It grows in step with the size of X. What happens next depends on where it can go. With enough room it leaves clean space; with too little it melts the space it just made.

X2, the leftover

What's usually left is a scrap of X itself, not a new kind of arrangement. In claim 5's terms, that is the "leftover X" option. The only candidates for a genuinely separate X2 are rarer leftovers: a small twist in some sealed boxes, and a handful of other resting states that turned out, once read, to be combinations of small defects in the sheet. All of them are small and fixed in size. The author's hypothesis is that the leftover is dark matter; see below.

Between them

The push stays fixed while the lump grows. At 64 points the lump is about 5 times the push that starts it; at 288 points it is 24 times. Once started, the change runs away by itself.

The leftover stays fixed while the lump grows. Its share of X's extra energy falls from about a fifth at 64 points to about a twentieth at 288.

Our reading, unreviewed When the heat has too little room, it melts patches of the new space into disorder. In Trugenberger's published account, disorder inside space is what matter is. So "the lump becomes matter" has a concrete counterpart in the model. That link is our reading, and nobody qualified has checked it.

Is the leftover dark matter?

The author's hypothesis Ordinary matter comes from the lump. Dark matter is the leftover: the part of X that didn't convert, made in the same moment. It doesn't interact with ordinary matter in the usual ways because it isn't made of the same stuff, but it carries energy, so it feels gravity.

Other researchers The closest published idea is Trugenberger's: dark matter as patches of the network stuck in a different arrangement, the way diamond survives where graphite would be more stable.

Our reading, unreviewed A tiny leftover may be exactly enough. Dark matter is about five times ordinary matter today, but because matter's share grows as space expands, at the start it only needed to be a sliver of the energy: roughly one part in a million if the universe began at about 10 billion degrees, and far less if it began hotter. So the leftover doesn't need to be big. It needs enough seeds, each leaving a scrap, and the number needed is tied to how hot the universe began.

Two ways it could add up, both the author's: many seeds, each leaving a scrap, or a quality of spacetime, energy kept spread through the geometry itself. A first look at the sealed runs favours the first. Clean sheets kept only 7 to 20% of the energy in the geometry, almost all of it the one scrap; every run whose geometry kept more than about half was one whose new space had melted.

What would test it: plant several seeds and see whether each leaves a scrap (the multi-seed test), check that the scraps last, and check whether they attract one another, the first hint of gravity in the model.

One mechanism, four things

The author's idea Space is stable, and stays stable. It does not have to decay for the loop to close — the return leg is paid for, not fallen into:

  • Folding space back up is expensive. That is the same fact as the Big Bang releasing energy when it unfolded: what came out then has to go back in now.
  • Gravity gathers. Wherever matter collects, energy concentrates.
  • A black hole is the one place with enough to pay. Nothing gets out of it, including space itself — so it is where space is folded back up, which is to say where X is made again.
  • A new Big Bang is two of those colliding, opening a new space elsewhere, not in ours.

It closes a loop: X opens into space, space is folded back up where enough energy is concentrated, and it can open again. Folding space is expensive — that is why unfolding gives energy off — and a black hole is the one place with enough to pay. That is also what makes it checkable, because a loop has to work in both directions in the same model.

So we ran it

Ours, new, unreviewed A black hole is a sealed box: nothing gets out, which is exactly the fixed-energy setting this project already had. So we gave a perfect sheet of space a budget of energy it could not get rid of, and looked at what the energy did to it — fold it, or melt it. Four settings of the rules, budgets from a half to sixteen units a point, three runs each.

  • Folding is the cheaper damage, four times cheaper per loop than tearing space. We wrote down before running that small budgets would therefore buy folds.
  • They don't. At every budget and every setting, the energy melts space instead. Folding rises as it gets cheaper — from 2 % of the damage up to 23 % — and never wins.
  • Why: there are far more ways to break a loop than to add one. Price moves it; the sheer number of ways decides it.

Two more checks, same day, and both came out the way the idea wants. Left alone, space does not fold: a cold sheet sat perfectly still, not one move accepted in three hundred passes. Folding happens where energy is concentrated, not everywhere. And nothing sits below flat space at all, once every kind of small loop is allowed — proved exactly rather than measured. Space is the bottom of the well and stays there, which is what the loop above needs of it.

And then we asked about gravity

Ours, new, unreviewed If gravity is the pull that gathers things back together, the smallest thing it must do in this model is make two leftovers pull on each other. That one is exact — no runs, no luck, just arithmetic at every possible distance.

  • Two leftovers side by side cost less than two apart. They share the damage and pay for it once. That is an attraction, and it is real.
  • Move them one step further and it vanishes completely. Not weakens — vanishes, to the last digit, at every distance we can make on the grid.
  • It has to be that way. The energy of an arrangement is a sum of separate little bills, one per connection, and each bill depends only on its own corner. Two leftovers that share no corner simply add up.

So in this model there is no long-range pull between leftovers, and there cannot be one. If gravity lives here at all, it is not carried by the energy — it would have to come from counting: more ways to arrange things when they sit close than when they sit far. That is a real and different measurement, and nobody has made it here.

What that does and does not show. It is weaker than it first sounds, and the weakness is worth stating. Melted, in this model, is not damage and not a hole: it is the random state, and the model's own authors read exactly that as matter and energy — for them a black hole is a region gone back to it. So “the energy melted the space” may be no more than what a great deal of energy in one place looks like, which is close to what a singularity means anyway. And heating a thing at equilibrium is more or less guaranteed to randomise it; squeezing is a different act, and this model has no way to squeeze. What the run does show is a fair contest between the two ways of absorbing the same energy, and the folded way lost badly. What is untested: a jolt into one small patch with cold space pressing in around it, which is what gravity actually does and what a shared budget cannot imitate.

The two hurdles, in plain terms. The sharp version of inflation failed once, because its bubbles never joined up, and this idea has to show that it escapes that. The standard picture also explains the exact pattern of tiny temperature ripples across the sky, and this idea has to reproduce it. Neither can be settled with our model; both are questions for a physicist. Open everywhere, not only here: what X is made of, and what sets the size of its energy gap.

Where this stands

The case for it, and what's left to test

The author's view I think this is how it happened. The idea is simple. It follows a pattern nature uses everywhere else: something stable for now, a push, a sharp change, a lump of energy released. And every test of its central claim has come back the way it said it would. A curled-up arrangement really does open out sharply, give off exactly the predicted energy, and balance its books to the last unit. When a simple idea keeps passing the tests put to it, that is worth taking seriously.

What the evidence shows The mechanism works, in one published model, under every test we could run. The steps that would turn a strong bet into an account of our universe are still ahead, and each is a test the idea could fail:

Why it deserves attention

  • The mechanism shows up in a model someone else published, with no new ingredient added to produce it.
  • Predictions were written down before the runs, and the key ones held: sharpness, the exact lump, the bonfire threshold, no stalling.
  • Some predictions failed, and they are reported alongside the ones that held.
  • The tools were checked against published results and exact answers before anything new was run.

What could still sink it

  • Size. Networks of at most 288 points. That isn't about the number of rules: nature seems to run on few, and so does the model (three). It's that sharpness only shows up clearly in big systems, so what counts is whether results hold steady as size grows. So far they do (the lump per point and the push don't change), with one trend to watch: in longer tubes the new space more often forms in several patches.
  • The stand-in is our choice. Nothing says X is a tube.
  • Why start ordered? Space doesn't form sharply out of random chaos in this model; the idea needs X to be a specific arrangement. A critic will ask where that order came from, and the idea doesn't answer that yet.
  • Some yardsticks came late. The bar this kind of change has to clear was set after the tube runs had already met most of it, and two pre-written test rules had to be fixed when they failed on cases where the answer was already known.
  • It has failed before. Other researchers Alan Guth's 1981 "old inflation" had a sharp change releasing the universe's energy. It was abandoned because bubbles of the new state never merged into one smooth universe. Whether this idea escapes that problem is unknown.
  • Nothing here touches time, quantum behaviour or relativity (claim 2).
  • No physicist has reviewed it, and reasoning done with an AI assistant is unreviewed, however confident it sounds.

What would count against it

  • In larger networks, the tube stops opening sharply: more points stuck in between, or the new space growing in scattered patches rather than one front.
  • X stops being stable-for-now in bigger networks. X only waits before opening inside a window of rule settings (at 1.25 it waits; at 1.5 it falls apart at once). If that window narrowed as networks grow, nothing like X would wait long enough to convert. So far the signs say it doesn't: the push needed and the waiting time stay the same from 48 to 192 points.
  • A physicist finds that we have misread the model.

What happens next

Planned tests, and why we're doing them

Each test is written down before it runs, with what would count as failing, and every result gets reported, whichever way it comes out.

Leftover

Plant several seeds and count the scraps

Why: the dark-matter idea needs leftovers to add up with the number of seeds.

Could fail if: one scrap appears however many seeds there are.

X itself

Find where X stops waiting

Why: to know how narrow the stable-for-now window is (somewhere between rule settings 1.25 and 1.5) and whether it holds as networks grow.

Could fail if: the window shrinks with size.

The product

Check that what the tube opens into is really space

Why: it has to measure as flat, two-dimensional space, not just be called space. First we reproduce the published measurements.

Could fail if: it isn't two-dimensional and flat at large scale.

Relativity

Look for a speed limit

Why: a fixed top speed, not built into the rules, is the first fingerprint of relativity.

Could fail if: the opening front and small nudges have no fixed top speed.

Gravity

Do scraps attract? What does a local spark do?

Why: attraction is the first hint of gravity, and a spark on flat space tells the author's black hole (re-curled space) from the published one (melted space).

Could fail if: scraps ignore or repel each other.

The bar

One fresh run to clear the success bar properly

Why: the bar for this kind of change was written after the first tube runs, so it needs a run at conditions nobody has seen, such as more points or another rule setting.

Could fail if: the change isn't sharp there.

And the one that matters most: someone qualified reads it. Nothing here counts until a physicist has checked it; a first note to the model's authors is the next step.
