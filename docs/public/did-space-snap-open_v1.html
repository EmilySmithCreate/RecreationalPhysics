Did space snap open?

An idea about where space came from: that it snapped open out of something curled up, one direction after another, and the energy it let go became everything in it. What the idea says, what a computer model showed, and what it would mean.

Emily Smith, with Claude (Anthropic) · Version 3, 25 September 2026 · Not yet reviewed by a physicist

Full methods and results (paper) — link coming soon The long version of this page →

The short answer

  • In a computer model, a close cousin of a published one, a curled-up arrangement opened out suddenly into a flat, space-like one. It gave off exactly the burst of energy worked out in advance, and the energy books balanced to the last unit. That is the change the idea describes, working.
  • It passed every test of sharpness we wrote down before the runs. One prediction failed: the leftover scrap doesn't grow as the space grows, so in this model it stays tiny. The idea doesn't need it to: the lump is what carries the energy.
  • The idea now says how the energy divides. Space's three directions open one after another, and the order decides what each releases: the first, ordinary matter and light; the second, dark matter; the third, dark energy. In a three-dimensional version of the model the directions do open one at a time, and the push that starts the change is again the same at every size. What the model doesn't do yet is carry on by itself past the first direction, or split the energy in today's proportions.
  • What's ahead: getting gravity out of the model, much larger networks, and a physicist's review. The first paper, on the curled-up arrangement, is going onto arXiv with the endorsement of the published model's author. Those steps are what would turn a working mechanism into an account of our reality.

Background

Things change in two ways

Settled science Cool water and nothing much happens until it reaches 0 °C. Then it freezes all at once, giving off heat as it goes, with ice and water side by side. Very clean water can even stay liquid below zero until something jolts it. It is stuck behind a hill it can't get over on its own.

Heat a fridge magnet instead and its pull just fades away to nothing. There is no hill, no burst of heat and no moment when half of it has changed. Physicists call the first kind of change first-order (sharp) and the second continuous (smooth).

How do you tell which kind you have? Settled science Let the system jiggle for a long time and count how often it turns up at each energy. That count is the statistical view of the same landscape, seen from underneath.

Settled science Two things make this hard to measure. Small systems hide the hill: with only a few pieces, everything can flip at once, so even a sharp change can look like one broad hump. The real test is whether the valley deepens as the system gets bigger. And not every change is either kind. The best-understood changes in the early universe were gradual handovers with no sharp moment at all. So the kind of change has to be measured, not assumed.

The idea

Space as a change of arrangement

The idea Space isn't the stage everything happens on. It is one settled arrangement of something deeper, which we call X because nobody knows what it is. X isn't random chaos. It is a specific arrangement that is stable for now, like a chemical that will react if something gives it a push. In this picture X is curled up, with fewer large directions than space has, and the change uncurls it. Its three curled directions belong together (below).

The idea has six parts, called claims. They are numbered because each can hold or fail on its own. The icons come back later, wherever a result speaks to that claim.

  1. Claim 1

    Space is one settled arrangement of something deeper, X.

  2. Claim 2

    Whatever X is, known physics (relativity, quantum mechanics) has to come back out of it.

  3. Claim 3

    X and space are two arrangements of the same stuff. One can turn into the other.

  4. Claim 4

    The change was sharp (two humps, not one) and gave off a lump of energy that became matter and light, and, in the newest version, dark matter and dark energy too.

  5. Claim 5

    Not everything converted. A leftover remains: either leftover X, or a different arrangement, called X2.

  6. Claim 6

    The energy books balance across the change.

Three directions, and what each one releases

The idea Space has three large directions, and in X all three are curled. They belong together, like a triad of colors: call them red, green and blue. In X they are alike, and once space has formed they are alike again; nothing marks one of them as red. What makes a direction red is only that it opened first, and which one goes first is chance. The order decides what each opening releases: the first releases ordinary matter and light, the second dark matter, the third dark energy. The same kind of energy opens all three. What a burp does not deliver goes back to the black hole it came from, where it can start another burp or wait. Time may be a fourth curled direction, opening with the other three or beside them; the model can test the pattern in which directions open, but it cannot say which of them is time.

Settled science The shares are today's, measured by the Planck satellite (2018). They are not fixed: matter thins out as space grows and dark energy does not, so dark energy's share has been growing. What is fixed from the start is the ratio of dark matter to ordinary matter, about 5 to 1.

Our reading, unreviewed In the model as published, every direction releases the same amount when it opens, so three openings would give three equal shares. Something has to make later openings release more. One candidate is a tie between the directions, which the author decided on 25 September: the directions share a fate, so a point with some directions open and some still curled costs extra. That tie makes each release depend on how many directions are already open, never on which one, which is the author's "the order decides" exactly. Tuned to today's shares it reproduces them, but tuning two dials to match two numbers is a fit, not a prediction. It becomes a prediction only if the model's other needs (X waiting, the change running, space surviving its birth) pin the dials by themselves.

This is the author's proposal. It is not established, not published, and not tested except as described below.

The test

A toy universe made of links

You can't test this on the universe, so we built on a published model by the physicists Carlo Trugenberger, Christy Kelly and Fabio Biancalana, called combinatorial quantum gravity. The model is a network of dots and links, and it has no positions at all. Each dot is linked to exactly four others. Small closed loops of links earn points. A computer reshuffles the links millions of times and we watch what pattern settles. Nothing in the model knows about space, so if something space-like appears, it appeared by itself. Since 24 September we also run the same rules with six links per dot, which gives three directions, and with eight, which gives four.

One setting is changed, and that matters. In the published model the scoring rule is a measure of how curved the network is, the network's version of the curvature in Einstein's gravity, and that holds at exactly one setting of one dial, which the model's authors fix at 1. At that setting the curled-up shape and the flat one score the same, so nothing is released when one turns into the other. The idea tested here needs the dial a little higher (we use 1.25), and there the rule is no longer a measure of curvature. So everything below about the curled-up shape is about a close cousin of the published model, not the model itself, and we don't call it combinatorial quantum gravity.

The author's view What the raised dial adds is a curling cost: the rule is still curvature, plus a charge for every tight curl, and that charge is exactly what a burp gives back. Where space is smooth nothing is curled tight, so the charge vanishes and only curvature is left. Its size is a constant of our reality: whatever value it has here.

Counting loops tells you the network's shape from the inside. It can settle as a flat sheet, a tube (one direction curled up), or a closed knot. The tube is our stand-in for X: a specific curled-up arrangement, stable for now, and higher in energy than the sheet. It is a stand-in, not X.

Why the sizes are small, and why these ones. The networks here have 36 to 288 points. A tube is always 4 points around, the tightest curl the network can make, and its length sets the size: 16, 24, 36, 48 or 72 points long gives 64, 96, 144, 192 or 288 points. Each experiment's sizes were fixed in its written plan before it ran, and every size we ran is shown. The smaller ones repeat earlier experiments so results can be compared; the largest is as far as run time on one laptop allows.

What this means for the idea

Space doesn't condense sharply out of random chaos in this model, at these sizes. That isn't the idea's question, since X is a specific arrangement rather than chaos, so it neither supports nor rules out the idea. What it does show is that our tools give the same answers as the published work, which is why the results that follow are worth taking seriously.

One arrangement into another: sharp

Tested at four sizes — the idea's prediction held

This is the change the idea actually describes. We start the network as a perfect tube and hold it at a fixed temperature. The tube sits 1 energy unit per point above a flat sheet, and at first it stays put.

Each run waits, then opens out into a flat sheet within a few hundred rounds. It gives off exactly the energy worked out beforehand by arithmetic, 1 unit per point. Some runs pause part-way on a small leftover (the dotted line) before they finish. Before running the main test, we wrote down three things "sharp" would have to mean:

Our result All three held at four sizes (64 to 192 points), on two independent sets of 30 runs each.

The formal verdict: a sharp change, in two steps. That is the pre-registered verdict at 64, 96 and 192 points. Twelve runs had come to rest on rarer leftovers, so we replayed them, saved the final network and read what each one is. Eleven matched their energy exactly. One, at 144 points, changed state in the very last stretch of its run, so that size is set aside, though the three tests above hold there too.

What this means for the idea

This is the core of what we were looking for. A curled-up arrangement opens out, one direction becoming large, and gives off a definite lump of energy. It happens sharply, with every piece claim 4 asks for: stable for now, a push, a seed, a spreading front and an exact lump. And it happens in a close cousin of a published model, one dial turned a little, with nothing else added to make it happen.

One thing it isn't: energy from nowhere. The energy was stored in the curled shape and let go, and the books balance.

Result 2

Speaks toClaim 4Claim 6

In a sealed box: a bonfire, not slush

Tested at three sizes — the idea's prediction held

In the runs above, a heat bath carried the lump away instantly. The idea is about what happens when it can't. So we sealed the box: no energy gets in or out, and the lump has to go somewhere inside. Would it feed the change like a bonfire, or smother it into half-and-half slush?

None ended as slush: part tube and part sheet, sitting still, with nothing melting. Our reasoning said slush was impossible, because it needs a temperature at which tube and sheet are equally happy and no such temperature exists. A single case would have shown that reasoning wrong.

Our result A bonfire, with a threshold. With enough room for the heat, the tube converts completely into a clean sheet. With too little, the heat melts the new space it has just made — melts meaning the points lose their neat two directions and go back to being connected any old way, which is the disordered state space came out of. The change undoes itself. At every size the switch-over falls between 1 place per 4 points and 1 per 2, where we had predicted 1 per 3.5. It moves in step with size, as predicted. Energy balanced to the last unit in every run, which is claim 6 holding in the model.

Result 3

Speaks toClaim 5

The leftover: one small scrap, however big

Tested — a leftover every time, and the same one at every size

Almost every sealed sheet kept a leftover: four points of the old curled arrangement that the front swept past without opening. What we tested was whether that leftover grows with the space — because that is what decides whether it is a component of a universe or a blemish on one. Freeze water quickly and a bigger block traps more bubbles; if the front shed scraps as it went, a longer tube would end with more of them, and the leftover would be a fixed share of everything, big enough to be some of what a universe is made of. We wrote that down before measuring.

Our result It doesn't grow. About one scrap per box at every size from 64 to 288 points — a single bubble however big the block. Eighty fresh runs, pre-registered.

What this means for the idea

Why it matters, and how much. A scrap is four points: 1.4 % of a 288-point box, 0.14 % of one ten times larger, and nothing at all at the size of a universe. So a single conversion leaves a single defect, not a share of anything. Claim 5 asked for a leftover and there is one, every run, every size — and the size of it does not budge as the space grows. That is a fact about how much of a conversion fails to finish, and it is the same fact at 64 points and at 288. What it is not is a large share of anything, and the idea never needed it to be.

What it does not touch. The energy given off and the opening of a new direction both do grow with size: a bigger tube gives a bigger lump. That is the result the idea actually rests on, and it stands. Our result A conversion that starts in several places leaves more scraps, but fewer than one per seed (what the scraps do).

Putting it together

What the model says about X, X2 and the lump

The idea names three things: X, the leftover (X2), and the lump of energy released between them. The model has a working stand-in for each: the tube for X, the four-point scrap for the leftover, and the measured burst for the lump. Here is how they behave, and how they fit together.

X

The tube sits a fixed amount above space: 1 unit per point at the setting we used. It is stable for now only inside a window. At rule setting 1.25 it waits hundreds to thousands of rounds. At 1.5, where the lump per point would be twice as big, it falls apart within 50 rounds. So in this model, a bigger lump comes with a less stable X. Mapped setting by setting, the tube waits at every setting from 1.05 to 1.35 and stops waiting between 1.35 and 1.40. Wherever it waits, the change keeps its sharp character, cleanest near 1. The formal verdict is inconclusive, because one criterion written beforehand turned out too tight for thirty runs per setting.

The lump

The lump is exactly the energy gap between X and space. We worked it out by arithmetic before running, and then measured it. It grows in step with the size of X. What happens next depends on where it can go. With enough room it leaves clean space; with too little it melts the space it just made.

X2, the leftover

What's usually left is a scrap of X itself, not a new kind of arrangement. In claim 5's terms, that is the "leftover X" option. The only candidates for a genuinely separate X2 are rarer leftovers: a small twist in some sealed boxes, and a handful of other resting states that turned out, once read, to be combinations of small defects in the sheet. All of them are small and fixed in size. In the newest version of the idea the leftover is not dark matter; what it does is below.

Between them

The push stays fixed while the lump grows. At 64 points the lump is about 5 times the push that starts it; at 288 points it is 24 times. Once started, the change runs away by itself.

The leftover stays fixed while the lump grows. Its share of X's extra energy falls from about a fifth at 64 points to about a twentieth at 288.

The author's view When the heat has too little room, it melts patches of the new space into disorder. Trugenberger's published account calls that disorder matter. The author does not: matter has kinds, conserved amounts and definite masses, and a random tangle has none of them. In this account, matter comes from the lump of energy, released by the first direction to open, and the structured leftovers are simply what the change left behind.

Where dark matter and dark energy come from

The author's hypothesis Not from the leftover. An earlier version of this page said the scrap was dark matter; the author replaced that on 25 September. Dark matter and dark energy are what the second and third directions release as they open, after the first has released ordinary matter and light (the triad, above). All three come out of the burp, in order, and the scrap is only what the change failed to finish.

What the scraps do

Our result We planted several seeds. With 1, 2, 4 and 8 seeds in one tube, the change left 1.1, 2.2, 2.5 and 3.4 scraps on average: more seeds, more scrap, but fewer than one each. Both guesses written down beforehand missed. The author's, one scrap per seed, held up to two seeds and fell short beyond. Ours, one per tube, was wrong from two seeds.

Our result Do the scraps last? Held at one fixed temperature, every scrap heals away in the end, sooner the warmer it is. Cooled instead, the way a young universe cools, they freeze in: every scrap survives a fast cooling, about four in five a slower one, and a third the slowest we tried. Pre-registered.

Our result In three directions we have not found a scrap that stays put. None of the small defects we built holds, and every bit of damage left by heat healed completely when cooled.

The first hurdle, and the answer this idea gives. The sharp version of inflation failed in 1983 for a stated reason: the space between the new bubbles grew faster than the bubbles did, so they never merged into one smooth universe. That failure is a race, and this picture has nothing to race against. The bubbles lost because the space between them was inflating faster than they could grow. X is a curled arrangement, not a growing space, so fronts spread and meet at their own pace with nothing outrunning them. And each new space opens elsewhere rather than into ours, so two universes never have to join up in the first place. The author's answer Said honestly: that is an argument, not a result. Nobody has checked it, and our model cannot — it has no expansion in it at all. And it costs something that belongs in the same breath: inflation's enormous stretch is what explains why the universe looks so flat and so even, so an X that never expands does not supply that explanation, and something else would have to.

The second hurdle, at the right height. The pattern of tiny temperature ripples across the sky is the most precisely measured thing in cosmology, and inflation predicts it well. The bar for a picture at this stage is not to derive every ripple — no candidate theory of the early universe does that from first principles either — it is not to contradict the pattern, and to have somewhere for it to come from eventually. This idea has a candidate and has not used it: ripples need something that varies from place to place, and a conversion starting in many places at once would leave a scatter of scraps. We have seen that more seeds leave more scraps; whether that could make the ripples is untested. Neither hurdle can be settled with our model; both are questions for a physicist. Open everywhere, not only here: what X is made of, and what sets the size of its energy gap. In our model that second one has an exact answer — the gap is set by a single dial, and its size is just how far that dial sits from the setting where curled and flat cost exactly the same. Nothing tells us what plays that dial in the real thing, and a small gap would mean sitting very close to the tie.

The story, end to end

From X to here, in eight steps

How to read the marks. A green tick means we tested it and it came out the way the idea needs. An amber half means we tested it and the answer was partial, or the model ran out before the question did. A red cross means we tested it and it went against the idea. A dashed ring means nobody has tested it at all.

Everything above is a separate test. This is the picture those tests are of, in the order it would have happened. Each step says plainly whether it is something we measured, something exact, or something the idea asserts with nothing behind it yet.

  1. X is waiting

    Tested — exact, at every size

    X is made of the same stuff space is, wired so that its directions are small instead of large. It isn't chaos and it isn't a soup — it is one particular arrangement, and it sits still. It holds more energy than space does, and it has no way to let that energy go by itself.

    In our model the curled arrangement sits a fixed amount above the flat one, the same amount at every size we built, from 36 points to 600.

    In three directions the fully curled arrangement is so symmetric that its directions cannot be told apart at all: nothing in X marks one direction as first. That is exact.

  2. Something small sets it off

    Tested — 320 runs, five sizes

    It does not fall apart on its own. It needs a push — and the push is startlingly cheap: twelve units, delivered anywhere in it, and the same twelve whether the thing is small or large. Below twelve, nothing ever happens: sealed up with nothing to spare, X waits for ever.

    320 runs at five sizes. Nothing under twelve ever started it; nothing at twelve or over ever failed to.

    In three directions the push is fixed too: sixteen units at every size from 192 to 512 points, nothing below it ever starting the change and nothing at or above it ever failing (pre-registered).

  3. The opening spreads as a front

    Tested — at every size

    Not everywhere at once. One spot goes first, and then a boundary sweeps outward with the old arrangement on one side and space on the other — and almost nothing in between. That is what a sharp change looks like from the inside, and it is the opposite of everything softening together.

    Caught mid-change, 99 points in every 100 read as one arrangement or the other, at every size.

  4. Energy comes out, one direction at a time, and a scrap stays behind

    Tested — exact in two directions; in three, the first opens and the others wait

    The lump is exactly what X was holding above space — one unit for every point converted, every run, every size. In the story it comes out one direction at a time: the first to open releases ordinary matter and light, the second dark matter, the third dark energy, and what the burp does not deliver goes back to the black hole. And the change never quite finishes: one small piece of the old arrangement is left stranded.

    The energy given off matched the number known in advance, exactly. The scrap is one, however large the space gets, so at the size of a universe it is a speck rather than a share of anything.

    In three directions, the first opens and the others wait. Given its push, the arrangement began opening one direction as a front, finished it in a third of the runs, and never opened a second (pre-registered). The author had predicted they would open together; that first version didn't hold · in progress. Her reading since: each direction takes its own push, from the energy the black hole keeps back. In four directions the cheapest push bought a single move and nothing followed. Next (running now): whether, with room enough, the first direction's release pays for the second.

  5. Space then stays put

    Proved — no run can overturn it

    Having arrived, space does not go anywhere. Nothing at all sits below it — not one arrangement out of the whole family, once every kind of small loop is allowed. So space is the bottom of the well: it has nothing to decay into, and nothing in the rest of the story asks it to.

    This one follows from the rules by argument, so no run can overturn it.

  6. Gravity gathers, over a very long time

    Tested, and the model runs out here

    Then the slow part. Matter clumps, clumps merge, and energy concentrates in fewer and fewer places. The idea needs this and does not explain it — and here our model runs out before the story does.

    We asked the smallest version of the question: do two leftovers pull on each other? Side by side they are cheaper, because their damage overlaps and gets paid for once. Move them a single step apart and that vanishes — not fades, vanishes, at every distance on the grid. The energy in this model is a sum of separate little bills, one per connection, each depending only on its own corner, so two things that share no corner simply add up. There is no long-range pull here and there cannot be one. We also tried the counting version with space held still: treating the points as interchangeable does not pull two scraps together either.

    That rules out one of the two ways a pull can happen. The other is still open: there may be more ways for everything else to arrange itself when two things sit close, and then close is where you would keep finding them — no pulling involved, and indistinguishable from an attraction from the outside. Heat does exactly that. We have not made that measurement.

    The author's idea Curve-first gravity. Einstein's account has mass curve space, and curved space move matter. The author keeps the second half and reverses the cause of the first: space has its own drive to curve, and matter supplies the energy that starts the curve rather than causing it. Gravity belongs to space, not to X. Wherever Einstein's account has been measured, hers has to give the same numbers; it differs on why.

    Where this gets tested. Not in two dimensions, where even Einstein's gravity gives no pull between things at rest. The three-dimensional version now runs, and its first answer is the same: flat space in this model is stiff — every way out of it costs a fixed amount — and published work says a medium that stiff passes on no pull at a distance. So gravity needs one more ingredient, and in three directions the scrap it would act on has not been found yet either. Our reading, unreviewed The candidate: a quantity living on the points that can vary smoothly from place to place. Worked out exactly, it would give a pull of Newton's shape if scraps are its sources. It is not in the model; adding it is the author's call. A pull would earn the name gravity by pulling things together, more strongly the more energy they carry, the same for every kind of thing, and weakening with distance the way gravity does.

  7. A black hole folds space back up

    Tested at both ends — the middle is untested

    Where enough energy has gathered, the idea says space stops being space — pushed back into the substrate it came from. It is uphill, paid for by the concentrated energy, which is why it takes something as extreme as a black hole. Nothing gets out of one, including space itself. The author's idea

    What we tested. A black hole is a sealed box, which is a setting we already had. We gave a perfect sheet of space a budget of energy it could not get rid of, and read every point afterwards to see how many clean directions it had left.

    They lose them. At every budget and every setting of the rules, the space stops being space. And it is not a near miss: counting every legal move out of ordered space, the number that build a new loop is zero. Order can only be broken, one step at a time.

    That is a description of this step, not a refutation of it. The state the energy drives it to is the one with overwhelmingly the most ways to be arranged — which is exactly what a black hole is taken to be in ordinary physics: the object with more ways to be than anything else of its size. And the same count says the way back is open rather than closed. Out of a melted region, 10,254 moves build a new loop, and the cheapest of them runs downhill. So remaking the substrate goes through the mess, not around it: break first, build second, and both halves of that are measured.

    How long the mess lasts depends on where the energy can go

    At ordinary energies it is not a place anything stays — caught mid-change, 99 points in every 100 read as one settled arrangement or the other, with almost nothing in between. But in a sealed box with a large budget it persists, for the plain reason that the energy has nowhere else to be. So “the mess is brief” is true where heat can leave and untrue where it cannot, and a black hole is the second kind.

    What is left untested, and it is the part that matters. What a melted region rebuilds into when it is squeezed rather than merely heated — and whether what it builds deserves the name X. Our one attempt at cooling a melt jammed partway, and when we checked, the chain had stopped rather than settled, so it says nothing. Our model has no way to squeeze anything, which is the one thing a black hole certainly does. Treating the points as interchangeable, which favours tidy closed shapes, did not change this: the sealed sheet still melted rather than folded.

    The author's mechanism The fold starts on one direction. Once one has curled, the arrangement is closer to X, and that speeds up the rest: the other directions fold faster, a collapse that feeds itself, even though the energy only hit one direction. Exact In the model each fold's wall is lower than the one before, which is her speeding-up, in the walls.

    Tested in three directions: energy packed into one place in flat three-dimensional space excited it and never started a fold; cooled, it healed back to perfect space (pre-registered). That makes five tests with no fold, all with named points. First versions didn't hold · in progress. Next (running now): the same with interchangeable points, where the fully curled state's enormous number of look-alike arrangements could pay for the fold that energy alone never starts. The counting says it can win only very close to the published dial setting.

  8. And it opens again, elsewhere

    Not tested

    A region of X, once made, is back at step one: waiting for a small push. When it gets one it opens into a new space, elsewhere, not into ours. The loop closes. And what a burp does not deliver stays in the black hole, where it can start another burp or wait. The author's idea

    This is the thinnest step, and it should be read that way. The author's guess is that two black holes colliding supply the push. Nothing we have says a collision is needed, or how many, or how big — and what we do have points the other way, because the push is that cheap, fixed, local twelve. Size matters at the far end instead: the energy given off grows with the amount converted, and whether the new space survives being born depends on there being somewhere for that energy to go.

A loop, not a line

The author's idea Most accounts of where space came from are a line: something happened, then space, then us. This one bends back. Space is made of X; gravity gathers; where enough gathers, space is remade into X, which can open again. The thing that emerged reaches down and remakes what it is made of. Nothing needs to have started it, because there is no bottom step to start from.

The shape has a name

Douglas Hofstadter calls this a strange loop. You climb through levels, each one made of the level below — and the top one reaches back down and acts on the bottom. His example is a mind: neurons make symbols, symbols make a self, and the self then acts on the neurons. You went up and arrived back at the bottom, and no level is the foundation.

Here the levels are the network and the space built out of it, and the reaching back down is a black hole. What makes it strange is not that it repeats — it is that it crosses levels. That is also what separates it from the other well-known loop in cosmology, Lee Smolin's, where black holes give birth to universes whose constants drift a little each time. That one is a family tree: every turn makes a new individual and the line never comes back. This one comes back.

Tested — one part held, the rest did not

Naming it is not decoration, because a strange loop makes a demand you can check: the upper level has to be genuinely its own, with laws that are more than a summary of the level beneath. We wrote down what would count as passing and tested it. It did not pass. One part held — the boundary between the two arrangements costs a fixed amount, the same in a small system as a large one, and two quite different rule-sets agree on it almost exactly, so it belongs to the arrangement and not to the rules. The rest missed, and how the change plays out over time was not captured at all.

And why you would expect to find yourself inside one

The author's reasoning Think of the shapes a reality could have. Some run for a while and stop. Some start and never stop, but wind down until nothing happens in them. Some go round. Let the ordering run on: the first kind had its turn, the second is still there with nothing going on inside it, and the third is still going.

Said carefully, because this is where the argument can be broken. "Loops are the most likely reality" sounds like counting one infinity against another, and nobody has an agreed rule for doing that — it is a real unsolved problem, not a technicality. But the argument does not need it. The claim that survives is about dynamics, not about census: in any system that keeps running, trajectories fall into cycles and stay there. That is what an attractor is, and it is ordinary mathematics. It needs a measure only over the starting conditions of one world, not over all possible worlds.

Then one more constraint, and it is the cheap one: anything capable of asking this question needs a stable spatial phase to evolve in, over billions of years. So of the shapes that keep going, the one we could be inside is one with a settled place to stand. A loop with a stable phase is not a coincidence we have to explain away — it is close to the only thing we could have found.

The author's measure Count per turn of the loop. Every loop turns for ever, so all have the same kind of infinity; what differs is what one turn produces. We are most likely to be inside the loop whose turn yields the most time, matter and energy in a stable phase, and ours never really ends: space expands and time runs on until the last star is eaten by the last black hole. Finding ourselves at this point in it is a smaller infinity, because fewer observers could have evolved by now. Our reading, unreviewed Counting per turn is a choice; counting per unit of time or per volume can rank loops differently, which is cosmology's known measure problem. A loop's turn is the strongest case for counting per turn that we know of.

And an argument that needs no counting at all

Anything with a beginning owes you an account of the beginning. Whatever you name as the cause, you can ask what put that there, and then what put that there, for ever. A loop owes nothing to anything outside itself: every part is accounted for by the part before, the whole way round, and there is no outside to point at. It is like a dictionary — every word in it defined using other words in it, and none needing one from beyond the covers. Nothing is left over to explain.

Neither of these is evidence, and they are not offered as any. No measurement could tell "reality is a loop" from "reality began". They are the reasons the idea has this shape, and the reason a stable spatial phase is what an observer would expect to be standing in. The evidence is everything above them.

The scorecard

The six claims, where they stand

Claim 1

Space is a settled arrangement of something deeper

Holds in the model

Networks with no positions do settle into space-like shapes. Published work agrees, including a reported proof for the simplest case.

Claim 2

Known physics comes back out

Half of it

More comes out than we expected, and the hard half does not. Out of the model, unasked: the ordinary law for how long a change waits before it starts, right to within about ten per cent on average over a ninety-fold range with nothing adjusted; exact energy books; the standard signatures of a sharp change; and space itself, with its number of dimensions a thing you count rather than a thing put in. It also holds several kinds of stuff at once — ordered space, a disordered melt, and structured leftovers — as one substance arranged in different ways. What is missing is the half the claim was written about: relativity and quantum behaviour. The model has no time in it at all, so relativity cannot even be asked. One attempt at the quantum half was made and did not work: counting the network's indistinguishable versions cannot produce the correlations quantum experiments measure, for any arrangement. The author's next idea there is untested. Gravity is not out yet either: flat space in this model is too stiff to pass on a pull (step 6). No approach anywhere has cleared this bar.

Claim 3

X and space are two arrangements of one thing

Holds in the model

Tube, sheet and knot are the same links rewired. Nothing is added or removed.

Claim 4

The change was sharp and gave off a lump

Shown in the model

The tube opens sharply and gives off exactly the predicted lump. Pre-registered verdict: a sharp change in two steps, at 64, 96 and 192 points. In three directions the push is fixed with size too, and the first direction opens as a front; the others don't follow by themselves.

Claim 5

A leftover remains

Holds, but small

A scrap of X is left inside the new space. It stays the same size however big the space, and more seeds leave more of it. It heals away at a steady temperature but freezes in when cooled. In the newest version of the idea it is not dark matter: that comes from the second direction to open.

Claim 6

The energy books balance

Holds (built in)

Exact in every sealed run. The model is built to conserve energy, and that held up through a conversion.

Where this stands

The case for it, and what's left to test

The author's view I think this is how it happened. The idea is simple. It follows a pattern nature uses everywhere else: something stable for now, a push, a sharp change, a lump of energy released. And every test of its central claim has come back the way it said it would. A curled-up arrangement really does open out sharply, give off exactly the predicted energy, and balance its books to the last unit. When a simple idea keeps passing the tests put to it, that is worth taking seriously.

What the evidence shows The mechanism works, in a close cousin of one published model, under every test we could run. The steps that would turn a strong bet into an account of our universe are still ahead, and each is a test the idea could fail:

Why it deserves attention

  • The mechanism shows up in a model someone else published, with one dial turned a little and no new ingredient added. At the published setting of that dial nothing is released, and we say so.
  • Predictions were written down before the runs, and the key ones held: sharpness, the exact lump, the bonfire threshold, no stalling.
  • Some predictions failed, and they are reported alongside the ones that held.
  • The tools were checked against published results and exact answers before anything new was run.
  • The first paper is going onto arXiv with the endorsement of the published model's author.

What could still sink it

  • Size. The results above use at most 288 points; runs with a few thousand are going now. That isn't about the number of rules: nature seems to run on few, and so does the model (three). It's that sharpness only shows up clearly in big systems, so what counts is whether results hold steady as size grows. So far they do (the lump per point and the push don't change), with one trend to watch: in longer tubes the new space more often forms in several patches.
  • The stand-in is our choice. Nothing says X is a tube.
  • Why start ordered? Space doesn't form sharply out of random chaos in this model; the idea needs X to be a specific arrangement. A critic will ask where that order came from, and the idea doesn't answer that yet.
  • Some yardsticks came late. The bar this kind of change has to clear was set after the tube runs had already met most of it, and two pre-written test rules had to be fixed when they failed on cases where the answer was already known.
  • It has failed before. Other researchers Alan Guth's 1981 "old inflation" had a sharp change releasing the universe's energy. It was abandoned because bubbles of the new state never merged into one smooth universe. Whether this idea escapes that problem is unknown.
  • Nothing here touches time or relativity (claim 2), and the one attempt at quantum behaviour failed as stated.
  • The directions don't yet carry on by themselves. In three and four directions the push opens one direction and stops. The idea's answer is a push per direction from the black hole's reserve; the model has to show that the rest follows, not be told to.
  • A fit is not a prediction. Today's shares can be reached by tuning two dials of the tie between directions. Unless the model's other needs pin those dials, matching the numbers shows nothing.
  • The three-dimensional version has not reproduced its published curve. The author chose to go ahead and come back to it.
  • The dial is off the published value. The curled-up shape only waits and opens with one setting raised above the model's own, where its rule is no longer a measure of curvature alone but curvature plus a curling cost.
  • No physicist has reviewed it, and reasoning done with an AI assistant is unreviewed, however confident it sounds.

What would count against it

The author's bar Reality doesn't need the change to happen the same way at every size in every instance. It has to happen, sharply, at one size, and go on to make new loops.

  • In larger networks, the tube stops opening sharply: more points stuck in between, or the new space growing in scattered patches rather than one front.
  • X stops being stable-for-now in bigger networks. X only waits before opening inside a window of rule settings (from 1.05 to 1.35 it waits; by 1.40 it falls apart at once). If that window narrowed as networks grow, nothing like X would wait long enough to convert. So far the signs say it doesn't: the push needed and the waiting time stay the same from 48 to 192 points.
  • A physicist finds that we have misread the model.
  • No tie that respects the order can make later openings release more without melting space or letting X fall apart: then the triad has no home in this family of models.

What happens next

Planned tests, and why we're doing them

Each test is written down before it runs, with what would count as failing, and every result gets reported, whichever way it comes out.

The triad

Does the order of opening set what each direction releases?

Why: in the idea, the first, second and third openings release ordinary matter, dark matter and dark energy. The model has to make later openings release more, from its own rules rather than from tuning.

Could fail if: every tie that keeps X waiting and space stable gives equal releases, or matches today's shares only when tuned.

The cascade

Does the first opening pay for the next?

Why: in three and four directions the push opens one direction and stops. With room enough, the energy the first releases might pay the second one's wall. Running now, at several room sizes and dial settings.

Could fail if: at every room size and setting, the second direction stays shut or the new space melts.

The product

Check that what the tube opens into is really space

Why: it has to measure as flat, two-dimensional space, not just be called space. First we reproduce the published measurements.

Could fail if: it isn't two-dimensional and flat at large scale.

Relativity

Look for a speed limit

Why: a fixed top speed, not built into the rules, is the first fingerprint of relativity.

Could fail if: the opening front and small nudges have no fixed top speed.

Gravity

Curve-first gravity, in three dimensions

Why: flat space in this model is too stiff to pass on a pull. The candidate ingredient is a quantity on the points that varies smoothly, which would give a pull of Newton's shape if scraps are its sources; adding it is the author's decision. Then: does it pull, how does the pull fall off with distance, and is it the same for everything?

Could fail if: nothing is pulled, or the pull doesn't weaken the way gravity does.

Black holes

Can counting fold space back up?

Why: energy alone has never started a fold. With interchangeable points, the fully curled state has vastly more look-alike arrangements, and near the published dial setting that could pay for the fold. Running now.

Could fail if: space still heats and heals rather than folding.

The bar

One fresh run to clear the success bar properly

Why: the bar for this kind of change was written after the first tube runs, so it needs a run at conditions nobody has seen, such as more points or another rule setting.

Could fail if: the change isn't sharp there.

And the one that matters most: someone qualified reads it. The first paper is going onto arXiv with the endorsement of the published model's author. No physicist has reviewed the work yet, and nothing here counts until one has.
