\title{Does concentrated energy re-curl space? Three sealed tests in a graph model of emergent geometry, and a melt
every time}

\author{Emily Smith}
\email{emilysmithcreate@gmail.com}
\affiliation{Independent researcher, Charlottesville, Virginia, USA}

\date{DRAFT, 25 September 2026}

\begin{abstract}
In the family of graph energies obtained from combinatorial quantum gravity (CQG) by raising the coefficient
$\lambda$ of its local term above 1 (only $\lambda = 1$ is CQG; everything here is at $\lambda = 1.25$, next to CQG
and not in it), the flat torus is the exact ground state and a curled direction costs $4(\lambda - 1)$ per vertex.
The author's hypothesis holds that a black hole is a region of space re-curled toward the arrangement it came from,
and that concentrated energy is what pays for the re-curling. We ask the model, sealed and energy-conserving, what
energy does to flat space, three ways: spread through a bath any move may draw on, packed into one place as heat only
one vertex may spend or as disorder wired into one patch, and spread then drained at four rates from a slow leak to
a quench. Read by rules fixed before the runs, vertices whose local dimension falls below 2 are folded and those
whose dimension rises above 2 are melted. At 36 to 256 vertices, with named points and with the points treated as
interchangeable, each test's verdict is a melt. In the local test 26 of 27 cells have a melted majority and one,
with interchangeable points at 64 vertices, healed; in the leaking test all 15 cells melted, 134 of the 144 draining
replicas; in the spread test the folded share of the damage stayed below one half at 64 vertices, while at 36 it
passed one half in one cell with interchangeable points, on three damaged replicas, which the rule reads by the
letter as folding there. No cell of the local
or leaking tests folds, the largest folded piece in them is 11 and 16 vertices, and a melt neither folds nor
flattens as its energy drains. At the lowest budgets a melt here is mostly one defect of about eight vertices with
broken squares, the only thing a single move can make from the sheet. At $\lambda = 1.25$, asked these three ways,
two-dimensional space did not re-curl. Every verdict quoted was pre-registered before its run.
\end{abstract}

\maketitle

\section{Introduction}

The companion papers \cite{paper1,paper2} study a curled torus opening into flat space and what it leaves behind. This
one asks the reverse: can flat space be curled back up? The author's framework needs it to be possible, because in
her picture a black hole is a region re-curled toward the curled arrangement, and the energy concentrated in it is
what pays the curling cost \cite{repo}. The published programme reads a black hole the other way, as a bubble that has
reverted to the random phase \cite{T25}. The two readings differ in what concentrated energy produces, a fold or a
melt, and that is measurable.

Two facts fix the terms. Above $\lambda = 1$ the flat torus is the exact ground state, nothing lies below it, and
leaving it costs at least 32 by any single move (Sec.~II). And the two kinds of damage have different prices: at
$\lambda = 1.25$ a square added to a flat sheet costs 4 (1 on each of its edges, which then carry three) and a square
destroyed costs 16, so curling is the cheaper damage by a factor of four per square. In price, a small budget favors
curling; but no single move out of the perfect sheet adds a square \cite{repo}, so a curl cannot be bought in one
step. The question is which the dynamics buys.

\section{Model, the sealed box, and the reading}

The state is a four-regular bipartite graph on $N$ vertices under the hard-core rule of CQG. With $S$ squares and
$X = \sum_e (S_e - 2)_+$ surplus squares,
\begin{equation}
  H = 16(N - S) + 4\lambda X ,
\end{equation}
so a square destroyed costs 16 and a surplus square costs $4\lambda$. The local dimension $d(v)$ is the number of the
six edge pairs at $v$ that close no square: 2 on the flat sheet, 1 on a curled direction, 0 in a 4-cube, above 2 where
squares are missing. A vertex is \emph{folded} if $d < 2$ and \emph{melted} if $d > 2$; a replica's outcome, read from
its final block, is FOLDED if at least four vertices are damaged and folded ones are at least as many as melted,
MELTED if at least four are damaged and melted ones outnumber folded, HEALED if fewer than four are damaged. Four is
the smallest curled object, one column. A cell's outcome is the majority over replicas (the spread test, T21, was read
instead by the mean folded share of the damage). The reading is tilted by the move set: the only single move out of
a perfect sheet breaks squares and leaves eight vertices at $d = 3$, so a replica that has bought one move and nothing
more reads MELTED. At the lowest budgets that is most of what MELTED means: with 32 to 64 units in one store, the
median final state holds 6 to 8 melted vertices and sits 32 units above the sheet, one broken-square defect rather
than a region returned to the random phase. At larger budgets the damage grows, to a median of 18 and 28 melted
vertices at 192 units in one store (144 and 256 vertices) and 73 at 576 units over nine.

Sealed dynamics keep stores of energy beside the graph \cite{Creutz83}: a proposed switch draws on one store, is
refused if the store cannot pay, and pays into it when it lowers the energy; $H$ plus the stores is conserved to the
last unit in every run reported here. Three protocols differ only in which store a move may use and where the energy
starts. \emph{Spread}: a shared bath of $2N$ stores, the budget in one of them, any move anywhere may draw on it
(the earlier test, T21, which also repeated the single-demon protocol of an exploratory predecessor \cite{repo}).
\emph{Packed}: either the sheet perfect and the budget in the store of one vertex under a per-vertex bath, in which a
move pays only from the store of the first vertex it involves; or the budget wired into the graph as disorder within
radius 3 of one vertex (2 at 64 vertices), the bath empty. \emph{Drained}:
the spread protocol with every store scaled by $1 - \ell$ after each block of 100 sweeps, at $\ell = 0.001$, 0.01,
0.1 and 1.0, the last a quench. Under the per-vertex bath a store below 32 can never act, because leaving the flat
sheet costs at least that, so the packed budgets are 32 to 192 units in one store, or 288 and 576 spread over nine.

With the points treated as interchangeable, every arrangement is weighted by its number of symmetries (the
relabelings that keep every edge), which favors a complete fold, a 4-cube with 192 symmetries, over any partial
state. The packed test was run that way at 64 vertices, the smallest size at which a complete fold exists, with the
fast automorphism count of Ref.~\cite{repo}.

\section{Spread through a bath: it melts}

\emph{T21, pre-registered.} At $N = 36$ and 64, budgets of 2, 4, 8 and 16 per vertex through the bath and 0.5 to 4
per vertex through a single demon, six replicas each, named and interchangeable points side by side. The
pre-registered reading is the mean folded share of the damage. At 64 vertices it stays below one half at every
budget under both weightings (at most 0.27, with interchangeable points at budget 2), and the verdict there, the
overall one, is MELTS EITHER WAY. At 36 vertices under the bath the rule reads FOLDS WITH INTERCHANGEABLE POINTS by
the letter: at budget 2 the interchangeable share is 0.61, from only three damaged replicas, against a named 0.43, and
at budget 4 the order reverses (0.25 against 0.40); we read that lean as fragile. This was the result that prompted the author's objection: a black hole is not
energy spread evenly but energy packed into one place, and heating a whole sheet at equilibrium randomizes it by
construction.

\section{Packed into one place: it melts}

\emph{T26, pre-registered, with the author's prediction that it re-curls.} Twenty-seven cells, twenty replicas each at
144 and 256 vertices and ten at 64, 50,000 sweeps (4,000 at 64), the final graph saved. In the per-vertex protocol the
first move a store can buy is the cheapest exit from the sheet, a loss of two squares, which leaves eight vertices at
$d = 3$: melted, by the reading. In most replicas that defect, or a larger melt, is what the final state holds; 4 of
the 240 replicas with the whole budget in one store end FOLDED, none of their cells by a majority.
Table~\ref{tab:t26} gives the census. No cell has a folded majority; the largest folded piece in any final state is
eleven vertices, at a budget of 96 in one store; the share of replicas that melt first and fold later is at most one
in twenty. A hot patch in a cold shared bath mostly stays melted at 144 and 256 vertices, healing in 0 to 20\% of
replicas, where we had expected it to heal; at 64 vertices the named patch melts in 7 of 10 and the interchangeable
one heals in 10 of 10 at 32 units and melts in 7 of 10 at 64. The verdict is MELTS: 26 of the 27 cells have a melted
majority, one healed and none folded. The author's prediction, confirmed as hers while the runs were in progress and
before any cell was read as a verdict, failed; ours, MELTS, held, apart from the healing we had expected of the hot
patch.

\section{Drained: it stays melted}

\emph{T27, pre-registered, with a prediction inferred for the author by the assistant, and marked on the record as
inferred, that the melt folds before it flattens.} The spread protocol at 144 vertices, budgets of 2, 4 and 8 per vertex,
drained at four rates and a sealed control, twelve replicas each, 30,000 sweeps. All fifteen cells, the control
included, have a melted majority: 134 of the 144 draining replicas melted, the largest folded piece anywhere 16
vertices. The quench freezes the disorder as it stands; the slow drains do not anneal it within the run. The verdict
is STAYS MELTED; ours held, and the prediction inferred for the author failed.

\section{Three directions: it does not even melt for good}

\emph{Pre-registered as T34, the author predicting that the energy re-curls space, one direction first and then the
rest; ours, that it melts.} The author's fold is of three intertwined directions, so the same question was asked of
flat six-link space (three directions; the six-link kernel's reproduction gate is open, and every result here carries
that caveat), at $\lambda = 1.25$, $6 \times 6 \times 6$ and $8 \times 8 \times 8$ tori, energies from 64 (the
cheapest move out of flat space) to one and two directions' worth plus a wall, packed into one vertex's store or spread
through a bath of $2N$ stores, eight replicas each, 50{,}000 sweeps. All 20 cells melted by the registered rule, and no
replica folded a single point. Quenched at zero temperature afterwards, every one of the 80 end states at $N = 216$
returned to perfectly flat space: the ``melt'' was thermal excitation of flat space, which the cold removes, not a
trapped disorder and not the start of a fold. With named points, then, concentrated energy excites three-direction
space and nothing more. The half of the author's mechanism that named points cannot see, a counting that favors the
fully folded, symmetric state when the points are interchangeable, pays only near $\lambda = 1.02$ and only at full
curling; that test is running.

\section{Discussion}

Three protocols, two weightings of the points, sizes from 36 to 256 vertices: under this energy at $\lambda = 1.25$
and this dynamics, energy given to flat two-dimensional space melts it, in the sense of Sec.~II, and does not curl
it, in each of the ways it was delivered and taken away here. Curling is the cheaper damage per square, and the
dynamics does not buy it, because every single move out of the perfect sheet breaks squares and none adds one
\cite{repo}. A fold would have to run through a seam where squares are briefly broken, as the curled torus's opening
does in reverse \cite{paper1}, and here the broken squares did not go on to fold.

What this does and does not show. It does not show that the author's black hole is impossible: her rule is that the
three space directions curl together, so the fold she means is three-dimensional, and this paper is two-dimensional.
It shows that the two-dimensional version of the question, asked three ways, has one answer, and that a re-curling
mechanism, if it exists in this family, needs either three directions or an ingredient this energy lacks. The
six-link version, with the energy spread and packed, is pre-registered and running (T34), and a version with
interchangeable points near $\lambda = 1.02$, the one place where counting symmetries could pay for the fold, is
pre-registered (T42) \cite{repo}. And it carries the model's standing caveat: the melt here is what Ref.~\cite{T25}
calls matter, and the author does not; what the melt is called does not change what it does.
