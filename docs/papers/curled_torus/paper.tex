\title{The curled torus burps: activated escape and conversion in a graph model of emergent geometry}

\author{Emily Smith}
\email{emilysmithcreate@gmail.com}
\affiliation{Independent researcher, Charlottesville, Virginia, USA}

\date{14 October 2026}

\begin{abstract}
We study how a curled torus gives way in four-regular bipartite graph energies next to combinatorial
quantum gravity (CQG). A coefficient $\lambda$ multiplies the price of a surplus square; only
$\lambda=1$ is CQG, and our decay experiments use $\lambda>1$, where the ideal curled torus lies exactly
$4(\lambda-1)$ per vertex above the zero-energy states. For a specified Metropolis edge-switch chain,
counting the exits offered per sweep predicts the mean first-exit time with no fitted parameter,
consistently with direct measurements. During conversion most vertices keep a curled-like or
flat-like square count, and one connected flat-like region usually dominates at half conversion,
though several early patches occur. Reconstruction of all 120 original endpoints at
$\lambda=1.05$ certifies narrow tori with shortest noncontractible loop six at every tested size;
the conversion does not produce two large directions in this sample. At $\lambda=1.25$, energy-conserving runs with a reservoir
escape on a 12-unit budget over $N=48$--192, and exhaustive neutral-class enumeration certifies 12 as
the lower bound at those sizes, while the available release grows with $N$. What the release produces
depends on the reservoir's thermal response; full release requires a zero-energy endpoint. Three earlier
registered conversion maps remain inconclusive. A fourth passes its stated criteria for
$\lambda=1.05$--1.30 at $N=64$--192 and its frozen predictions at the held-out $N=288$. These results support a bounded kinetic claim and
leave the conversion mechanism and reservoir equilibration open.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Combinatorial quantum gravity (CQG) \cite{T17,KTB19,T25} treats space as a network: a discrete Ricci
curvature, summed over a graph, favors configurations with geometric structure. Published equilibrium
studies ask how geometry emerges from a random phase, and they do not all simulate the same set of
graphs: Ref.~\cite{KTB19}, Sec.~4, describes a restricted bipartite simulation in which no edge has surplus
squares, whereas Ref.~\cite{T25} discusses the uncapped curvature action, and Ref.~\cite{GV21} finds
first-order behavior in a related model with no surplus price and no fixed bipartition. They are
context, not checks of the same model. Kelly's thesis \cite{Kelly22}, p.~219 and Fig.~4.58,
reports small four-regular hysteresis loops over $N=50$--500 but declines a definitive first-order
inference.

We ask instead how a specified ordered arrangement escapes and converts at fixed coupling. It is a
tube: a torus curled to a period of four edges in one direction. It is stable for now
in the energetic sense: at the sizes enumerated (Sec.~\ref{sec:model}) no single move out of the
perfect torus lowers its energy for the $\lambda$ studied, yet lower states exist. We call its
energy-releasing conversion a ``burp.'' Removal of the four-cycle signature does not establish
macroscopic decompactification: the reconstructed original endpoints retain a six-edge shortest
noncontractible loop as $N$ grows. This is a
deformation next to CQG, not a CQG allotrope in the sense of Ref.~\cite{T24}.

Why deform CQG at all? At $\lambda=1$ an edge carrying three squares costs exactly what an edge
carrying two costs, so the flat torus, the curled torus and the 4-cube are degenerate: conversion
between these ideal states releases no energy (equal energies need not imply equal free energies).
The coefficient
$\lambda$ lifts that degeneracy and changes nothing else: the states, the moves and the hard-core rule
are those of CQG. For $\lambda>1$ the ground states are the arrangements with two squares on every
edge, a condition that fixes neither connectedness nor topology, and each curled direction costs a
known energy per vertex. The family is therefore a controlled laboratory for a change between two
ordered arrangements in which the barrier, the release and the relic can each be counted exactly, and
$\lambda\to1$ connects it to CQG.

Every kinetic statement below (waiting times, barriers, fronts) is about one
Markov chain: the local edge switch used to sample the model \cite{KTB19}, with Metropolis acceptance
and an ordered-pair proposal that are our choices (Ref.~\cite{T25} uses Glauber acceptance). Detailed
balance makes the Boltzmann distribution stationary but does not show mixing at our sizes, and we do not
claim that the chain is the physical time evolution of emergent space. Endpoint energy differences
depend on the Hamiltonian, not the proposal rates. The cheapest single escape cost also depends on
the allowed move set; it is not a measured barrier to completing the change. Rates depend on the proposal and
acceptance rules, including how waiting time scales with size (Sec.~\ref{sec:model}).

A plain-language edition with diagrams accompanies the source (Methods); its chapters are cited as
``PL, Ch.~$n$'' where a concept is developed at greater length.

\section{Model and exact results}
\label{sec:model}

Diagrams for this section are in PL, Chs.~2 and~3.

A state is a simple undirected four-regular graph on a fixed labelled bipartition, $N/2$ vertices on
each side, obeying the hard-core rule: no two vertices share more than two neighbours
\cite{KTB19,T25}. Connectedness is not enforced. The energy is a price list on squares (four-cycles).
With $S$ the number of squares, $S_e$ the number on edge $e$, and $X=\sum_e(S_e-2)_+$ the surplus
squares on edges carrying more than two,
\begin{equation}
H = 16\,(N - S) + 4\lambda X = 4 \sum_e \bigl[(2 - S_e) + \lambda\,(S_e - 2)_+\bigr],
\label{eq:H}
\end{equation}
so a missing square costs 16 and a surplus square $4\lambda$. The hard-core rule limits $S_e\le3$.
At fixed dimensionless coupling $g>0$, which acts as a temperature in the units of $H$, the target is
$\pi(G)\propto e^{-H(G)/g}$, and a valid symmetric proposal is accepted with probability
$\min(1,e^{-\Delta H/g})$. At $\lambda=1$ this is the CQG curvature action: the convention
$H=-4\sum_v\sum_{w\sim v}\kappa(v,w)$ of Ref.~\cite{T25}, Eq.~(21), with
$\kappa(v,w)=-(2-S_{vw})_+/2$ on these bipartite graphs, gives $H=4\sum_e(2-S_e)_+$, each undirected
edge counted once. For $\lambda\ne1$, $H$ is not this curvature action.

\emph{Zero is the floor (exact).} Rewriting Eq.~(\ref{eq:H}) as
\[
H=4\sum_e\bigl[(2-S_e)_+ +(\lambda-1)(S_e-2)_+\bigr]
\]
shows $H\ge0$ for $\lambda\ge1$: no state lies below flat. For $\lambda>1$, $H=0$ if and only if every
edge carries exactly two squares; at $\lambda=1$, at least two. Flat lattice tori are ground states,
and so are disconnected unions of them, so topology must be checked separately.

\emph{The ladder (exact).} Periodic bipartite tori need even periods. Curl one direction of a torus to
period four and the walk around it becomes a square, which puts a third square on every edge of each
wrap-around 4-cycle. For even $L>4$ the $4\times L$ torus therefore has $S=5N/4$ and $X=N$, and sits
$H/N=4(\lambda-1)\equiv\varepsilon$ above a zero-energy state. Curling the other direction too gives
the 4-cube ($4\times4$), with $H/N=8(\lambda-1)$: each curled direction costs
$4(\lambda-1)$ per vertex in these ideal products (Fig.~\ref{fig:tori}). A decay that ends with
residual graph energy $H_f$ releases $Q=\varepsilon N-H_f$, which is $\varepsilon N$ only for a
zero-energy endpoint.

\emph{Counting signatures.} A vertex has six pairs of incident edges. Let $d(v)$ be the number of
pairs that close no square: 2 on a flat lattice torus, 1 on a one-curled torus, 0 on the 4-cube. This
is not a geometric dimension. Under the hard-core rule a pair
closes at most one square, so with $q_v$ squares through $v$, $d(v)=6-q_v$ and $\sum_vd(v)=6N-4S$.
The recorded conversion coordinate is
\[
f=\frac{5N/4-S}{N/4}=\overline d-1,
\]
which equals the fraction of vertices at $d=2$ only when no signature other than $d=1$ and $d=2$ is
present. Melting raises $f$ too, and even $d=2$ does not certify
flat order (a valid single switch at $N=64$ produces vertices whose incident edges carry $[1,2,2,3]$
squares, not $[2,2,2,2]$). We therefore call $d=1$ curled-like and $d=2$ flat-like counting signatures.

\emph{Other stuck states at $N=18$ (exact enumeration).} Enumerating all 26 graph classes and solving
the inequalities $H>0$ and $\Delta H>0$, linear in $\lambda$, for every valid single switch, with no grid in
$\lambda$, gives precisely two strict local minima above the ground state on $\lambda\ge1$:
$(S,X)=(21,12)$ on the open interval $1<\lambda<8/5$ and $(22,16)$ on $1<\lambda<4/3$. This
small-system certificate is not a large-$N$ phase diagram.

\emph{The way out: counting the exits.} The chain's one move swaps one partner each between two vertices of
the same side (an edge switch preserving the bipartition), accepted by the Metropolis rule with the
hard-core rule enforced. A proposal draws two edges one after the other, each uniformly from the $2N$
edges (a vertex of one side and one of its four slots). There are $4N^2$ equally likely ordered
proposals, and every switch is proposed in two ways. A sweep is $2N$ attempts.

From the perfect $4\times L$ torus, at the sizes enumerated below, the cheapest switches are of two
kinds. Move A ($\Delta S=-2$, $\Delta X=-4$) costs $32-16\lambda$ and can be made in $3N$ ways, hence
$6N$ ordered proposals; move B ($\Delta S=-4$, $\Delta X=-10$) costs $64-40\lambda$ and can be made in
$2N$ ways, $4N$ proposals. A sweep therefore offers move A $2N\times6N/4N^2=3$ times and move B twice,
whatever $N$. Weighting each offer by its acceptance gives a two-channel approximation to the mean
first-exit time with nothing fitted,
\begin{equation}
\tau_{AB}(\lambda, g) = \bigl[3 e^{-(32 - 16\lambda)/g} + 2 e^{-(64-40\lambda)/g}\bigr]^{-1},
\label{eq:tau}
\end{equation}
with A the cheaper for $\lambda<4/3$ and both costs positive in the kinetic range used here. For a
constant full per-attempt hazard $p$ the exact first-exit law is geometric, with mean $1/(2Np)$
sweeps; the exponential is its small-$p$ approximation.

First, $N/2$ switches change neither $S$ nor $X$: each reconnects two
neighbouring columns at opposite points, twisting the torus globally without leaving its energy.
Breadth-first enumeration exhausts the neutral classes reached this way at $\lambda=1.25$ for
$N=48,64,96,144,192,288$. At each size there are three side-preserving isomorphism classes, all with
the same full list of moves and minimum exit cost 12. Every valid switch is checked at each
representative and every neutral target belongs to the exhausted list; since relabellings map switches
bijectively, this certifies closure at these sizes, not for arbitrary $L$. Second,
Eq.~(\ref{eq:tau}) leaves out every dearer exit. Each costs at least 10 more than the cheaper of A and
B for $1.05\le\lambda\le1.45$, but those that break two distant edges at once are offered a number of
times per sweep that grows in proportion to $N$ (87 at $N=192$). Counted exactly, they add at most
2\% to the exit rate at the $g=1.5$ conditions of the decay maps, and 16\% at the hottest point of
Fig.~\ref{fig:data}(a) ($g=2.5$, $N=144$), within its error bar. Third, that Eq.~(\ref{eq:tau}) does
not depend on $N$ is a property of the proposal, not of the barrier: the chain draws its two edges
from the whole graph, so any one region is offered a way out about $1/N$ as often per sweep. On a
size-fair clock, which gives every region the same chances at every $N$ ($N/96$ sweeps to the unit, as
in the companion paper on the relic), the waiting time falls as $1/N$.

\section{The cost of leaving}
\label{sec:cost}

At $\lambda=1.25$ the cheapest move out of the perfect curled torus, move A, costs
$\Delta E^\ddagger=12$. This opens the door: it is a first-exit cost, not a proven minimum of the
maximum energy along a complete conversion path (the minimax barrier of energy-landscape analysis
\cite{LLC26a}), and whether an excursion then grows or falls back is
a separate question (Sec.~\ref{sec:exit}). The neutral-class enumeration of Sec.~\ref{sec:model} makes
12 a lower bound at the enumerated sizes; an energy-conserving test asks whether it is also enough
(PL, Ch.~4).

To seal the system, that is, to make it isolated with its total energy strictly conserved
(microcanonical dynamics), we replace the external bath at fixed $g$ by a Creutz reservoir
\cite{Creutz83}: $C$ stores
of nonnegative energy. Each valid candidate move selects one store uniformly; the move is refused if
that store cannot pay $\Delta H$, and otherwise the store's energy changes by $-\Delta H$, so that
$E_{\rm tot}=H+\sum_iE_i$ is conserved. Stores and edge proposals are both selected globally: a seed
is a total activation budget, not energy deposited at a chosen vertex. The recorded maximum drift of
$E_{\rm tot}$ is zero in all T9 runs at $\lambda=1.25$ (an observed check, not a claim about bitwise
arithmetic at arbitrary $\lambda$).

\emph{A push that does not grow with size (exploratory).} With one store initially holding
$s\in\{4,8,10,11,11.5,12,13,16\}$, the torus left its initial $(S,X)$ values in 8 of 8 runs at each
tested $s\ge12$ and in 0 of 8 at each $s\le11.5$, for each $N=48,64,96,144,192$ (320 runs of 12,000
sweeps). The lower half of this staircase is exact at these sizes: by the neutral-class certificate
of Sec.~\ref{sec:model} all moves that change the energy cost at least 12, so nonnegative stores with total
budget below 12 cannot leave the basin. The upper half is an observation, dependent on protocol and
duration. Eight successes give a two-sided exact 95\% binomial interval of $[0.631,1]$ at an
individual setting (zero of eight gives $[0,0.369]$), and the runner checks $(S,X)$ once per sweep,
so its success flag measures escape, not completed conversion. The push is fixed while the available
ideal-state energy decrease $\varepsilon N$ grows with the system.

\section{The first exit}
\label{sec:exit}

Table~\ref{tab:clocks} defines the clocks used below, which are not interchangeable; PL,
Ch.~5, develops the rate count.

\emph{Testing the count (exploratory).} The prediction was written into the run's configuration
before it ran, but not pre-registered. Over twelve conditions ($g=1.4$ to 2.5, $N=64$ and 144, 16 runs
each) and a ninety-fold range of times, the ratio of the measured mean waiting time to the move-A term
of Eq.~(\ref{eq:tau}) has a plain mean of 0.98, every ratio between 0.71 and 1.62
[Fig.~\ref{fig:data}(a)]. The scan reuses each replica's seed across couplings, so its cells are not
independent; the direct count below is the rate test.

\emph{The direct count (T22).} Forty further runs in each of six cells ($\lambda=1.05,1.10,1.25$;
$N=64,96$) count exits attempt by attempt. All 240 first exits are observed, and their means are
consistent with Eq.~(\ref{eq:tau}) in the exponential-model 95\% intervals in the Supplemental
Material. An opened door is not a departure, though: many exits fall back into the curled torus. Of
the 240 runs, 239 reach $f\ge0.25$ before the cap, and for these completed runs the reciprocal of
mean exits per run is 0.56--0.68, with replica-bootstrap intervals spanning roughly 0.45--0.78 across
cells. This estimates successful departures per counted exit under the $f\ge0.25$ commitment rule. It
is not a correction factor for the exit rate, which would need independent excursions and the time
spent in failed ones and in growth.

\emph{A memoryless wait? (exploratory).} A constant chance of exit per sweep gives a memoryless,
exponential wait. The coefficient of variation is a coarse diagnostic, not sufficient to establish
memorylessness. Dividing each time by its condition's mean and pooling over conditions, the 432
first exits we recorded give a Kolmogorov--Smirnov distance $D=0.035$ from the unit exponential
(nominal $p=0.53$; shared-stream caveat in Methods) [Fig.~\ref{fig:data}(c)]. The 240 of them counted
move by move (T22, above) are consistent with an exponential using the mean of Eq.~(\ref{eq:tau}),
nothing estimated ($p=0.85$). This does not establish exponential tails in each cell.
Detected-decay waits show stronger departures, including the later T38 tail-test failures
(Sec.~\ref{sec:lambda}).

\section{The conversion}
\label{sec:conversion}

The decay itself was pre-registered: at
$\lambda=1.25$, $g=1.5$, thirty decays at each of $N=64,96,144,192$, with the adjacency read when the
conversion fraction first crossed 25, 50 and 75\%. The predictions, averaged across replicas, were a
memoryless waiting time (coefficient of variation between 0.7 and 1.3) and, at half conversion, at
least 80\% of vertices at $d\in\{1,2\}$ and the converted vertices in one piece holding at least 70\%
of them. All three component tests hold at every size and on two independent sets of seeds
(Table~\ref{tab:t7}). They show a dominant connected flat-like region in the evolving graph at half
conversion, not unique nucleation or a single moving interface. The measured means show no clear
decrease with $N$ over these sizes; detected waits need not have the same size dependence as first
exits.

The same pre-registration listed $\lambda=1.5$;
there the torus does not persist under the tested protocol at $g=1.5$ (its number of squares per
vertex falls to 0.95, below the flat torus's 1, within 50 sweeps in every replica), and no verdict was
applied. A separate pre-registered test of whether a coarse law of a single front governs these
decays (T12; 64 decays at $g=1.5$ and 2.0, under Metropolis and Glauber acceptance) came out NOT
ESTABLISHED: 21 of the 59 that nucleated converted in more than one patch, mostly at the warmer
coupling, and the front's rate per sweep fell roughly as $N^{-0.7}$ (on the size-fair clock, $N^{+0.3}$).

The 25 and 75\% snapshots, recorded but not registered, give the same
picture: the mean share at $d \in \{1,2\}$ is 0.988--0.997 and 0.985--0.995 across the eight cells, against
0.988--0.998 at 50\% (PL, Ch.~6).

\emph{What is released.} The energy difference between the ideal states is exact; what a given decay
releases depends on where it ends. After settling, 24 to 28 of 30 decays per size have a
window-averaged release within 1\% of $\varepsilon$ per vertex. This energy criterion alone does not
certify torus topology. The other endpoints hold defects, read from their wiring: most often
combinations of small defects of 8 to 45 units, and less often the four-point relic of
Sec.~\ref{sec:sealed}, a closed loop of four vertices at $d=1$ with $S=N+1$ and, in its commonest
form, $X=6$, costing $24\lambda-16$ (14 at $\lambda=1.25$). Whether these states are stable or only
slow is not known. The energy criteria were written after endpoints had been inspected, so this part
is exploratory. Under them the full two-state classification holds at $N=64,96,192$, while one
$N=144$ run fails because its state changes inside the final measuring window. The prespecified
waiting and morphology criteria pass at all four sizes in both sets of thirty runs; a passing
component test is not a passing combined classification.

\emph{A count is not a geometry.} At $\lambda=1.05$ all 120 saved endpoints of the map of
Sec.~\ref{sec:lambda} have zero energy. Filling every square as a face and checking two faces per
edge, a cyclic vertex link, connectedness, orientability and Euler characteristic zero certifies
torus topology for all 120 graphs. A retrospective translation-lattice reconstruction now also
checks every adjacency. Each is a square-grid quotient isomorphic to a $4\times L$ tube with a
two-site seam shift. Its shortest noncontractible loop is six edges; a shortest independent loop
has length $L=N/4=16,24,36,48$. The six-edge loop does not make it a rectangular $6\times L$ torus.

The same retrospective audit covers all 3,521 zero-energy saved endpoints in the reached subset
of T60 (35 cells, including $N=288$). Of these, 3,446 are connected tori with shortest loop six,
73 are disconnected closed square surfaces, and two are connected nonorientable surfaces with
Euler characteristic zero, hence Klein bottles. Surface checks include cyclic vertex links;
width alone cannot certify topology. The two rows outside the reached subset also end at zero
energy after settling, but are excluded from this denominator. The supplement gives the selection,
algorithm and per-file certificates. This is a finite-sample result, not an arbitrary-size theorem.

A seam shift of $2m$ sites gives a zero-energy torus with shortest loop $4+2m$ (verified for
$m\le4$, $L\ge24$), so the energy neither favors nor opposes widening beyond six: it prices
four-cycles and nothing longer. Counted as lattice wrappings (not isomorphism classes, and not a
statement about what the dynamics reaches), zero-energy tori with shortest loop six are 8.5\% of the
236 wrappings at $N=192$ and 5.2\% of 387 at $N=288$. Every switch out of a zero-energy torus costs
energy: by exhaustive listing at $N=48$ and 96, at least $16\lambda$ from the torus with shortest loop six
(no square broken, four surplus squares made) and at least 32 from those with shortest loop eight to
twelve. The observed endpoints are the nearest one.

A separate equilibrium scan at $\lambda=1.25$ has a single energy-distribution peak over the tested
sizes and couplings and is INCONCLUSIVE under its stated rules. Neither that finite-size observation
nor the kinetic conversion studied here establishes a thermodynamic first-order transition.

\section{Sealed system: the reservoir and the relic}
\label{sec:sealed}

Sealed, the released energy has to go somewhere, and the $C$ stores of Sec.~\ref{sec:cost} are the
only place (PL, Ch.~7).

\emph{Reading a temperature off the stores.} At $\lambda=1.25$, $\Delta H=-16\Delta S+5\Delta X$ is an integer.
The initial T9 stores contain 0 or 12, and allowed exit costs include 12 and 25, with greatest common
divisor one, so store energies have no common increment coarser than one, though this does not prove every
allocation can be reached. If in equilibrium with a graph at fixed coupling (canonical), an
unbounded store with levels $k\delta$ has a geometric energy distribution, whose mean $u_D$ is tied
to the coupling by
\[
u_D(g)=\frac{\delta}{e^{\delta/g}-1},\qquad
g=\frac{\delta}{\ln(1+\delta/u_D)}.
\]
For $\delta=1$, $u_D(3.5)=3.024$, and $u_D=0.5$ corresponds to $g=0.910$. We report the coupling inferred this way
only as a proxy valid at equilibrium: store histograms were not saved, so their distribution cannot be
checked.

Each move and its reverse are equally likely, so the uniform distribution over reachable graph-and-store
states at fixed total energy is stationary, though not shown to be reached. If all integer bath allocations at
energy $M=E_{\rm tot}-H(G)\ge0$ are accessible, their number is $\Omega_C(M)=\binom{M+C-1}{C-1}$, and
each graph's weight is multiplied by this count. Changing $C$ therefore changes the entropy
as well as the thermal response.

\emph{Enough room, or not.} The seed sits in one of $C$ stores, and $C$ sets the reservoir's heat
capacity ($C=1,N/16,N/8,N/4,N/2,N,2N$; $N=64,96,192$; twenty runs each of 30,000 sweeps; 420 runs).
With too little room the
run ends in the registered class ``melted'' (at least 15\% of vertices at $d=0$ or $d\ge3$) in 17 to
20 of 20 runs for $C\le N/16$ and in 9 to 16 of 20 at $C=N/8$. At $C=N/4$ most runs end in neither
class. That class is damage and not the random phase. A sealed run holds $\varepsilon N+s$, and with
$X\ge0$ the squares per vertex cannot fall below $1-(\varepsilon N+s)/16N$, about $15/16$ here; the
154 runs so classified end with 0.94 to 1.00 squares per vertex, against about 0.13 in the random
phase. With enough room,
$C\ge N/2$, the ``sheet'' counting criterion holds in 18 to 20 of 20; 18/20 and
20/20 successes have exact 95\% binomial intervals $[0.683,0.988]$ and $[0.832,1]$. The criterion
requires $f\ge0.9$, at least 90\% at $d=2$, and less than 5\% at $d=0$ or $d\ge3$; it is not a
topology test. The observed crossover lies between $N/4$ and $N/2$ on this coarse grid. A conditional
equilibrium energy-balance estimate at crossover coupling $g_m$ would be
\[
C^*\simeq\frac{\varepsilon N+s-U_{\rm graph}(g_m)}{u_D(g_m)}.
\]
Taking $g_m\simeq3.5$ and neglecting seed and residual graph energy gives $N/3.024$: an approximate
energy budget, not a calibrated crossover prediction. Stores have no upper capacity, so a small bath
heats; it does not fill up. No sustained stall with both signatures present was observed under the
stated criterion and duration; this does not exclude equilibrium coexistence.

\emph{Retained heat does not widen the endpoint.} A later registered test (T61; $N=96$, 192 and,
held out, 384; $\lambda=1.25$ and 1.40; $C/N=2$ to $1/4$) drained, cooled and read the endpoints.
With ample reservoirs at $\lambda=1.25$ they keep a shortest loop of six; with small ones, and at
$\lambda=1.40$, most are square-lattice regions of differing short period joined at defects, often
disconnected. None of the 720 endpoints is a connected zero-energy torus with shortest loop above six.

\emph{What remains: about one relic at every size.} In the coldest box ($C=2N$; T10, also 30,000
sweeps) a scrap of the old arrangement usually remains. A relic here is a connected component of the $d=1$ induced subgraph in the final snapshot, classified further where
wiring is available. It is the four-point loop above (14 units), its points lying along the torus and
spanning one to four of the torus's original columns, or occasionally a two-vertex twist (20 units).
The number of relics per converted torus is $0.90\pm0.31$, $0.85\pm0.37$, $1.05\pm0.22$ and
$0.95\pm0.22$ at $N=64,96,192,288$ (mean $\pm$ standard deviation over twenty runs each; a weighted
fit against $N$ gives a slope of $0.0003\pm0.0003$ per vertex, standard error). So the count is of
order one, with no measurable growth, across the sizes we could reach. Where the relic sits shows no
detected association with where the change began: the pre-registered test of whether it sits opposite
the start or at it finds neither, and, checked afterwards, a randomisation test that rotates each
relic through every column of its torus with the start held fixed places the observed mean distance
at $p=0.67$ and $0.59$ ($N=64$ and 96; 36 and 28 runs with one relic). These tests can miss other
positional structure. Two cautions. The coupling read off the stores ranges from 0.84 to 1.01 across the
final baths, so even read at equilibrium it does not support a blanket ``below $g=1$''
statement. And the relic count is a finite-duration observation, not a conclusion about the
equilibrium density of defects.

\section{Dependence on \texorpdfstring{$\boldsymbol{\lambda}$}{lambda}}
\label{sec:lambda}

T8 turns the knob: $\lambda=1.05$ to 1.45 in steps of 0.05 at $g=1.5$, using four sizes and thirty
runs per cell (Table~\ref{tab:lambda}); its $\lambda=1.25$ comparison uses T7's data. The registered
criterion for a torus that waits (persistence) is that more than half the runs have $f_{200}<0.25$.
It holds from 1.05 to 1.35 at every size, and fails
at 1.40 and 1.45. This is a boundary of the persistence criterion, not where the barrier vanishes: move B
still costs energy until $\lambda=1.6$. Nor does Eq.~(\ref{eq:tau}) derive it. The equation gives a
mean of 200 sweeps near $\lambda=1.345$, but an exponential first exit with 50\% survival at 200 would
instead require a mean $200/\ln2$, and $f_{200}$ also includes growth.

In persistent cells, means at half conversion give 95--100\% in the two counting signatures and 76--100\% of flat-like vertices in the
largest component. Release is fullest nearest $\lambda=1$: the full-window-release fraction falls to 79\%
at 1.30 and 46\% at 1.35 (the table retains this energy-window criterion rather than treating it as a
topology test). The topology certificate for the $\lambda=1.05$ endpoints is in
Sec.~\ref{sec:conversion}; PL, Ch.~8, walks through the map.

\emph{Three maps, each inconclusive.} Three independently seeded maps fail their full prespecified
acceptance criteria. In the thirty-run map (T8), the wait CV falls outside 0.7--1.3 in nine of 24
persistent cells, and the endpoint-energy criterion fails for two to five runs per size at
$\lambda=1.35$. In a 120-run map (T23), the sample-size-adjusted exponential CV criterion passes
through 1.25 and in three sizes at 1.30, but the energy-window criterion fails at all sizes at 1.30
(thermal window averages need not match the energy of a saved endpoint). A second 120-run map (T24)
tests endpoint validity and reports energy separately: all required saved graphs are valid, but the CV
criterion fails at $(N,\lambda)=(64,1.25),(64,1.30),(192,1.30),(144,1.35)$, and validity is not
evidence for full release. All three combined classifications are INCONCLUSIVE, while their
counting-signature and dominant-component means pass through $\lambda=1.30$. Full rules and cell
results accompany the data.

Several early patches are common even when one component dominates later. From $\lambda=1.30$ to
1.35, the fraction of runs with several flat-like pieces at quarter conversion rises from 0.44 to 0.69
in T23 and 0.43 to 0.68 in T24, while the corresponding full-release or zero-energy fractions fall
from 0.71 to 0.47 and 0.74 to 0.49, respectively (T24's ``FLAT'' class means $H=0$ only).

\emph{What the detector times.} The decay detector first rests,
taking the spread of $S/N$ over a run's first 200 sweeps as that run's noise, and only then checks
every five sweeps whether $S/N$ has fallen more than three times that spread below its curled value
(Table~\ref{tab:clocks}; the ``watch'' of PL, Ch.~8). Its mean wait $W$ and the first-exit
prediction, compared over two decades in Fig.~\ref{fig:lambda}(a), are therefore distinct
observables, for two reasons. First, nothing is recorded before sweep 205. For the idealized floor
$W=\max(T,a)$ with exponential $T$ of mean $\tau$, $E[W]=a+\tau e^{-a/\tau}$, which at $a=205$ gives
462 and 265 sweeps at $\lambda=1.30$ and 1.35, against 403--505 and 280--350 measured (the dashed
curve: this floor alone, not a calibrated detector prediction). Second, the threshold is the run's own: a torus that begins
to change inside the window widens its threshold and is caught late or never, which changes both the
threshold and the sample selection (Methods).

The second effect is how we read the two exceptional T24 waits at $N=64$: 20,270 sweeps
($\lambda=1.25$) and 32,155 (1.30), or 23.98 and 76.74 times the predicted first-exit means. They are
not waits of a resting torus. The rows saved for those two runs record $f_{200}=0.75$ and 0.875 and
place the 25, 50 and 75\% snapshots at sweep 205, the first check after the resting window: both tori
had converted three quarters or more inside the window from which the detector estimates
$\sigma_{\rm rest}$. The threshold was correspondingly low, and $W$ records when $S/N$ at last fell
below it, from endpoints that retained energy (window releases 0.744 of 1.000 and 0.563 of 1.200 per
vertex).

\emph{Dedicated tail test (T38).} A prespecified tail count asked whether long waits form a second
population. With $w'=W-200$ and $\widehat\tau=\operatorname{median}(w')/\ln2$, it counts the waits
$k_{10}$ above $10\widehat\tau$: TAIL for $k_{10}\ge3$, NO TAIL for $k_{10}\le1$, otherwise UNCLEAR.
At $\lambda=1.25$ the result is UNCLEAR at $N=64$ ($k_{10}=2$; 3942 of 4000 runs recorded) and NO
TAIL at $N=192$ ($k_{10}=1$; 1000 of 1000); at 1.30 it is TAIL at both sizes ($k_{10}=14$, 3868 of
4000; and $k_{10}=5$, 1000 of 1000).
Two TAIL cells give the registered classification TWO POPULATIONS, which stands as scored; it does
not establish two physical basins. A retrospective, exploratory reading of the saved rows, made
after the data were seen (no new run; Methods and Supplemental Material), uses two recorded
quantities that do not depend on the detector's threshold, $f_{200}$ and the sweep of the first
sampled $f\ge0.25$, and accounts for most of it by the resting window. (i) All 190 runs without a
detector crossing have $f_{200}\ge0.75$ and reach $f\ge0.25$ at sweep 205: they converted inside the
window and are the fastest runs, not long survivors. The tail statistic excludes them. (ii) The
fastest detected waits are exits inside the window: of the runs detected within 20 sweeps of its end,
80--84\% have $f_{200}>0$, and the fraction of all runs with $f_{200}>0$ is 0.144 and 0.146 at
$\lambda=1.25$ ($N=64$ and 192) and 0.274 and 0.314 at 1.30, against $1-e^{-200/\tau_{AB}}=0.211$ and
0.380 for a first exit before sweep 200. (iii) The scale $\widehat\tau$ is taken over all detected
waits, those fast ones included: it is 750, 671, 267 and 238 sweeps in the four cells, against means
of 917, 858, 443 and 446 for runs with $f_{200}=0$. The cutoff $10\widehat\tau$ is therefore only
5.7--8.9 $\tau_{AB}$, and the reference expectation $ne^{-10}$ does not apply to it. Measured against
their own mean, the $f_{200}=0$ runs have 0, 0, 3 and 0 waits beyond ten means, where 0.16, 0.04,
0.13 and 0.03 are expected.

That leaves three waits at $\lambda=1.30$, $N=64$ ($W=4705$, 5000 and 5080). A registered replay of
that cell from its seeds (T58) returns all 4,000 saved rows unchanged. For the three runs it records
every five-sweep block: before detection the square count never exceeds the torus's, no vertex reads
$d=0$, and the graph stays connected. One wait is the window again: an excursion inside it lowered
the threshold, and three later exits that fell back went undetected. The other two read as the
perfect torus at every block, for 5,000 and 4,670 sweeps. Neither registered prediction for the
three held (a hidden doubly curled state; a lowered threshold in all three). Those for the cell did.
Timed with no detector, the
first exits of the 4,000 runs have mean $1.071\pm0.017$ $\tau_{AB}$ (four standard errors above the
count; Methods), one lies beyond $10\tau_{AB}$ (0.18 expected), and no run ever exceeds the torus's
square count. An exploratory replay of these runs, read after every sweep, finds no sweep with more
squares than the torus and separates the two: the $W=5000$ run is the perfect torus through sweep
4,998 ($11.9\,\tau_{AB}$), while the $W=4705$ run left it for three sweeps between two block
readings, $7.1\,\tau_{AB}$ after the window. One long wait remains, which chance allows about one
time in six.

A second registered run (T59) settles two points this leaves. Counted by the move in 4,000 fresh runs
at the same setting, the first exit has mean $1.005\pm0.016\,\tau_{AB}$. Read every five sweeps, the
same runs give a mean later by $0.096\pm0.007\,\tau_{AB}$, and 94\% of that comes from first exits
that return before the reading. The 7\% belongs to the reading interval, not to the count. At
$\lambda=1.05$, where three waits near $9.5\,\tau_{AB}$ had appeared among 200 (Methods), 3,000 fresh
move-counted first exits have means $0.992\pm0.023$ ($N=64$) and $0.946\pm0.031\,\tau_{AB}$ ($N=96$),
with one beyond $8\tau_{AB}$ where 1.0 is expected.

\emph{Fourth map and held-out size (T60).}
A fourth pre-registered map uses move-level first exits and 120 replicas per cell. All 24
window cells, $\lambda=1.05,1.10,\ldots,1.30$ and $N=64,96,144,192$, pass its stated gates and
waiting-CV, local-signature and largest-component criteria. Frozen predictions for all six
window settings pass at $N=288$, where the conversion criteria also pass. At $\lambda=1.35$
the largest-component criterion fails at $N=192$ and 288. These are classifications by the stated criteria,
not evidence sufficient to classify an equilibrium transition.

At $N=288,\lambda=1.30$, the mean largest-component fraction at half conversion is 0.751181,
above the 0.7 threshold, but only 65 of 120 individual replicas exceed 0.7. A CV acceptance
band tests one summary statistic, not the full exponential distribution. Moreover, a retrospective
application of T24's residual-wait CV criterion to the T60 rows passes all 24 smaller-size
window cells too. Thus the fourth map's success cannot be uniquely attributed to the repaired
first-exit clock: the sample and diagnostic changed. The three earlier verdicts remain inconclusive.

\section{Discussion}

The burp belongs to the $\lambda>1$ family. At $\lambda=1$, which is CQG, the flat, curled and 4-cube
tori are degenerate and the available decrease $\varepsilon=4(\lambda-1)$ vanishes: the change has
zero ideal-state energy difference.

The strongest quantitative kinetic result is that counting moves predicts the mean first exit with
nothing fitted, consistently with directly counted exits and with the stated correction for the dearer
exits left out. The energetic lower bound for sealed escape, 12 units at $\lambda=1.25$, is certified over the
tested sizes, while the available release grows with the system. Conversion snapshots support
dominance by a connected flat-like region, but do not establish a unique seed or one moving boundary.
Endpoint geometry is certified for the stated T8 and reached zero-energy T60 sets; the reconstructed
tori remain narrow, and retained heat did not widen them in a registered test (T61). Finite-bath
equilibration and geometric opening in other regimes remain open.
The detected-decay tails are largely accounted for, retrospectively, by
conversion inside the detector's resting window. A replay of one cell (Sec.~\ref{sec:lambda})
finds one more such case and one wait of a length chance allows. Counted by the move in 7,000 fresh
runs, first exits are on the count at $\lambda=1.05$ and 1.30 with no detected excess of long waits; five-sweep readings
lengthen the mean by 8--10\% because some first exits return unseen.
The inconclusive maps and the T38 classification are part of the result.

\emph{Relatives in condensed-matter kinetics (context, not validation).} Energy-conserving conversion
that ends with residual defects has relatives outside this model. In an isolated electron-lattice
chain, order forms after a sudden change by nucleation and growth and can end arrested, with immobile
defects, when the energy available falls below a local barrier \cite{JC26}. The defects there are
kinks between two symmetry-related orders, removed in pairs, and the dynamics is a physical equation
of motion; neither is established here. The barrier to completing the change, which this paper does not
compute, is in that literature a minimax barrier, obtained by exact enumeration on small systems and displayed as a
disconnectivity graph \cite{LLC26a}.

Not claimed: that this chain is the physical time evolution of anything; a first-order transition in the
thermodynamic sense; a latent heat; isotropic decompactification; a unique moving front; a general
large-size law beyond the enumerated and measured sizes; an explanation for the cold-box defect count; or any rate for interchangeable (unlabelled)
vertices, which would change the rates here but not the energies.

\emph{Outlook (motivation, not a result).} This work is motivated by the hypothesis that space emerged from a
specific ordered arrangement through a sharp change of this kind, a lattice cousin of false-vacuum decay
\cite{C77}, releasing a definite energy and leaving relics behind. Nothing here tests that hypothesis beyond
showing classical activated escape and energy-releasing conversion in a specified graph model.
No tunneling or Lorentzian spacetime dynamics has been computed. Measuring growth of both geometric periods,
interfaces, and histories recorded attempt by attempt up to completion would make the next tests
more discriminating.

\section*{Methods and data}

\emph{Sampler and validation.} Simple bipartite switches preserve degree and the hard-core rule;
invalid proposals are rejected. All clocks use sweeps of $2N$ attempts.
The stationary measure follows from symmetric proposals and Metropolis detailed balance.
Exhaustive small-graph checks and canonical averages validate the sampler at $N=16,18$;
ergodicity and equilibration at the sizes run are not proved. The square-density comparison at
$N=160$ agrees with Ref.~\cite{KTB19}, Fig.~8a, to rms 0.005 but differs from Ref.~\cite{T25}, Fig.~3,
by up to 0.41 between $g=2$ and 6.3. Given the differing stated protocols discussed in the Introduction,
this remains an unresolved reproduction issue, not a validation of the entire uncapped equilibrium curve.

\emph{Conversion protocol.} The runner advances in five-sweep blocks, recording square counts and
the threshold crossings in Table~\ref{tab:clocks}. The main conversion stage stops at $f\ge0.98$
or the configured cap; the settling stage follows even if that crossing was not reached.
In T7b and T8, settling uses 600-sweep windows. The current stopping rule requires two consecutive
window releases to agree within $0.005\varepsilon$ and the last to lie within $0.01\varepsilon$
of full release. Otherwise it runs to the configured settle cap. Defected endpoints can therefore
exhaust the cap even when long-lived. T7 uses a fixed 300-sweep settling stretch.
T7b's nominal main and settle caps are 30,000 sweeps each; T8 uses 100,000 for each.
Recorded replay configurations extend selected T7 settling caps. The frozen configuration, not the
nominal duration, identifies each replay. Saved final graphs are post-settling states, whereas the
25/50/75\% histograms are taken during conversion.

\emph{Analysis design.} Waiting and morphology criteria for the conversion maps, and the dedicated
tail-count test, were fixed before their respective runs, as were the gate, labels and cell
predictions of the T58 replay (one of its three target rows already implied a lowered threshold)
and of T59 (one of its seven replay targets had been replayed before registration). Endpoint-energy criteria were partly
chosen after inspecting data, so the energy classification is exploratory. The Arrhenius and seed
scans, the sweep-by-sweep replay, pooled distribution checks, topology certificates and interval estimates are also exploratory
analyses, not independent confirmatory experiments. Identifiers such as T8 and T38 point to the
configurations and analyses in the Supplemental Material.
Our computational derivations remain unverified by an independent physicist.

\emph{Uncertainty and selection.} Replicas, not vertices, are the independent units within a setting. We report 95\% exact binomial intervals for success fractions, percentile bootstrap intervals
for replica means (10,000 resamples, seed 20261009), and exponential-model intervals
$[2\sum T/\chi^2_{2m,0.975},2\sum T/\chi^2_{2m,0.025}]$ for complete first-exit samples.
The last are conditional on the continuous exponential approximation, not distribution-free bounds.
For the T7b largest-component means, the intervals at $N=64,96,144,192$ are respectively
$[0.983,1]$, $[0.928,0.986]$, $[0.906,0.995]$, $[0.864,0.971]$.
The median is one in each cell, but 0, 1, 3 and 5 of the thirty individual runs have a fraction
below 0.7 (0, 2, 4 and 9 in the first set). Thus a passing mean does not describe every replica.
Map waiting summaries are restricted to runs reaching $f\ge0.75$; this selection
can favor successful conversions. Counts of observed events, omissions and snapshot availability
are given in the Supplemental Material.

\emph{Exploratory distribution checks.} We divide each wait by its cell mean
and compare the pooled sample with the unit exponential using the Kolmogorov--Smirnov distance $D$.
A parametric bootstrap refits cell means in each of 10,000 simulated datasets. It reproduces the
sampling grid and residual-time convention, not the threshold set by each run's first 200 sweeps.
For the 432 first exits, two of eighteen individual cells have $p<0.05$, both at $\lambda=1.05$. The 240 attempt-counted T22 exits, normalized by Eq.~(\ref{eq:tau}) without fitted
means, give $D=0.039$, $p=0.85$. The Arrhenius scan reuses a seed across couplings at each
size and replica. The pooled bootstrap simulates cells independently, so its 432-wait $p$-value
does not model this dependence and is nominal. T22 includes $\lambda$ in its seed derivation.
For detected waits, the unfiltered T7/T8 residual sample (865 waits with $W>205$) gives $p=0.03$;
T7 alone gives $p<10^{-3}$. Selecting T8 cells passing persistence, $f_{200}=0$ and $W>205$ gives
589 waits with $D=0.030$, $p=0.55$. Having $f_{200}=0$ does not rule out an earlier exit and return.
Pooling and fitting means can mask cell
heterogeneity, and KS is not a decisive far-tail test.
Three of 200 mixed first-exit and detected waits at $\lambda=1.05$ exceed 75,000 sweeps
(9.4--9.7 $\tau_{AB}$). A registered audit (T59) replayed them from their seeds. The detected one
read as the torus at every block. The two move-counted ones were offered exits A and B at 2.94--3.03
and 1.94--2.04 per sweep in every 5,000-sweep stretch, against the counted 3 and 2. With the fresh
sample of Sec.~\ref{sec:lambda} we read the three as chance; two such first exits among 80 have
probability about $10^{-5}$, computed after seeing them.

\emph{Retrospective reading of the decay rows.} The reading in Sec.~\ref{sec:lambda} uses only
columns saved by the registered runs, \texttt{f\_200} and the snapshot sweeps, through
\path{scripts/read_wait_detector.py}. It was made after the T24 and T38 results were known, is
exploratory, and changes no registered classification. A run is called perfect at sweep 200 when
$f_{200}=0$; this does not exclude an excursion that returned inside the window. The replay (T58)
reruns registered configurations from their seeds with a read-only recorder; its gate requires every
saved column to return unchanged. Its detector-free first exit is the first five-sweep block at which
a run no longer has $S=5N/4$, $X=N$ and every vertex at $d=1$ (the basin of Table~\ref{tab:clocks},
neutral classes included). An exit that returns within a block is not seen (the exploratory
sweep-by-sweep replay of nine runs shows two), which lengthens the measured mean wait. T59 measures
the lengthening for this cell, $0.096\pm0.007\,\tau_{AB}$, by timing fresh runs by the move and by
five-sweep readings together; its readers make the T22 chain's draws in the same order and return
T22's saved first exits to the attempt. No T38 waiting-graph files are in the frozen
\texttt{results/} inventory, so in the three cells not replayed endpoints cannot reconstruct
histories.
