Everything you have ever seen happened in space with three large directions you can move in. We know its
rules well. What we do not know is how it came to be.

This paper tests one piece of one hypothesis about that: a sharp change from something else into
space. In a small world of dots and links it asks three questions. What does it take to start such a
change? What comes out when it happens? What is left behind?

\sub{Who is saying what}
The project keeps four kinds of statement apart, and this edition tags them where it matters, so
that a sentence is never quietly promoted from one kind to another.
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
\item \Exact\ Arithmetic, or a complete listing of every possibility: true whatever a run does.
\item \Meas\ A simulation whose rules and predictions were written down before it ran, with its
verdict. \MeasX\ means a run that was not formally pre-registered, or a closer look at runs already
read.
\item \Ours\ A reading of a result, offered by the author and her AI assistants and checked by no
physicist.
\item \Claim\ The author's hypothesis about reality, stated as hers.
\item \Pub\ Other researchers' published results.
\end{itemize}
Numbers in square brackets point to the sources listed at the end.

\chapterpage{1 \enspace THE PLACE WE START}{We live in space, and we cannot rerun its birth}

\textbf{The author.} Emily Smith is a software engineer, not a physicist, and also a startup founder
and a foster mom. Her recreational time is limited and the computing bills are her own. Fittingly, the
project repository is called RecreationalPhysics. She works, on the side, to map her intuition about how
reality is organized onto what humanity knows about the universe, and to share what she learns with
other curious people.

\textbf{Her hypothesis.} \Claim\ Space is one settled arrangement of something deeper, which she
calls X because nobody knows what it is. X was not chaos. It was a specific arrangement, stable for
now, the way a reagent is stable until something gives it a push. The change that made space started
at a seed, spread as a front, released a definite amount of energy all at once, which became matter
and light, and left a remainder. Across the change the energy books balance.
Figure~\ref{fig:idea} is that paragraph as a picture.

\textbf{The approach.} That is a hypothesis, not a result, and nothing in a computer can prove it
about reality. What a computer can do is take a small world whose rules are written out in full, in
which space is an arrangement and not a given, and ask whether a change of this kind can happen there
at all. If the answer were no, the idea would have no home in that world. If it is yes, the idea gains
a laboratory, and every number in it becomes a thing to check. Three habits keep the laboratory
honest: published results are reproduced first, each prediction is written down before its run, and
a result that goes against the author is reported as prominently as one that goes her way.

\textbf{The parts of this paper.} Chapter~2 is the starting point: a published model, what we
reproduced of it, and the one thing we change. Chapters~3 to~8 are the experiment. They take one
specific arrangement and ask what it is, what it costs to start changing, how long it waits, what
takes shape, where the energy goes, what remains, and whether the answers hold at other settings.
Chapters~9 and~10 say where the simulations stop, what held, what failed, and what the next papers
take up.

\textbf{A toy, and why this one.} Physicists call a small world of that kind a \textbf{toy model}:
simple enough that every consequence of its rules can be counted or simulated, and not to be mistaken
for the real thing. Ours starts from a published one, combinatorial quantum
gravity~\cite{T17,KTB19,T25}, for four reasons. Space is not built into it: there are only dots and
links, and geometry has to form. Its energy is a version of the curvature in Einstein's theory of
gravity. It has published results to check our code against. And at the smallest sizes every possible
arrangement can be listed, which gives exact answers to test the simulations on.

\Pub\ With those two prices the energy measures how curved the network is: a graph version of the
curvature in Einstein's equations of gravity. It has no meters or joules in it. We call its amount a
\textbf{unit}, and leave the conversion into physical energy for a later paper. The published work
also studies a simpler version with the surplus price switched off, where a third square is free.

\Pub\ A run is a long chain of swaps, each one proposed at random. A swap that lowers the energy is
always accepted. A swap that raises it by $\Delta E$ units is accepted with a chance of
$e^{-\Delta E/g}$, where $e$ is a fixed number, about 2.7, and $g$ plays the part of temperature. We
call $g$ \textbf{the warmth}: the bigger the cost, the rarer the swap, and the warmer the network, the
more often a costly swap goes through. Picture an endless \textbf{bath} that lends energy for an uphill swap and
takes back what a downhill swap gives off. Time is counted in \textbf{sweeps}: one sweep is two
proposed swaps for every dot. This is the standard way to sample such a model, not a claim about how
anything moved in real time.

\sub{Reproduced before changed}
\Pub\ The published work starts from a random tangle of links, cools it, and asks how geometry
condenses out of the tangle. Sharply, the way water freezes, giving off heat with ice and water side
by side? (Very clean water can even stay liquid below zero, stuck, until a jolt sets it off.) Or
smoothly, the way a fridge magnet loses its pull as it warms, with no burst of heat and no moment
when half of it has changed? Physicists call the two kinds \textbf{first order} and
\textbf{continuous}. Published work reports continuous behavior for curvature-based
models~\cite{KTB19,T25} and first-order behavior in a related model with no surplus
price~\cite{GV21}, where the network breaks into closed pieces.

\Meas\ We checked published results before extending the model.\footnote{The published simulations are
not identical experiments: the 2019 simulation forbids surplus squares, the later curvature action
permits them, and the study with no surplus price does not use the same fixed two-group networks. Our
square-density curve differs from a 2025 figure~\cite{T25} by up to 0.41 over part of its range. Given
the different stated protocols, agreement with the 2019 curve does not validate our whole curve, and
this reproduction issue remains open. Kelly's doctoral thesis~\cite{Kelly22} adds a hint for four
links per dot: cooled and then warmed again, the network does not quite retrace its path, though the
thesis declines to call the evidence definitive.} Our simulations match the 2019 square-density curve~\cite{KTB19} with a root-mean-square
difference of 0.005 at the same size, match exact averages at sizes small enough to list every
possible arrangement, and reproduce the sharp change into closed pieces with the surplus price off.
Then, with the rules written down first, we measured the jump as the tangle orders. With the
surplus price off it is large, but what forms is a pile of closed pieces, not a space. With the full
price list we found no clear jump at any size we ran, which settles nothing: a small network can hide
a jump a larger one would show.

\sub{The one thing we change}
The toy turns one price into a knob. In the toy a surplus square costs $4\lambda$ units, where
$\lambda$ (lambda) is a number that can be turned up or down, so we call it \textbf{the knob}. With
the knob at exactly $\lambda = 1$ the toy is the published model. With the knob at $\lambda = 0$ it is
the published simpler version. Every other setting is our addition, and there the energy is still a
perfectly good rule for a network, but it is no longer a curvature. So two names are kept apart from
here on. \textbf{The published model} is theirs. \textbf{The toy} is ours: a close cousin of
combinatorial quantum gravity, not the thing itself.

We have not found published work that sets the surplus price to any value above 1~\cite{search}. In
print, as far as we have found, are the two settings just described and, in the text of two of the
papers~\cite{KTB19,KT19}, a version in which a third square is forbidden outright. So, barring a gap in
our search, the approach taken here is new.

Why turn the knob at all? Not to settle the published question: neither this paper nor the author's
idea rests on it. What she asks of the toy is plainer: does a prepared arrangement give way with a jump in energy at
the sizes run, and how large is the jump? Chapter~3 shows how the knob makes room for that question.

\takeaway{The published model is a network with a price list, 16 per missing square and 4 per surplus
square, which makes its energy a curvature. We reproduced it before changing anything; with the full
price list our sizes show no clear jump out of randomness, which settles nothing either way. The toy
changes one thing: a surplus square costs $4\lambda$, and only $\lambda=1$ is the published model.}

\chapterpage{3 \enspace THE PHASE BEFORE}{What if what came before space was not random?}

{\footnotesize\color{ink!55}\textbf{The experiment:} {\color{accent}\textbf{the arrangement}} \textbullet\ the cost \textbullet\ the wait \textbullet\ the change \textbullet\ the energy \textbullet\ other settings\par}

The published question starts from a random tangle. This program asks a different one: what if what
came before space was not random at all, but a specific arrangement, stable for now, with energy to
give? To ask it in the toy, we first need to say what space looks like in a network with no
positions, and what the alternative to it could be.

it is a thin ring, like a hula hoop, four steps around the thin way and $L$ steps around the long way.
The sheet is a fatter doughnut, long both ways (Figure~\ref{fig:shapes}). Nothing in the toy is
open-ended, and nothing can be: every dot must have its four links, so there is no edge to stop at,
and with a finite number of dots a walk that keeps going must come back around.

Two words are kept apart from here on. \textbf{Flat} is about each dot's surroundings: every link on
exactly two squares, the wiring of a plain grid, at zero energy. \textbf{Wide} is about the whole
shape: how many steps it takes to walk around it and come back. A doughnut from a bakery is not flat,
since its skin is stretched on the outside and squeezed around the hole, but the toy's doughnut is the
game-screen kind and is not bent anywhere. The sheet is flat and wide. The tube is neither.

Can anyone inside tell a wide doughnut from an endless grid? Suppose something lived in the network
and could learn only what it reaches by walking links. Let it count the dots it can reach from home in
one step or fewer, then two, then three. \Exact\ On an endless grid the dots it can reach make a
diamond that grows by one ring each step: 5 dots, then 13, then 25, then 41. On a grid that wraps, the
diamond grows the same way until it has spread halfway around. Then the walk that set out one way and
the walk that set out the other way arrive at the same dot from behind, and that dot is counted once
where an endless grid would have two different dots there (Figure~\ref{fig:count}). The count comes out
one short. That is the first thing an inhabitant can notice about the shape it lives in, and it can
notice it only at half the way around: at two steps on the tube, at four steps on a grid eight around,
at six steps on a grid twelve around.

\Exact\ So from inside, a doughnut much wider than its inhabitants is open in every way they can
check: until they walk halfway around, their counts are an endless grid's. The tube is the opposite
case. Its count falls short at two steps, as early as a count can, because its way around is the
smallest loop the rules allow. That direction has no room in it at all, which is what $d=1$ records:
from inside, the tube is little more than a line.

\Pub\ Astronomers put the same question to real space. On the largest scales, observations are
consistent with zero spatial curvature within the cosmological analysis used to interpret
them~\cite{Planck18}, though gravity still curves spacetime around stars and planets. Whether space is
also endless is a separate question. A search of the sky for the repeated patterns that a space closed
up like a doughnut would show found none on scales smaller than the part of space we can
see~\cite{Planck15topo}. Flat has been measured. Endless has not.

\Claim\ This is where the toy meets the author's picture. In it, what came before space was curled
tight, as the tube is, and the change opened it into the space we live in, with three large
directions. \Ours\ For that story, flat is not enough. The space we see is also wide, wider than the
part of it we can observe, while a change that made every point flat and left the ring narrow would
give its inhabitants a world they could walk around in a few steps. So the picture becomes three
questions, taken up in order.
Does the change make a direction flat, or wide as well? Chapter~6 measures it. If only flat, what would
pay for the widening? That is for a later paper. And with three curled directions instead of one,
do they open together, and how wide? That needs a network with six links per dot, which is how a grid gets three directions, and a
later paper.

This is what the knob is for. \Exact\ At the published setting, curled and flat cost the same, so
turning one into the other gives off nothing. Equal cost does not make the two equally likely, and it
does not forbid a change. Turn the knob above 1, and a tube
that ends at zero energy gives off exactly $4(\lambda-1)$ units per dot. That is a reason to study this
cousin of the published model. It is not proof of a first-order transition, and it is not a reason
why nature should choose it.

That release is what the rest of this paper is about, so it gets a name of its own. The author calls
it \textbf{the burp}: the energy given off, and by extension the event that gives it off, a quiet wait,
a start, a spreading change and a definite release.

\Exact\ The changed rule is the published curvature energy plus $4(\lambda-1)$ times the surplus
square count. The extra term vanishes when that count is zero. \Claim\ The author proposes reading
it as a \emph{curling cost}: energy has to be paid to curl space tight, and is given back when it
uncurls. She holds that as something to test. \Exact\ In the toy it is plain bookkeeping: a curled
tube sits $4(\lambda-1)$ per dot above the flat arrangement, so uncurling gives that much back and
curling again would have to pay it. \Ours\ What is not supplied is a derivation. At $\lambda=1$ the
rule is the published graph version of the curvature in Einstein's gravity; nothing known requires
the extra term or fixes its size, so its meaning for nature is unverified.

\sub{Stable for now: is there a window?}
Energy to give is not enough. The clean water of Chapter~2, still liquid below zero, has energy to
give and sits there. For the tube to be
a stand-in for X it must be stuck: leaving its equal-energy family must cost energy. \Exact\ At 18 dots
the toy is small enough to list every allowed arrangement: there are 26, counting arrangements that
differ only in the names of their dots as one. Among them, arrangements above the flat sheet from
which every single swap costs energy exist for knob settings between 1 and 1.6, and for no larger
setting. So the knob has a window, and a narrow one.

\sub{The stand-in}
Our stand-in for X is therefore the tube. At $\lambda = 1.25$ it sits exactly one unit per dot above
the flat sheet. It is a stand-in and not X: it has one direction with room in it where space has three, its
curled direction is always four dots around, and it lives in a network of a few hundred dots. What it
shares with X is the shape of the idea under test: a specific, curled, stable-for-now arrangement with
energy to give.

\sub{The running example}
Take a tube of 96 dots: four around, 24 along. At $\lambda = 1.25$ it holds 96 units above the flat
sheet. That is the burp on offer. We will follow this tube through the rest of the paper so that the
amounts stay concrete. It is a running example, not an invented history: the evidence comes from
hundreds of repeated runs and, where possible, from counting every allowed move.

Now the question that decides whether any of this is a story at all. Ninety-six units are waiting to
come out. What does it cost to start?

\takeaway{A direction, in the toy, is a pair of links at a dot that closes no square. Curling a
direction four steps around turns the walk around it into a square, which puts a surplus square on its
links. The tube has one direction with room in it where the sheet has two, and sits $4(\lambda-1)$ per
dot above it: one unit per dot at $\lambda=1.25$. Both close on themselves: the tube is a thin ring and
the sheet a fatter doughnut, flat at every dot and, from inside, as good as endless to anything much
smaller than the way around it. At $\lambda=1$ there is nothing to release, which is why the
knob sits above 1, and why the toy is a cousin of the published model and not that model itself.}

\chapterpage{4 \enspace THE OBSTACLE}{What does it cost to start?}

{\footnotesize\color{ink!55}\textbf{The experiment:} the arrangement \textbullet\ {\color{accent}\textbf{the cost}} \textbullet\ the wait \textbullet\ the change \textbullet\ the energy \textbullet\ other settings\par}

Before any number, consider what the number could mean. If the push needed were tiny, a few units,
then any warmth at all would set the tube off, and it would not be stable for now. If the push were
as large as the burp itself, 96 units for our tube and more for a bigger one, then starting the
change would cost what the change gives back, and the cost would grow with the size of the tube.
Nothing small could ever start something large. A sharp change of the kind pictured at the start of
this paper
needs a middle answer: a push that is small, and that does not grow with the size of the thing being
pushed, so that a bigger tube releases a bigger burp from the same spark. A fixed push and a growing
release is the condition for a runaway.

So: is the cheapest exit from a perfect tube small, fixed, or neither?

\sub{Pricing the cheapest exit}
\Exact\ Start from a perfect tube and try every swap the rules allow, the one move of Chapter~2. Each
one breaks some squares and removes some surplus, and the price list gives its cost. Most are
expensive. Some cost nothing: they twist the tube around itself without changing any dot's
neighborhood, so they do not count as leaving. Among the swaps that do leave, two kinds are cheapest.
Call them route A and route B. Here are their bills at $\lambda = 1.25$, where a surplus square costs 5:

\sub{Is 12 really the floor?}
One cheap exit from the perfect tube is not the same as no cheaper exit anywhere nearby. The free
twists lead to other arrangements with the same energy, and one of those might have a cheaper door.
\Exact\ So the calculation followed every free twist, at six sizes from 48 to 288 dots, until no new
arrangement appeared. At each size there are exactly three reachable arrangements, counting
arrangements that differ only in the names of their dots as one; every one has the same menu of exits,
and the cheapest costs 12. That is a complete listing at those sizes, not a proof for every size: at
32 dots the pattern is different.

\chapterpage{5 \enspace THE WAIT}{How long before the door opens, and what kind of waiting is it?}

{\footnotesize\color{ink!55}\textbf{The experiment:} the arrangement \textbullet\ the cost \textbullet\ {\color{accent}\textbf{the wait}} \textbullet\ the change \textbullet\ the energy \textbullet\ other settings\par}

A rim of 12 says what a push must be. It does not say when a tube left to itself will go. For that we
put it back in the bath of Chapter~2 and let the network jiggle at warmth $g$. A swap that climbs the
rim, costing 12, is accepted with chance $e^{-12/g}$: warmer, and the rim is cleared more often.

\sub{A lottery with counted tickets}
Now the wait becomes a lottery, and every ticket can be counted. \Exact\ In one sweep the computer
proposes two swaps for every dot, $2N$ in all for a tube of $N$ dots, each one picked at random from
every swap there is. How many of those proposals are exits?

Take route A. A tube of $N$ dots has $3N$ different route-A swaps: three for every dot, so a longer
tube has more ways out. But a longer tube also has more swaps of every other kind, and those grow
faster, as $N$ times $N$. So the chance that any one proposal lands on a route-A exit is 3 in $2N$,
smaller for a longer tube. A sweep makes $2N$ proposals. Multiply the two and the length cancels:
\[
2N \;\times\; \frac{3}{2N} \;=\; 3 .
\]
A sweep offers three route-A exits on average, whatever the size of the tube. A longer tube has more
exits, each one is harder to hit, and with two proposals per dot in a sweep the two effects balance
exactly. The same count for route B gives two.

Each offer of A is accepted with chance $e^{-12/g}$, each offer of B with chance $e^{-14/g}$. So the
chance of leaving in any one sweep is $3\,e^{-12/g} + 2\,e^{-14/g}$, and the mean wait is one divided
by that, with nothing fitted:
\[
\text{mean wait} \;=\; \frac{1}{3\,e^{-12/g} + 2\,e^{-14/g}} \quad\text{sweeps.}
\]
At $g = 1.5$, the warmth used for most runs below, that is 845 sweeps. Chemists know this shape as an
Arrhenius law. Here both of its ingredients, the height of the rim and the number of tries per sweep,
are counted from the arrangement rather than fitted to the data (the technical paper~\cite{tech},
Section~II, gives the formula for any $\lambda$).

\sub{Does the tube keep the appointment?}
\MeasX\ A first scan at $\lambda = 1.25$, six warmths, two sizes and sixteen tubes each, over a
ninety-fold range of waits, came within 2 percent of the count on average.

\Meas\ A stricter, pre-registered test then counted every single accepted swap in 240 further runs
across six settings of $\lambda$ and size, and compared the first exits with the full formula, both
routes. All 240 first exits were observed, and their mean arrived when the formula said it would in
every cell, within the scatter expected from that many runs.

\sub{What kind of waiting}
If the chance of escape is the same on every attempt, the waits are \textbf{memoryless}. It is like
rolling a die until a six comes up: twenty rolls without one do not make a six any likelier on the next
roll, and a tube that has waited a long time is no nearer to leaving. One sign of a memoryless wait is
that the waits spread out by about as much as their average. \Meas\ That check was pre-registered, and
it passed at every size in the main run. \MeasX\ Checked afterwards, the whole pattern of first exits
fits that picture too.

\Meas\ One more thing the move-by-move count revealed: the first swap out does not always lead
anywhere. Think of that first swap as a door opening. About a third of the time the door closes again,
and the tube goes back to being a perfect tube. Only 56 to 68 percent of first exits went on to a real
change, with no trend in $\lambda$. The formula predicts when the door opens, not the departure.

\sub{Two clocks}
\Exact\ The three tickets a sweep come from a cancellation, and the cancellation belongs to the clock.
Every proposal is picked from the whole network, so in a tube twice as long any one neighborhood is
offered its swaps half as often per sweep, while there are twice as many neighborhoods. A clock can
instead give every neighborhood the same chances whatever the size of the network. We call it the
\textbf{fair clock}: one fair sweep gives each neighborhood the chances it gets in one sweep of a
96-dot network, which takes $N/96$ sweeps. On the fair clock every 32 dots of tube offer one route-A
exit per fair sweep, so a tube twice as long offers twice as many and waits half as long.

\end{center}

\Ours\ The toy has no time in it, so neither clock is the ``right'' one. The fair clock is the natural
one for comparing sizes, and the next paper in the series uses it. It also carries a caution: on the
fair clock, the longer a stuck tube, the sooner it goes, so ``stable for now'' describes the sizes that
were run.

\whyhere{If the measured wait had disagreed with the counted lottery, something larger than a single
swap would be holding the tube shut, and 12 would have been the wrong number. It agrees. The rim is the
single swap, and the lottery is the whole explanation of the first exit.}

\takeaway{Count the exits a sweep offers, multiply by the chance each is taken, and you predict the
mean wait with nothing fitted. The tube keeps that appointment over a ninety-fold range of waits, and
its waiting is memoryless, like rolling for a six. But about a third of the time the door closes
again: an opened door is not a departure.}

\chapterpage{6 \enspace THE CHANGE}{What takes shape on the other side}

{\footnotesize\color{ink!55}\textbf{The experiment:} the arrangement \textbullet\ the cost \textbullet\ the wait \textbullet\ {\color{accent}\textbf{the change}} \textbullet\ the energy \textbullet\ other settings\par}

Chapter~5 was about the first swap out. This chapter follows the change through the tube and asks
what the tube turns into.

\sub{A zipper, or several?}
The natural picture is a zipper: one spot of the tube opens, and the opening runs along the tube the
way a zipper runs along a jacket. A moving boundary like the zipper's slider, with opened tube behind
it and curled tube ahead, is called a \textbf{front}. \Exact\ The instrument is the direction count
$d$ of Chapter~3: dots still in the tube read 1, dots on a flat sheet read 2, and a disordered dot
reads something else. A progress meter runs from 0 (all tube) to 1 (all flat), and the program
photographs the whole network when the meter first reads a quarter, a half and three-quarters. These
are stages of the change, fixed in advance, and nothing is special about them.

\Meas\ At $\lambda = 1.25$ and $g=1.5$, thirty tubes at each of four sizes were run twice, with three
predictions written down first: a memoryless wait; at least 80 percent of dots in one of the two
signatures at the half mark; and at least 70 percent of the flat-like dots in one connected region, on
average. All three held at every size in both runs. At every stage photographed, 98.5 to 99.8 percent
of dots read as tube or sheet, with almost nothing in between.

\sub{What the tube turns into}
\Exact\ A count of $d=2$ at a dot means only that the four-step curl is gone there: two buildings can
have the same number of rooms and different floor plans. So the finished networks were rebuilt from
their saved wiring. \MeasX\ The ones that finished at zero energy, with nothing left over, are rings
again: one surface, flat at every dot, six steps around where the tube was four, and as long as the
tube was. That held for all 120 read at $\lambda = 1.05$, and for 3,446 of the 3,521 zero-energy
finishes of the map in Chapter~8 (the others came apart into separate pieces, or closed up with a
twist).

around the whole doughnut. The shortest walk back to the start is now six steps: four around and two

\sub{The energy comes out exactly}
\Meas\ When a tube finishes at zero energy, the energy given off is exactly one unit per dot
at $\lambda = 1.25$: the arithmetic of Chapter~3, now measured. In about 26 runs of
every 30 the release was within one percent of that. The rest kept small defects, 8 to 45 units' worth read from
their wiring, and held back that much. The ideal gap is exact; how much of it comes out depends on
where the network stops.

\whyhere{The hypothesis pictures a seed and a spreading front. The photographs test what a toy can
check: whether the two arrangements sit side by side with almost nothing between, and whether one
connected region dominates. They do not by themselves establish a single front or a wide space.}

\takeaway{Halfway through, about 99 dots in 100 read as tube or sheet, and one connected region usually
dominates: sharp in the sense written down beforehand, though not proof of a single zipper. The clean
finish is a ring six around where the tube was four. Its seam has slipped two dots, its rings have
joined into spirals, and only that first slip is paid for. A finish that keeps a scrap can hold more
than one width.}

\chapterpage{7 \enspace CLOSE THE LID}{Where does the energy go when it has nowhere to go?}

{\footnotesize\color{ink!55}\textbf{The experiment:} the arrangement \textbullet\ the cost \textbullet\ the wait \textbullet\ the change \textbullet\ {\color{accent}\textbf{the energy}} \textbullet\ other settings\par}

Apart from the budget test of Chapter~4, every run so far sat in the bath, and the bath carried the
released energy away as fast as it appeared. If a change like this made all of space, there was no
outside for its energy to go to. So seal the system again, as in Chapter~4, and watch what the burp
does to its own surroundings.

\Exact\ The sealed system of Chapter~4 had one store. Here there are $C$ stores beside the network,
each holding a non-negative number of units. Every proposed swap picks one store at random: an uphill
swap is refused unless that store can pay; a downhill swap deposits its release there. Nothing enters
or leaves, so network plus stores is constant to the unit, and it was checked to be constant in every
run.

Return to our 96-dot tube, holding 96 units. Put 12 more in a store and seal the lid. The account
reads 108. Suppose the change runs and leaves one 14-unit scrap in the sheet, as it often does in a
cold box. The network has gone from 96 to 14; the stores have gone from 12 to 94.

\sub{The surroundings can spoil the ending}
Here is the twist. The released energy is now in the stores, and the stores pay for uphill swaps.
Energy that has nowhere to go can buy swaps that break the very squares the change has just made.
How much damage depends on how many stores there are. \Pub\ Many stores share the release thinly, like
a large cold room; few stores concentrate it, like a small hot one.

\Meas\ Pre-registered: tubes of 64, 96 and 192 dots, seven choices of $C$ from a single store to two per
dot, twenty runs at each setting, 420 runs. The outcomes were classified by rules fixed beforehand:
sheet-like if at least 90 percent of dots read $d=2$ with almost no melted dots; melted if 15 percent
or more read as disorder; stalled if both orders sat side by side with nothing melting; in between
otherwise.

\Meas\ A bonfire with a threshold, as the pre-registered verdict put it. Where the heat has room, the
change feeds itself to completion; where it does not, the heat damages what the change made
(``melted'' in this classification is a label: at least 15 percent of the dots have lost their neat two
directions). The switch-over moves in step with size, as a release of one unit per dot shared among the
stores predicts. \Ours\ A rough energy budget puts it near one store per three dots; the measured
switch-over sits between one per four and one per two.

\Meas\ No run ended stalled, with tube and sheet side by side and nothing melting, in 420 runs of
30,000 sweeps. That is an observation about these runs. It does not show that such a standoff is
impossible, which would take evidence these sealed runs do not supply.

\sub{What ``melted'' means here}
\Exact\ A sealed box holds only the energy it started with: at $\lambda=1.25$, one unit per dot,
plus the push. A missing square costs 16 units. So the burp's own heat can break at most about one
square in sixteen, however few stores there are. \MeasX\ Every one of the 154 networks classed as
melted still had 94 to 100 percent of its squares. That is damage: warm
fabric with flaws in it. It is not the random tangle the published model starts from, which has
about an eighth as many squares as flat fabric. The burp cannot melt its own product.

\Meas\ A later registered test asked whether heat kept in the stores makes the new ring wider. It
does not. With plenty of room the rings ended six around; with little room they ended as
patchworks, six around in one stretch and eight or more in the next, with flaws frozen in where the
stretches meet (the joints of the next paper). None of 720 ended as one clean ring wider than six.

\sub{What a cold box leaves}
\Meas\ Eighty fresh runs in the coldest box, two stores per dot, at 64, 96, 192 and 288 dots. Almost
every finished ring kept one scrap of the old arrangement: four dots in a loop that still closes a
square, holding 14 units. The count was about one per box whatever the size of the tube (0.90, 0.85,
1.05 and 0.95 per finished ring). The prediction written down beforehand was that it would grow with
the space, and it did not. A second test found the scrap anywhere along the tube, not where the change
began or met itself. What scraps are, how many kinds there are, and which of them last is the next
paper's subject.

\whyhere{What the burp turns into, and whether the energy books balance across the change, only have
meaning in a sealed system. In a bath the burp leaves and nothing can be asked of it. The sealed box is
where the balance becomes a measurement.}

\takeaway{Sealed, the release has to go somewhere, and where it goes decides the ending: into enough
room and the sheet completes, into too little and the heat damages the new space without melting it or
widening it. The books balance to the unit in every run. Nothing stalled half and half. A cold box
leaves about one 14-unit scrap, whatever the size of the tube.}

\sub{What the map shows}
\Meas\ Three things.
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
\item \textbf{Where the tube is stuck.} At every setting from 1.05 to 1.35, and at none from 1.40 up.
Wherever it is stuck the change keeps the character of Chapter~6: 95 to 100 percent of dots in one
signature or the other at the half mark, and one connected region holding most of the flat-like dots.
\item \textbf{When it leaves.} The mean wait follows the counted lottery of Chapter~5 over a
hundred-fold range of waiting times, with nothing fitted.
\item \textbf{How clean the ending is.} Cleanest near the published setting, and messier as the knob
turns. At 1.05 every one of 120 tubes ended as a perfect flat torus, at 1.25 about 88 percent did, at
1.35 fewer than half, and beyond the edge the change starts in several places at once in most runs and
ends mostly on a sheet with defects.
\end{itemize}

\Ours\ There is a trade here. A larger $\lambda$ means a larger burp per dot but a less patient X and a
messier birth; a smaller $\lambda$ means a cleaner change that releases less. The ideal release is
$4(\lambda-1)$ per dot, reached only when the change ends at zero energy, and it vanishes at the
published setting, so this family of rules has no setting with a large release and a quiet, clean X
at once.

\sub{Does it hold up?}
\Meas\ The map was scored against rules fixed before it ran, and it passes: in all 24 cells from
$\lambda=1.05$ to 1.30 and 64 to 192 dots, the waits spread as a memoryless wait should, the dots sit in
one signature or the other, and one connected patch dominates. A larger size, 288 dots, was predicted
before it was read, and all six settings landed inside their predicted ranges. At 1.35 the
connected-patch test fails at 192 and 288 dots, so the stretch where everything holds is 1.05 to 1.30.

Two things keep that pass in proportion. It took four tries: three earlier maps each failed one of
their rules and stay inconclusive, for reasons that mostly traced to the measuring and not to the tube, and
the technical paper~\cite{tech} has that story. And the single patch weakens toward the edge: at 288
dots and $\lambda=1.30$ the largest patch holds 75 percent of the flat-like dots on average, against a
cutoff of 70, and only 65 of 120 single runs clear it.

\whyhere{The project's first success condition is no cherry-picking: a family of rules may be explored,
but only along knobs fixed in advance, and every setting run is published. The map is that rule
applied, and it also locates where the stand-in for X stops being stable for now.}

\takeaway{The tube is stuck, and the change keeps its character, from 1.05 to 1.35, and the map passes
its registered tests from 1.05 to 1.30, including a size predicted in advance. A bigger release comes
with a messier change. None of this is a test of a thermodynamic first-order transition, and the
finished tori are still narrow.}

\sub{The knob $\lambda$: 1.05 to 1.45}
Below 1.05 the burp gets smaller and cleaner, not longer-awaited. \Exact\ The wait levels off as the
knob approaches 1 (the counted lottery gives about 8,300 sweeps at 1.05 and about 14,000 at 1.001),
while the release shrinks to nothing at exactly 1, where curled and flat tie. Below 1 the ladder
flips. \Meas\ Above the range, at 1.5,
the tube falls apart within 50 sweeps in every run. \Exact\ At 1.6 route B costs nothing, and at 18
dots no stuck state above flat exists; by a setting of 5 to 10 the toy behaves as one with a third
square simply forbidden. Nothing curled is stable for now out there.

\sub{The warmth $g$: 1.4 to 2.5, with the maps at 1.5}
\Exact\ Colder, the wait grows like $e^{12/g}$: about 50,000 sweeps at $g=1$ and nearly three million
at $g = 0.75$. \MeasX\ At $g=1$, two of four tubes were still tubes after 30,000 sweeps, which is why
the range stops there. Hotter, the tube opens almost
at once and from many places, and near $g \approx 3.5$ the flat sheet itself melts into the random
tangle; the reservoir estimate of Chapter~7 used that melting point. How it moves with the size of
the sheet is taken up in a later paper, and until then every warm result carries a size caveat.

\sub{The size $N$: 48 to 288 dots}
The upper limit was computing time, spent on repeats rather than size.
\Exact\ The exit count was certified at 48 through 288; a control at 32 dots breaks the pattern, and
16 dots is the knot. \Meas\ Counted in sweeps, the front's speed fell roughly as the size to the power
$-0.7$. \Exact\ Counted on the fair clock of Chapter~5 the same numbers give a speed that does not fall
with size, and if anything rises a little, as the size to the power $0.3$. Like the wait, the speed
compares across sizes only once a clock is chosen. The technical paper claims nothing
beyond 288; networks into the thousands of dots are the subject of a later paper on the scrap.

\sub{The push and the stores}
\Exact\ With a push below 12 nothing can leave by single swaps at the certified sizes, and a tube
with nothing to spare waits for ever. \MeasX\ Pushes of 12 to 160 units at 64 dots all left, and the
hotter the box the more disordered the sheet it ended with, as predicted from the toy's equilibrium
curve before the run: 0.97 squares per dot measured against 0.95 predicted at the smallest push, 0.81
against 0.81 at the largest. Larger pushes were not run. \Meas\ With a single store every tube ends
damaged; two stores per dot leave clean sheets with one scrap. Colder boxes than that were not tried.

\takeaway{The box is small and its edges are known. Past most of them lies either an exact statement
(nothing below 12 can leave; nothing is below flat), a later measurement with its own paper, or a
plain ``unknown.'' The one edge the toy cannot push on its own is the clock.}

\sub{What the toy shows, and where it stops}
\Ours\ The toy gives the larger hypothesis a concrete first question: can a prepared curled
arrangement wait, escape and release an amount of energy that can be calculated? Under the stated
rules, it can. But its ending limits the comparison with the picture that opens this paper: the runs
stop at the nearest flat arrangement, the narrow one, and their own trapped heat does not widen it.
Two cautions go with the result. The fixed cost is the cost of opening the door, not of committing to
the change. And an energy gap between two ideal arrangements is not a test of a thermodynamic
first-order transition.

\Claim\ The author's wider proposal is that an organized arrangement came before space. This paper is a
controlled test of part of that proposal in a toy, not a test against observations of the universe.
What it hands to the next model: the local energy can be priced exactly, the first exit can be
predicted, and flatness dot by dot is not enough to make a shape wide. What did not go the way it
might have: the remainder did not grow, a single front was not established, and three registered maps
stayed inconclusive before a fourth passed.

\sub{What comes next}
The question that began the work survives its first test. Can an organized arrangement give way
sharply, release energy and leave a remainder? In this small laboratory it can, and the first step
can be priced to the unit. Two things were left standing at the edge of what this paper measured,
and each has work of its own coming, with its predictions written down first.

\textbf{The scrap.} A scrap is the only thing in the new space that still carries the old
arrangement, so the next paper, \emph{What the change leaves behind}, asks what it takes to keep one.
\Meas\ One result: the scrap counted here is not permanent. Cool the new sheet slowly enough and it
heals away, while another kind of leftover survives every cooling we could run.

\textbf{Three directions.} The tube has one long direction, and space has three, so the paper after
that, \emph{How curled directions open}, runs the same rules with six links per dot. \Meas\ One
result: escape from the four-link tube does not settle the question for more directions. Later runs
find a separate barrier for each curled direction. Some end in wider shapes, but no fixed budget has
been shown to open every direction.
