# A phase-changing reality: the programme, and where each piece stands

*Draft for the owner's review, 24 September 2026 (revised the same afternoon). Not for publication until she has read it. Every claim says
whose it is: **the author's** (Emily Smith's hypothesis), **measured** (a pre-registered test in the public
repository, with its verdict), **exact** (arithmetic or complete enumeration), or **ours** (the analysis
drafted with Claude, unreviewed by a physicist). Nothing here has yet been read by a physicist (the author's
own bar, S5, is not met).*

---

## The claim, in the author's words

Our spacetime is one settled arrangement of something deeper, X: a specific, relatively stable arrangement
with its own dimensions, curled where ours are open. X was stable for now, not for ever. A push started the
change in one small part of X; it spread as a front, X opened into space, and the change came out as a burp: a fixed
release of energy that became the Big Bang's matter and radiation. What did not convert remained as a scrap:
dark matter. Energy was conserved across the change. The rules of the two phases are their constants and do
not change from one generation to the next. Inside the space that forms, black holes are regions that re-curl
towards X; one that grows large enough gets the activation energy and burps a new space: the same burp, the
same scrap. Every possible arrangement of what a Big Bang produces therefore gets to exist. The quantum world
is the set of undetectably different versions of an arrangement, all standing at once, until an interaction
makes them distinguishable.

## Where each piece stands

The tests use one published model of emergent geometry (Trugenberger's combinatorial quantum gravity) as a
toy: space is a network, X's stand-in is a torus with one direction curled (spherical cows being unavailable),
and the model's local-term coefficient λ is a knob. Only λ = 1 is his model; the torus results are at λ > 1,
where the energy is no longer a curvature, so they are about a neighbour of his model, and are never called
combinatorial quantum gravity. Anything about gravity is tested in three dimensions only: in two, even
Einstein's gravity gives no pull between masses at rest (general knowledge, to verify), so no number measured
in 2D is carried into a claim about gravity. **A toy model can show that a mechanism is possible or
impossible inside a class of models; on its own it cannot show that reality works this way.** That is the
author's claim, and the model is how she tests its pieces.

| **1. A stable-for-now arrangement opens sharply into space** | **Measured** (T7, T9, T10, T11; paper 1) | At λ = 1.25 the curled torus decompactifies (its curled direction opens): it waits, starts at one place, runs as one front with the two orders side by side, releases exactly ε = 4(λ − 1) per point, and leaves about one small relic at every size tried. The wall it must climb is one move costing exactly 12, at every size. The wall's height is a fact about the energy, like the height of a hill; the waiting and the front are how this simulation walks over it, one local swap at a time, and another way of walking would change them. In the author's view the points are interchangeable (ours, exact; not yet run): that changes no energy, so the wall, the burp and the relic stay as they are, but it makes the torus stickier. A perfectly symmetric arrangement looks the same from every point, one move breaks that, and counting look-alike arrangements as one then favours staying put, the more so the bigger the space. | A slide instead of a switch; a push that grows with size. Neither happened. |
| **2. It is not a fluke of one setting** | **Measured** (T8; inconclusive by the letter, for a stated reason) | From λ = 1.05 to 1.35 the change is stuck for now, runs as one front with the two orders side by side, and releases exactly the predicted burp at every size; it is cleanest near λ = 1 and leaves more defects as λ grows; above 1.35 the curled torus is not stuck. Waiting times follow a formula with nothing fitted over two decades; counted move by move, the first exit arrives when the formula says, and about a third of exits fall back before the front takes hold, at every λ (T22). The pre-registered verdict is inconclusive because one criterion (memoryless waits) was set too tight for thirty decays per setting. Checked afterwards on the whole spread of waits (not pre-registered), they are memoryless, like a radioactive nucleus: however long the torus has waited, its chance of going in the next moment is the same, once a quirk in how the waits were clocked is allowed for. One oddity is unexplained: at λ = 1.05 a few waits are far longer than that picture expects. | A floor below which the change stops being sharp: none found down to 1.05. |
| **3. The rule: curvature plus a curling cost** | **Exact** (the arithmetic); **the author's** (what it means) | The author's rule is the published energy, the total curvature, unchanged, plus one term: every link curled tight costs 4(λ − 1) extra (VISION Update 23). The burp per point is exactly that cost, ε = 4(λ − 1): zero at λ = 1, the published model, where curling is free and nothing is released; below 1 the change runs the wrong way. The author's value is about 1.25 in 2D; in 3D her fully curled X is stuck only for 1 < λ < 1.2 (piece 11). These are different models asking different questions, not rival values for one model. **The author's position:** the curling cost is a fact of X's arrangement (a line in F, perhaps from the three directions being intertwined), a constant of our loop, still present in spacetime, where it is what a black hole pays to re-curl space; its value is whatever gives the biggest loop with the longest cold period (piece 10; series paper 3). | Open: whether a rule that is curvature plus a curling cost gives back Einstein's gravity at large scale (claim 2). Ours, unverified: where space is smooth nothing is curled tight and the extra term is zero, so the rule there is curvature alone; the test is in 3D. |
| **4. The new space must have room for the burp** | **Measured** (T9; T18 across λ) | Sealed, the change completes only if the surroundings can absorb the burp; otherwise the new space melts. The room needed grows with the size of the space, and faster than the burp as λ rises (12, 36, 72 stores at λ = 1.10, 1.25, 1.40): a bigger burp is a harder birth. | A condition the author's theory must meet: in her theory melting is not real, so the burp must always have somewhere to go. |
| **5. The scrap: fixed size, and more seeds make more** | **Measured** (T10, T17) | About one relic (the scrap) of fixed size per tube at every size tried, from one seed; with 1, 2, 4, 8 seeds, 1.10, 2.15, 2.50, 3.40 relics: more seeds, more scrap, but fewer than one each (pre-registered verdict: BETWEEN). At fixed temperature, near λ = 1 the change leaves no scrap at all. | A scrap that grows with the space, or more seeds leaving no more scrap. Neither happened. The two guesses at the exact count both missed: the author's one per seed held up to two seeds and fell short beyond; ours, one per tube, was wrong from two seeds. The truth lies between. |
| **6. The scrap lasts** | **First version did not hold; in progress** (T19) | Held at one fixed temperature, every relic heals away in the end: within 500 to 13,000 sweeps at coupling 1.25 and 4,000 to 80,500 at 1.0. In a very cold sealed box (T10) it lasted the whole run. The colder, the longer it lasts. | Next: reality cools as it expands, so the right test is a race. Does the scrap freeze in before it heals? (Cosmology calls this freeze-out; ours, from general knowledge.) Cool the box on a schedule after the burp and count survivors. It fails if the scrap heals at every cooling speed. |
| **7. Gravity from counting what we cannot see** (the author's name for her account: **curve-first gravity**) | **First version did not hold; in progress** (O22, O32) | Tested so far only with space frozen. With the wiring held fixed, two relics exert no pull on each other through energy (exact), and with points interchangeable, counting does not pull them together either (it slightly prefers them exactly opposite). Frozen space cannot bend, so nothing can carry a pull; in Einstein's gravity too, masses pull through the bending of the space between them (ours). **The author's position (24 September): it is not a wish.** Gravity is a mathematical result of some local degrees of freedom being interchangeable and unobserved from our large-scale view of reality. Cost and count can both act, as the two halves of one free energy: refolding costs energy, counting favours it, and it happens where enough energy is packed in to pay. In her framing, curved space moves matter: space curves using energy that matter provides, and the three space directions curl together, never one alone. Published relatives of gravity from counting: Jacobson (1995) and Verlinde (2011), general knowledge, not read by us. **Curve-first gravity** (the author's term, 24 September): space has its own drive to curve, and matter supplies the energy that starts the curve rather than causing it; gravity belongs to the spacetime phase, not to X's rules, and the barrier X sits behind is in F, not in gravitational energy. It differs from general relativity in the cause, and must agree with it wherever general relativity has been measured (claim 2). | Next, in her terms: does curved space move matter? The real case, with space free to bend, relics free to move, and points interchangeable, measuring the average pull at each separation (the standard name is the potential of mean force). In the model that needs a sheet warm enough to rearrange but not so warm that it melts (a cold sheet accepts no moves at all), so the first job is to find that window. A caution (general knowledge, not read by us, to verify): the model's space is two-dimensional, and in two space dimensions even Einstein's gravity gives no pull between masses at rest, so this model may be the wrong place to see a pull even if the idea is right. **Decided 24 September: the pull is tested in three dimensions (piece 11), never read from 2D.** What would earn the name gravity: a pull that attracts, grows with the energy present, is the same for every kind of relic, and in 3D weakens with distance roughly as 1/r². |
| **8. Black holes as re-curled regions** | **First version did not hold; in progress** (O20, T21) | Energy spread evenly through a sealed sheet melts it rather than folding it, with named points and, at 64 points where complete folds exist, with interchangeable points too; at 36 points, a fragile lean towards folding. **The author's position (24 September):** the melt is not a real phase with any stability, so melting briefly is fine as long as the region folds soon after. The melt can be the barrier between a new space and the black hole that birthed it, closing until the next 12 is available. | Next: a black hole is not energy spread evenly but energy packed into one place. The untested case is a local push into cold surroundings (the local-spark protocol, still to be built). It fails if a local push also melts rather than folds. Second test, in the author's terms: in the model a melt lasts as long as its energy stays, so let the energy leak away at several speeds and see whether the melt folds before it flattens. It fails if every melt ends flat. |
| **9. Quantum behaviour from counting versions** | **First version did not hold; in progress** (T15 rungs 0b, 1, 3a) | **The author's framing (24 September): counting was a probe, not the expected mechanism.** It was tried because its mathematics was already in hand (the model's own renaming counts), not because she expected it to work. Counting alone can never beat Bell's inequality, for any fixed arrangement (argument, rung 3a); the model's small loop is not a resonator inside the sheet (rung 0b). The next rung passed: with interchangeable points, time spent in each arrangement equals its share of the count (rung 1, validation). Why counting falls short (ours): a count gives every version a positive weight, and positive weights only add, so two versions can never cancel. Quantum mechanics needs cancelling (interference), which is what a phase supplies. Smolin's real-ensemble quantum mechanics, the closest published relative, has it: his versions are real copies that affect one another, and they carry a phase. | **The author's position (24 September): versions do not affect one another; they are not different in the first place.** Next: a rule under which versions can cancel one another; the author expects it to come from the loop (piece 10) and has not yet chosen one. The most concrete candidate we know of (ours; general knowledge, not read by us) is the exchange phase: swapping two identical things gives back the same state up to a sign, −1 for fermions (Pauli exclusion is two swapped versions cancelling), and in two dimensions any phase at all (anyons), because a swap there traces a loop that cannot be undone. The phase belongs to the swap, not to either version. Whether this gets past rung 3a's argument is not known. It fails if the rule cannot reproduce the Bell violations that experiments measure. |
| **10. The cycle: new spaces from black holes, every arrangement existing** (the author calls it a strange loop, after Hofstadter) | **The author's; untestable in the model** | The network model used here (Trugenberger's, not Smolin's) has no black holes and no expansion. It can map the ingredients (how stuck X is, the push, the burp, the room, the scrap), not count offspring. **The author's position (24 September): versions are not copies**, neither the renamings of one arrangement nor identical arrangements in other spacetimes. So Smolin's route to quantum behaviour is not hers, and she expects the missing rule of piece 9 to come from the loop itself: something about a strange loop that his picture does not use. | Smolin's argument: if a large object had copies, it would behave quantum-mechanically. The author's answer is that other spacetimes' arrangements do not count as copies. Ours: the counting already makes large objects classical without copies, since a complicated arrangement has exactly one version (rung 0: a melt, and a sheet with three different defects, each have one). |
| **11. The three space directions curl together** (three dimensions, six links per point) | **Exact so far; the reproduction gate is set up, not run** (O41; Gate C) | The author's rule: the three directions are intertwined, curl together and open together, so one activation releases the whole burp. In 2D that rule cannot be tested (a gas of fully curled pieces is not stuck at any λ > 1, O40). In 3D (exact, O41): the fully curled state, the 6-cube, is stuck for now only for 1 < λ < 1.2, and each curled direction holds a third of the burp. Like the 2D torus, this window lies off λ = 1, so results there are about a neighbour of the published model. | First, reproduce the published 3D curve ([T22] Fig. 3, N = 500, λ = 1) before any 3D result is read; the acceptance criterion is the author's to decide. Then: does one push open all three directions at once, and does a curved region pull a relic towards it? It fails if the directions open one at a time, or if no pull appears. |

## What is new here, as far as we have found

We have not found published work that varies the coefficient of the model's local term, or that studies one
ordered arrangement of it turning into another (literature check of 24 September, recorded in the repository).
That is the ground of paper 1. The one place where the author's question meets the published model at its own
value of λ is the allotrope (piece 12).

## What is claimed, and by whom

The author claims that reality works this way. The claim at the top is hers, stated to be argued with. The
model's results are narrower: in the model, piece 1 is established, and the others are measured, in progress
or untested, as marked. No physicist has checked any of it yet.
