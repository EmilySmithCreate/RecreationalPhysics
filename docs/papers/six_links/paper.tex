\title{How curled directions open, three and four: exact walls and pre-registered opening tests in a graph toy
with six and eight links per vertex}

\author{Emily Smith}
\email{emilysmithcreate@gmail.com}
\affiliation{Independent researcher, Charlottesville, Virginia, USA}

\date{DRAFT, 11 October 2026}

\begin{abstract}
We ask how a torus with several curled directions opens, in a graph toy with six and eight links per vertex. The toy
is the energy of combinatorial quantum gravity (CQG) with the coefficient $\lambda$ of its local term raised above
1, later with a tie between directions; only $\lambda = 1$ is CQG. Exact: each curled direction costs
$4(\lambda - 1)$ per vertex; each curled state has its own cheapest move out, its wall; the walls rise as directions
open, so the energy ties no direction to another; by a published theorem whose proof we have not read, a fully
curled start larger than one hypercube is a gas of pieces. Measured, in thirteen pre-registered tests: the first
move's price is fixed with size; in no six- or eight-link setting did most runs open every curled direction into one
connected region, from one push or in a warm bath. A gas opens cube by cube; a connected torus usually opens one
direction or none; full openings were minorities, beside damage, and by shape about a third of them are the start
glued together differently. The author's predictions that one push or a warm bath opens every direction failed, as
did several of ours. She then chose a tie that releases everything at a vertex's last opening. Under it, in a warm
bath, her slab stayed shut or broke, and the untied control showed no difference. The published six-link curve is
not reproduced, so every result concerns our family only.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Combinatorial quantum gravity (CQG) \cite{KTB19,T25} gives a regular graph an energy built from its squares
(4-cycles). A companion paper \cite{paper1} raised the coefficient $\lambda$ of that energy's local term above 1 and
studied a four-link torus with one direction curled. It found activated escape followed by rapid changes in
square-count signatures, often with a dominant connected converted region; a unique moving front and macroscopic
opening of both periods were not established. Only $\lambda = 1$ is CQG. Everything here is at $\lambda > 1$, some of
it with an added tie between directions. We call it our family, or the toy: next to CQG and not in it.

\emph{The hypothesis's claim (the author's \cite{repo}).} The curled arrangement that precedes space, which she
calls X, has three or four directions curled together; they open together; and one activation releases everything.
With four links the claim cannot be posed: a fully curled state, a gas of 4-cubes, has no wall at any $\lambda > 1$
and falls apart at once (O40). With six and eight links it can. This paper poses it twice: exactly, by pricing the
single moves out of every rung of the ladder of curled states; and by runs, each registered before it ran, with the
author's prediction and ours recorded before any result was read.

Statements are of four kinds and are labeled: \emph{exact} (arithmetic or complete enumeration); \emph{measured}
(a pre-registered run, with its registered verdict in capitals and whose prediction held or failed);
\emph{interpretation} (ours, unverified); and \emph{the hypothesis's claim} (the author's). ``Ours'' marks
predictions and readings drafted with an AI assistant. Nothing here has been reviewed by a physicist. Tags such as
T39 and O81 name entries of the project's pre-registration and assumptions files \cite{repo}.

Three caveats govern everything below. First, the published six-link curve of CQG \cite{T22} is not reproduced by
our kernel (Sec.~\ref{sec:gate}), and the eight-link kernel is checked against no published curve: the published
simulations we rely on are for two and three directions \cite{KTB19,T22}. The author decided to proceed with that
gate open. Every six- and eight-link statement is therefore about our family and its kernel, not about the published
model. The kernel is the four-link code with the number of links read from the array. At four links it makes the
companion paper's chain draw for draw, and its energy and moves are checked by brute force against an independent
implementation; it is validated at four and six links only. Second, the toy has no time. A direction of a torus is a
direction, and which one would be time the toy cannot say. Third, ``curled'' and ``open'' below are square-count
signatures (Sec.~\ref{sec:exact}), not measured geometry.

Published continuum work treats a change in the number of large dimensions as a decay by tunneling or nucleation
\cite{GM04,CJR09,BSV10}; there the compact directions change together by construction (a single volume modulus, or a
sphere). A fourth paper considers one compact direction opening from a parent with one fewer large dimension
\cite{GHR10}. These are benchmarks for what a toy of this kind would have to reproduce, not support for any result
here. Their texts were searched by an AI assistant, and no quotation has been checked against the published versions.

\section{Definitions and exact results}
\label{sec:exact}

\subsection{The energy, the ladder and the counts}

With $D$ directions a vertex has $2D$ links. Graphs are $2D$-regular and bipartite, and no two vertices share more
than two neighbors (the hard-core rule, as we read it). With $S$ squares, $S_e$ squares on link $e$ and
$X = \sum_e (S_e - (2D - 2))_+$ surplus squares,
\begin{equation}
  H = 16\Big(\tfrac{D(D-1)}{2} N - S\Big) + 4\lambda X ,
\end{equation}
derived by us from the published definitions as the four-link energy was (Q21), and not validated against a
published curve.

\emph{Exact} (O41, O50). The flat $D$-torus, every side at least 6, has $H = 0$. A side of length 4 is a
\emph{curled} direction: it puts one extra square on every link along it and costs $a \equiv 4(\lambda - 1)$ per
vertex. The cost is additive, so a torus with $k$ curled directions, the $k$th \emph{rung} of the ladder, lies $ka$
per vertex above flat. The six-link torus with every side 4 is the 6-cube (64 vertices); its eight-link
counterpart is the 8-cube (256).

The statistic $d(v)$ counts the pairs of links at $v$ that close no square. On the exact tori it equals the number
of directions that are not curled: $D$ on the flat torus, one less per curled direction. Where squares are missing it
exceeds $D$. We call a vertex with $d = D$ \emph{open} and one with $d > D$ \emph{damaged}, and we say a direction
\emph{opens} at a vertex where $d$ rises by one. An \emph{open region} is a connected set of open vertices at one
reading. These are counts of squares and of connectivity. On a general graph they do not certify a dimension, a
product-torus geometry, a macroscopic period or a moving front \cite{repo}.

\subsection{The walls}

The chain's move is a \emph{switch}: it replaces two links by two others and keeps every vertex's number of links. A
\emph{wall} is the cost, $-16\,\Delta S + 4\lambda\,\Delta X$, of the cheapest single switch out of an exact torus. A
state is \emph{stuck for now} where its wall is positive, which is a property of the energy alone. \emph{Exact}
(O49, O50): every switch out of each curled six-link torus was listed and priced; with eight links, and for flat
six-link space, only switches with nearby partners were listed, a shortcut checked against the full list on the
$4 \times 4 \times 18$ torus. Table~\ref{tab:walls} gives the cheapest kind at $\lambda = 1.25$. Four things follow,
all exact.

(i) The walls rise as directions open. With six links they are $96 - 80\lambda$, $96 - 64\lambda$ and
$96 - 48\lambda$ out of the rungs with three, two and one directions curled; with eight, $160 - 128\lambda$,
$160 - 112\lambda$, $160 - 96\lambda$ and $160 - 64\lambda$. Each exceeds the one before by $16\lambda$ or
$32\lambda$, so the doubling 20, 40, 80 at eight links holds at $\lambda = 1.25$ only (O55). The energy ties no
direction to another: each has its own wall, and the later walls are the higher.

(ii) The cheapest move out of flat space costs 32, 64 and 128 with four, six and eight links, at every $\lambda$
(O50, O51). Its ratio to the last rung's wall at $\lambda = 1.25$ is $32/12 = 2.7$, $64/36 = 1.8$ and
$128/80 = 1.6$: more directions leave less room between paying the last wall and breaking flat space (O65).

(iii) The fully curled gas is stuck for now only for $1 < \lambda < 1.2$ with six links and $1 < \lambda < 1.25$ with
eight (O41, O50).

(iv) At larger $\lambda$ another kind of move can undercut the one in Table~\ref{tab:walls}. On the two-curled
six-link torus it is $128 - 88\lambda$ (4.8 at $\lambda = 1.40$), so that torus is stuck for $1 < \lambda < 1.45$
(O100); on the three-curled eight-link torus it is $192 - 136\lambda$ (1.6 at 1.40; T48). A short open side also
changes the wall: the $4 \times 4 \times 6$ torus admits a move at $96 - 80\lambda$ that a long open side does not
(O49).

Walls do not settle whether a bath warm enough to pay a later wall leaves flat space intact. In an exploratory scan
at $\lambda = 1.10$ and 1.25 the flat $6 \times 6 \times 6$ torus keeps all 12 squares per vertex up to $g = 6$ and
is melted at $g = 10$ (5.1 squares per vertex), with no coupling between at which it rearranges and stays ordered
(O51).

\subsection{A fully curled start is a gas}

\emph{Exact, applying a published theorem \cite{Mul79}; its statement was checked in the abstract and in later
citations, and its proof has not been read by us (to verify)} (O85). A vertex with every direction curled ($d = 0$)
has every two of its links on a square, so any two vertices with a common neighbor have a second one; the hard-core
rule allows no third. An arrangement curled everywhere is therefore a $(0,2)$-graph: any two vertices have two
common neighbors or none. A connected $(0,2)$-graph of degree $k$ has at most $2^k$ vertices, with equality only for
the $k$-cube. With $2D$ links that is $4^D$: 64 at six links and 256 at eight. So a fully curled start larger than
one hypercube is a gas of separate pieces. The gas starts below are not a choice of ours.

\subsection{Ties}

A \emph{tie} adds $T_f = \sum_v f(d(v))$ to $H$, with $f(0) = f(D) = 0$ and $f = 0$ at damaged vertices. It is a rule
put in by hand, at the author's decision \cite{repo}, and the toy with it is our family. \emph{Exact}, six links
(O70): the gaps between rungs become $a - f(1)$, $a + f(1) - f(2)$ and $a + f(2)$ per vertex in order of opening;
their sum stays $3a$.

Two forms matter below. The \emph{follow} form is $f(d) = \kappa(D - d)$ for $1 \le d \le D$. At $\lambda = 1.25$
its walls out of the gas, the two-curled torus and the one-curled torus are $-4$, 16, 36 at $\kappa = 0$; 20, $-6$,
20 at $\kappa = 1$; 44, $-30$, 4 at $\kappa = 2$; and 56, $-42$, $-4$ at $\kappa = 2.5$; flat space's wall stays 64
(O68, O71). It makes the gas stuck and removes the walls of the partly open rungs, the middle one first. The form
\emph{all at the last} is $f = (0, a, 2a, 0)$ for $d = 0$ to 3, with no constant to fit. Every rung then lies $3a$
per vertex above flat, so nothing is gained until a vertex's last direction opens (O89). Table~\ref{tab:tied} gives
its walls (O106).

Three protocols are used. \emph{Sealed}: a bath of $C$ stores, one holding the push and the rest empty; a proposed
switch draws on one store at random and is refused if that store cannot pay; $H$, the tie where there is one, and the
stores are conserved together. \emph{Local heat}: sealed, with one store per vertex, so released energy stays with
the vertex that releases it. \emph{Warm bath}: the Metropolis chain at a fixed coupling $g$, with no push.
Conservation does not establish equilibrium, and a mean store energy is not a calibrated temperature \cite{repo}.
The sealed dynamics are those of the companion papers \cite{paper1,paper2}.

Table~\ref{tab:runs} lists every test. Each registration defines its per-replica outcome words from the census of
$d$. In brief, at a run's last reading: STUCK or STAYS, most vertices still on the starting rung; ADVANCES, the most
populated rung is above the start; OPENS (T48, T53), at least half the vertices open and the largest open region
holding at least half of all vertices; MELTED or DAMAGED, a quarter or more of the vertices damaged. A cell's
outcome is its majority, and the verdict follows from the cells by the registered rule.

\subsection{The price of the first move is fixed with size}

\emph{Measured} (T32). A single store held the push, at $N = 192$, 288, 384 and 512, ten replicas per push, 20,000
sweeps. No replica left the start at 12, 14 or 15. Every replica left at 16 and above, at every size: FIXED WALL,
sharp. A replica counts as leaving when its squares change at all, so this is the price of the first move, the wall
of Table~\ref{tab:walls}. It is not the price of a conversion, as the next tests show.

\emph{Interpretation (ours, unverified; O103).} On the four-link tube and on the $4 \times 4 \times L$ torus the
cross-section is fixed, 4 and 16 vertices, so the boundary between converted and unconverted parts cannot grow. On a
$4 \times L \times L$ torus it grows with the converted patch. A first-move price that is fixed with size is
therefore not evidence that the barrier to opening a slab is fixed. Section~\ref{sec:tie} returns to this.

\subsection{One push, untied}

\emph{Measured} (T30; O54, O64). At $\lambda = 1.25$, push 16, twelve replicas per cell and 100,000 sweeps, 84
replicas in seven cells. By the final census the first curled direction opened completely, no vertex left at
$d = 1$, in 28. In 21 it went most of the way (15 to 24\% of vertices still at $d = 1$). In 32 it stalled early (36
to 53\% still at $d = 1$; energy 1.86 to 1.95 per vertex against 2.0 at the start). In 3, at the hottest baths, it
went past the one-curled rung; at $C = N/8$ two of twelve replicas reached a defective near-flat state (88 to 90\%
of vertices open), just under the registered bar. At $\lambda = 1.10$ a push of 26 (the wall is 25.6) bought one
move, which neither healed nor grew. No torus cell has a majority that is flat or on the one-curled rung: NEVER
OPENS. The second direction never opened in a cold bath.

The gas of eight 6-cubes at $\lambda = 1.10$ is scored separately (T30-gas). In 21 of 24 replicas the energy fell
from 1.2 to between 0.24 and 0.41 per vertex and about 40\% of vertices ended open, in patches whose largest held 36
to 64 vertices; one cube holds 64. None rested on the level with one direction open for the registered 5,000 sweeps.
Three did not leave. By the letter the verdict is FIRST ONLY, which for the gas means two of three directions open.
It was never one space.

\emph{Measured} (T33; O62). With eight links at $N = 2304$ each start was given a push of 20, its wall (19.2 for
the gas). In every replica the push bought one move, the energy rose by that move's cost (3.000 to 3.009 and 1.600
to 1.608 per vertex), the bath was left empty and nothing changed in 50,000 sweeps. NO CASCADE; no pattern was
tested, because nothing opened.

\emph{Measured} (T40, T39, T41; O81). Larger pushes, 20 to 160, did not start four directions: NEVER STARTS from the
three-curled torus and from a gas of four 8-cubes, with no replica in which most vertices left the starting rung.
Scanning the bath size found no window in which the first release pays the next wall and the space survives: NO
WINDOW in all four rows. With six links most replicas at $\lambda = 1.25$ ended partly converted with no flat
majority at any bath, and at 1.40 the smaller baths read MELTED; with eight links every cell read STUCK. The simplest
three-direction case, one curled direction given its wall (36 and 28.8), stayed curled in all 144 replicas over
100,000 sweeps: NEVER OPENS.

\subsection{One push, with a tie}

\emph{Measured} (T44; O79). Under the follow tie the gas of eight 6-cubes was given its tied wall (20, 44, 56) in
baths of $N/2$ and $2N$ stores. All 48 replicas read STUCK: 97\% of vertices were still at $d = 0$, and the push
made one patch of about 16 vertices. NOT ALL at every $\kappa$.

\emph{Measured} (T45, T46; O79). A tie with two constants was run in four settings on the same gas, and one with
three constants in two settings on a gas of nine 8-cubes, each with two pushes: the first wall alone, and the sum of
the positive walls. Their constants had been fixed by a target ratio of releases that the record has since withdrawn
\cite{repo}; the runs stand as run. NOT ALL in every setting. With six links the setting
$(f(1), f(2)) = (0.528a, -a)$ at $\lambda = 1.25$ rested with two directions open and the last curled, where its
exact wall of 52 put it; the other settings read MELTED or PARTLY OPEN by cell. With eight links all six replicas
given the first wall (17.61) read STUCK, 99\% of vertices still at $d = 0$, and all six given the sum (about 174)
read MELTED: 37 to 38\% of vertices at $d = 2$, 23 to 24\% at $d = 3$, 2.5 to 2.7\% at $d = 4$ and 26\% damaged.

\subsection{What the saved wiring shows}

\emph{A retrospective reading of saved final graphs, not pre-registered; it changes no verdict} (O85; 156 graphs of
T44 to T46). From a gas, no open region
exceeded one starting piece: at most 64 of 512 vertices with six links and at most 4 of 2304 with eight. One open
region in 156 graphs held vertices of two pieces. In one T45 setting at $\lambda = 1.40$, 10 cubes in 24 graphs
opened completely on their own. The damaged vertices are joints and seams, not disordered blobs. In that setting 50
to 81\% of them are ends of links between two starting pieces; in the other settings, and in the connected torus of
T39, 33 to 69\% touch both an open and a curled vertex; nowhere do more than 5\% have only damaged neighbors. So the registered word MELTED in these
runs does not mean a melted space. Each cube opens, or partly opens, inside itself, and the cubes do not join into
one space (O85, O100). The graphs of T30-gas were not among those read; its largest open patches are no larger than
one cube.

Two consequences. The share of open vertices cannot tell one large region from many small ones; the largest open
region can, and it is recorded at every reading from T48 on. And a connected start differs: from the
$4 \times 4 \times 18$ torus (T39, $\lambda = 1.25$, $C = N/16$) the largest open region reached 186 to 250 of 288
vertices in 9 of 12 replicas, with seams beside it.

\subsection{Released energy kept in place, and one cube alone}

\emph{Measured} (T48; O91). With local heat and no tie, the four-link control read ONE SPACE at $\lambda = 1.25$ (15
of 16 replicas open) and ADVANCES ONLY at 1.40 (5 of 16). The two-curled six-link torus read ADVANCES ONLY at 1.25:
13 of 16 replicas rest with one more direction open and the last curled. At 1.40 it read DAMAGED by the letter. The
one-curled torus read STAYS in 32 of 32. The three-curled eight-link torus read STAYS at 1.25 and ADVANCES ONLY at
1.40, where 12 of 12 rest one rung up.

Not scored, and the result closest to the claim: at $\lambda = 1.40$ the two-curled torus met the OPENS rule in 5
replicas of 16, every curled direction open in one region holding at least half the vertices, reached between 45,000
and 197,000 sweeps; 7 were damaged. At that $\lambda$ the start's wall is 4.8 and each rung's gap is 1.6 per vertex.
It is a minority, beside damage, at one size.

\emph{Measured} (T49; O92). One hypercube alone, 32 replicas per cell, with named points and with interchangeable
points (unlabeled graphs, where a state is weighted by its number of symmetries). The 6-cube where it is stuck
($\lambda = 1.10$ and 1.15, pushes 8 and 4): named points read PART OPEN (31 and 27 of 32; 1 and 5 fully open);
interchangeable points read STUCK (20 of 32) and MIXED. Where it has a move downhill ($\lambda = 1.25$, no push):
PART OPEN with both, fully open in 4 of 32 named and 14 of 32 interchangeable. The 8-cube read STUCK, 32 of 32, at

\section{The tie the author chose, and its first test}
\label{sec:tie}

\emph{The hypothesis's claim (the author's, 9 October \cite{repo}).} Her decision: the directions are tied so that
nothing is released until a vertex's last direction opens, all at the last; untied runs are a stepping stone and
serve as controls. Her statement, not a decision: the stand-in for the curled start that matches her picture is the
slab, $4 \times L \times L$, two directions open and one curled.

\emph{Exact} (O89, O106; Table~\ref{tab:tied}). Under this tie the slab's wall falls from 39.2 at $\lambda = 1.05$
to 4.0 at 1.25 while flat space's wall stays 64, and the gap to flat is $3a$, 0.6 to 3.0 per vertex. The gas and the
rod lie at the same $3a$ as the slab, so the openings that lead from them to the slab gain nothing, and their walls
are 8 to 30. \emph{Interpretation (ours, unverified).} Those earlier openings would have to be carried by a bath. The
ratio of the slab's wall to flat space's is 0.34 at $\lambda = 1.15$ and 0.20 at 1.20. Untied at $\lambda = 1.25$
it was $36/64 = 0.56$, and the four-link tube, which converted in a bath at $g = 1.5$ \cite{paper1}, has
$12/32 = 0.375$. At $\lambda = 1.25$, with a wall of 4, the slab is not stuck in any bath. Two cautions. The single
move is not the whole barrier in three directions. And under the tie as implemented a damaged vertex pays no tie, so
a slab vertex is relieved of its $2a$ by breaking as well as by opening.

\emph{Exact} (T54; O118, as corrected). T54 prices completed patches: the energy $\Delta H(k)$ of a slab whose
curled direction is opened over a patch of $k \times k$ columns, wired as the saved end states of T48 are wired.
These are energies of completed square patches and not a barrier: going from one patch to the next takes $2k + 1$
switches, and T54 did not price the states between. The registered verdict words are FIXED WALL, CRITICAL PATCH and
NO FINITE PATCH, read over completed patches at a held-out size, $L = 16$, after a prediction for it was written
from a fit at $L = 8$ and 12. The author's prediction was CRITICAL PATCH, with a small patch as her lean; ours was
CRITICAL PATCH, amended before the held-out size to FIXED WALL at $\lambda = 1.40$ under the tie. At all three sizes
$\Delta H(k)$ is the same exact quadratic, $ak - bk^2$, with nothing left over, for every patch that does not meet
its own image. Untied, $a = 80 - 16\lambda$ and $b = 16(\lambda - 1)$ (read off the values, not derived): at
$\lambda = 1.25$ it reads 56, 104, 144, 176 for $k = 1$ to 4, and the fitted curve peaks at 7.5 columns, at 225; at
$\lambda = 1.10$, at 19.5 columns and 608. Under the tie $a = 176 - 112\lambda$ and $b = 48(\lambda - 1)$: the fitted
curve peaks at 1.5 columns and 27 at $\lambda = 1.25$, 2.2 and 45 at 1.20, 3.3 and 77 at 1.15, and 5.5 and 145 at
1.10. The tied numbers at $L = 8$ were computed before the author confirmed her tied prediction, as the registration
discloses. The prediction for $L = 16$ was written from these, each within 10\%; for the untied $\lambda \le 1.25$
the fitted peak lies beyond what $L = 16$ can hold, and those were registered as unreadable there. \emph{The
held-out size} ($L = 16$): the quadratic fitted to its own values has exactly the same coefficients, so all six
readable numbers hold. The verdict word, read as registered over every $k$ computed, is RISES AGAIN, a word that is
reported and not scored, at $\lambda = 1.10$ to 1.20 tied and at 1.40 untied: the last patch, $k = 15$, wraps the
torus and lies above the quadratic. It is FIXED WALL at $\lambda = 1.25$ tied, where two whole patches tie at 24,
and at 1.40 tied, where the first patch is free. So the author's CRITICAL PATCH is not scored in four rows and fails
by the letter in the two that are scored; ours fails at tied 1.25 and holds at tied 1.40. Among the scored rows NO
FINITE PATCH does not appear.

\emph{Exact, and a correction} (11 October 2026; an outside review found it with its own code, and ours reproduces
it). The fitted peak is not a barrier and bounds none. Between the first two patches under the tie at
$\lambda = 1.25$, which both stand at 24, the best order of the three switches passes through 24, 20, 45, 24: the
highest energy met is 45, above the fitted 27. At $\lambda = 1.20$ the first two steps reach 62 and 66 against a
fitted 45, and at 1.15, 80 and 98 against 77. Nor are squares the cheap shapes. A single line of columns along one
of the construction's two directions costs $a - b$ for its first column and $96 - 80\lambda$ more for each further
one under the tie: 24, 20, 16, 12, 8, 4 at $\lambda = 1.25$, level at 32 at 1.20, and rising by 4 and by 8 a column
at 1.15 and 1.10; untied, every further column costs $32 - 16\lambda$. An earlier draft said the construction
``bounds the barrier from above''. That is withdrawn, and with it the reading that the tie turns the climb into one
a bath could make. The barrier to opening the slab's curled direction, tied or untied, is not known: it needs a
search over paths with every state priced, shapes other than squares included.

\emph{Measured} (T56, stage 1; O115). The slab ($L = 8$ and 12; 256 and 576 vertices) and the rod ($L = 18$ and 36)
were held in a warm bath under this tie at $\lambda = 1.10$, 1.15, 1.20 and 1.25 and $g = 1.5$ to 3.0, eight replicas
per cell, 200,000 sweeps, with four untied slab cells at $\lambda = 1.20$ as the control. No cell of 64 has an OPENS
majority. The slab's window reads STAYS at $\lambda = 1.10$, 1.15 and 1.20 and ADVANCES ONLY at 1.25, at both sizes.
The rod's reads STAYS at every $\lambda$ and both lengths. The control reads NO DIFFERENCE: tied and untied both
stay. SEVERAL for both shapes. Of 256 tied slab replicas 2 read OPENS; of 256 tied rod replicas 17, all at
$\lambda = 1.20$ or 1.25 in baths of $g = 2.0$ to 3.0, beside damage. None of the 19 ended flat: 0.74 to 2.6 per
vertex was left. The author's prediction, that the slab OPENS IN A WINDOW into ONE SPACE, failed at every $\lambda$.
Ours failed too: a window at 1.15 and 1.20, and that the tie would make the difference. SEVERAL held.

Two things, not scored, say why. First, the tied slab is damaged where the untied one is untouched: 5 to 10\% of its
vertices on average at $\lambda = 1.20$, between an eighth and three tenths at 1.25 in every bath, and none in the 32
untied replicas. That is the second caution above: the table charges a damaged vertex nothing, so it rewards
breaking. Second, where the slab stayed, opening takes more than the single move, though how much more is not
known: T54's completed patches stand 32 to 145 above the slab at $\lambda = 1.20$ to 1.10, the states between them
stand higher, and a line of columns costs 32, 40 and 48 for its first column and no less for each further one
(O118, as corrected). An earlier draft quoted T54's fitted peaks here as the barrier; that is withdrawn.
\emph{Interpretation (ours, unverified):} our prediction read the single-move wall as the barrier, against the
first caution. By the registered rule the
prediction for the held-out sizes ($L = 16$ and 72) is that nothing opens. That stage has not been run, so nothing
here is a claim about size.

\subsection{OPENS by the count, and the shape}
\label{sec:shape}

\emph{A retrospective reading of saved final graphs: exploratory, exact for each graph; it changes no verdict}
(O116). OPENS asks for open vertices in one connected region. That is a count. It does not say how wide the region
is. One rod of T56 ended with every vertex open, none damaged, and 1.2 per vertex above flat space. So for every
replica that reads OPENS we counted the vertices at each distance from a vertex. The $4 \times 4 \times 18$ rod has
at most 32 at any distance, and its farthest vertex is 13 steps away. The flat $6 \times 6 \times 8$ torus, with the
same 288 vertices, has up to 60, and its farthest is 10 away. That rod of T56 has the rod's numbers: it is the
starting torus glued together differently, so that no vertex reads as curled. Sorting each replica that reads OPENS
by the nearer profile: for T48's two-curled torus at $\lambda = 1.40$, 4 of 5 are nearer flat space; for T53, 7 of
9; for T56's rod, 9 of 17. T56's two slab replicas are a little wider than the slab. In the four-link control of T48
at $\lambda = 1.25$, 9 of the 15 replicas that read OPENS have the tube's profile and 6 are wider. The companion
paper's maps show the same at scale: of 9,215 exactly flat end states, 9,208 are the tube with its seam shifted by
two, a flat torus six links around where the tube was four. So OPENS and ONE SPACE in Table~\ref{tab:runs} are met
by the count in every case listed, and by the shape in about two thirds of the six-link ones. A width or a distance
profile belongs in any registration that uses those words.

\section{The reproduction gate}
\label{sec:gate}

This project reproduces a published curve before interpreting results built on the same code. The six-link target
is Fig.~3 of Ref.~\cite{T22}: squares per vertex against $\ln \hbar g$ for $D = 3$ at $\lambda = 1$, which is CQG.
Its caption gives neither the size of the graphs nor a protocol; we ran $N = 500$, the size the paper states for
another figure (O96). \emph{Measured.} Under the plain reading of the axis our curve is ordered at every published
coupling (Gate C, failed; O43). A reading in which the weight goes as $1/g^2$ was pre-registered and FAILS, and the
same code's two-dimensional figure is not compressed (ONE POWER) (Gate C$'$; O53). One difference is since accounted
for (O96): Eq.~(1) of Ref.~\cite{T22} counts every link twice and our energy counts it once, a factor of two in the
coupling. With the coupling halved, our cooling legs cross 9 and 6 squares per vertex at 0.677 and 0.737, and at
0.970 and 0.961, in $\ln \hbar g$, against the published 0.666 and 0.996; this was reported after the run and not
scored. Two differences remain. The published curve reaches 2 squares per vertex a factor 2.8 sooner in coupling,
and its plateau is 10.07 against our 11.4. \emph{Interpretation (ours, unverified).} Two readings are open and
neither is settled. The published runs may have been short and out of equilibrium: in an exploratory run a fast
heating leg from a slowly cooled state comes near the published plateau and width (O45). Or the published graphs may
carry triangles and pentagons, which the paper's text allows and our bipartite kernel does not (O96); against that,
the published hot end, 1.09 squares per vertex, sits at our bipartite value, 1.15, where such graphs would sit near
half of it. The gate stays open. The exact results of Sec.~\ref{sec:exact} are properties of the energy as we
derived it and do not depend on the sampler. Every measured statement is about our family and its kernel.

\section{Discussion}

The summary so far is negative. \emph{Exact:} the ladder is additive; every rung has its own wall and the walls
rise as directions open; flat space's wall is 32, 64 and 128 at every $\lambda$; and a fully curled start larger than
one hypercube is a gas. \emph{Measured:} the price of the first move is fixed with size, and in thirteen
pre-registered tests, untied or under the ties run, no push of any size tried, no bath size and no warm bath gave a
six- or eight-link setting in which a majority of runs had every curled direction open in one region. A gas opened
cube by cube. A two-curled torus opened one direction and rested, or opened fully in a minority at $\lambda = 1.40$,
beside damage. A one-curled torus given its wall did not move, and under the author's tie in a warm bath it stayed or
broke. The four-link control read ONE SPACE at $\lambda = 1.25$, though by shape most of those tubes were still
tubes (Sec.~\ref{sec:shape}). The author's predictions that one push, or a bath, opens every direction failed in every test that
could score them; her
predictions that the first move's price is fixed with size (T32) and that openings start in several places (T53)
held. Several of ours failed too: FIRST ONLY for the tori of T30, the windows we expected at $\lambda = 1.40$ and
1.50 (T39, T41, T53), and the push that starts four directions (T40).

In the project's form for a failed prediction \cite{repo}: \emph{previous claim} (the author's), one activation
opens every curled direction and releases everything. \emph{Failed because} each direction has its own wall and the
energy ties nothing: a push the size of the cheapest move opens one direction, or one cube, and stops (T33, T40, T44
to T49; O103). \emph{Replacement} (the author's): the curled start is one connected piece with a direction already
open, and a bath supplies each push in turn. \emph{Its test}, T53, read ADVANCES ONLY: the bath opens the first
direction and not the second before it damages the space. \emph{Second replacement} (the author's): the tie all at
the last, with the slab as the stand-in. \emph{Its test}, T56, read STAYS or ADVANCES ONLY for the slab, with NO
DIFFERENCE from the untied control: an opening that spreads takes more than one move, and the table rewards
breaking (O115). A
third replacement is the author's to propose; none is written here. \emph{New falsification tests:} T54 to its
held-out size under the tie, and the exact price of the cheapest breaking move out of the slab against the cheapest
opening one. The branch is abandoned in this family if T54 reads NO FINITE PATCH, or a barrier above flat space's,
at every $\lambda$ at which the slab is stuck; or if a table that also charges damage, chosen by the author and
priced exactly first, still does not open it (O115). That condition was written when T54's numbers were taken for a
barrier. With the correction above, the test that would decide it is a search for the lowest path, which is not yet
designed.

What the curled start may be is the author's to say. Her statements so far \cite{repo}: it may begin with one
direction already open; a foam of small fully curled pieces that open separately and then join is a possibility she
holds open and has not adopted; it may be long in one open direction with two curled; and the shape that matches her
picture is the slab. In the toy, only connected starts with a direction already open have reached one large open
region, and no run has shown separate pieces joining (O85).

\emph{Interpretation (ours, unverified).} What stopped the untied openings is the wall of the next direction, which
rises as directions open while flat space's wall stays fixed. The tie lowers the slab's single-move wall
(Table~\ref{tab:tied} against Table~\ref{tab:walls}) and puts the whole gap at the last opening. In T56 that was not
enough: where the wall was high the slab stayed, and where it was low the slab broke, because the tie as written
charges nothing for a broken vertex. A tie is put in by hand: even a positive result would show only that the
mechanism is possible in this family with that rule, not that reality has it. The room a conversion needs in two directions \cite{paper3}, and whether
concentrated energy curls flat space \cite{paper4}, are treated in companion drafts. No outcome here says anything
about the real universe.
