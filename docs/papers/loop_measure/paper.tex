\title{When an observer finds itself: light cones, a universe's age, and two pictures of dark energy}

\author{Emily Smith}
\email{emilysmithcreate@gmail.com}
\affiliation{Independent researcher, Charlottesville, Virginia, USA}

\date{20 October 2026 (draft; series paper 16)}

\begin{abstract}
A hypothesis in which universes are born from black holes and multiply each generation needs a measure: a rule for
where an observer should expect to find itself. We state the measure the author has fixed (counted per universe, by
each universe's own age, once per civilization, with the ages weighted by the comoving volume of the causal diamond,
and an observer defined as a civilization in which at least one intelligent being asks this particular question) and
report its first two calculations, both in standard cosmology with published inputs and rules written before any number. Under the
once-per-civilization unit the stellar reference class is invisible: counting each civilization once at a fixed delay
after its planet forms gives the cosmic star-formation history shifted by that delay, whatever the stars, so every class
tried is typical; of the units tried, the published ``we are early by a thousandfold'' returns only under those that
weight each planet by how long it lives. The one input that moves the peak is Carter's hard-steps delay. Under the
diamond weight one class goes late, two go late or typical depending on the inputs, the planet-years count goes from
early to typical, and only the author's own class, sun-like stars with the hard-steps delay, stays typical, with our
age near its median. We state why that is a declared assumption holding together and not a finding: the class is built
from our case, thirteen combinations were computed, and the choices were made with the numbers known. The
measure's first genuine test must be a quantity it predicts that was not put in; candidates are named. The diamond's
far edge is the past light cone of the universe's end, so the weight depends on how dark energy behaves late. In the
author's picture dark energy is a true constant and the diamond closes; in the published model whose network the
companion papers use, dark energy falls toward zero as its coupling runs, and if the acceleration ends there is no
event horizon, the diamond never closes, and the same measure would make our age early. The light cones therefore
separate the two pictures, and the measured equation of state of dark energy decides between them independently of any
observer. Nothing here depends on the toy model of the companion papers.
\end{abstract}

\section{The measure as decided}

The hypothesis is the author's, and what follows is her claim about reality, not a result (the record \cite{repo};
its full statement is planned as series paper 17, not yet written). In her conjecture as now worded, a universe ends
when its last black hole has dissolved and every black hole births many daughters (VISION Updates 44 and 59); each
universe runs an exact copy of one clock from its birth to its heat death, with no clock across universes (Update 68).
The measure follows from four decisions of the author's:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Per universe, by its own age.} Universes of one setting are counted whole and lined up at their births;
there is no moment common to the tree, so the youngness pressure of eternal inflation \cite{Freivogel11,BFY08} has no
common time to act in (Update 68).
\item \emph{Once per civilization.} Each civilization that ever arises counts one, placed at its birth, however long it
lasts (Update 71). With her definition of an observer (item 4) the author named what a civilization's length depends
on: things the asking itself can affect (how long a being lasts, how it learns and passes information, its lifecycle,
how destructive it is). Reading that as the reason the measure does not weight by duration is ours (Update 71).
\item \emph{The causal diamond as the weight on ages.} The places that count at age $t$ are those an observer at $t$ can
be causally tied to: the region bounded by the future light cone of the beginning and the past light cone of the end,
whose comoving volume is $V_c(t) = \tfrac{4\pi}{3} r(t)^3$ with $r = \min(\eta(t), \eta_\infty - \eta(t))$ in conformal
time \cite{BHKP07} (Update 71).
\item \emph{An observer} is a civilization in which at least one intelligent being asks this particular question
(Update 71).
\end{enumerate}

In these terms the probability that a randomly chosen observer of reference class $R$ finds itself at age $t$ of its
universe is
\begin{equation}
p_R(t) \propto V_c(t)\, W(t) \int dm\, \xi(m)\, \psi(t - d_R(m))\, \mathbf{1}[d_R(m) < \tau_R(m)],
\label{eq:measure}
\end{equation}
with $\psi$ the cosmic star-formation rate density, $\xi$ the initial mass function, $\tau_R$ the per-planet window of
the class, $d_R$ its delay from a planet's formation to a civilization (a fixed value, or a distribution for the
hard-steps form), and $W(t)$ the observation window in which a universe's history can still be learned. The count
assumes at most one civilization per habitable planet, which is how the once-per-civilization unit is computed (T72).
Two numbers are read per class: the age of the peak and $F_R = P(t < 13.8\,\mathrm{Gyr})$, the share of the class
before our age.

\section{Inputs}

All inputs are published, and each was read at the source by an assistant agent before use; the author has not read
these passages (the reading notes of 12 and 19 October 2026 \cite{repo}).
Refs.~\cite{LBS16,BHKP07,KS07,HKW18,LL18,Kipping25,Carter83,Hanson98} were read in full, Ref.~\cite{MD14} in full
except its tables and figures, which were read through their captions, and Refs.~\cite{Bryson21,SB21} in part (Bryson
and others to the end of their Sec.~V, the appendices not read; Snyder-Beattie and others in their main sections, the
equations not transcribed). Quotations come from text conversions. The star-formation history is the fit of Madau and
Dickinson \cite{MD14}, Eq.~15, converted to cosmic
time in a flat cosmology with Planck 2018 parameters and continued into the future with the exponential tail of Loeb,
Batista and Sloan \cite{LBS16} ($e$-fold 50~Gyr; 20~Gyr and none as sensitivities). The initial mass function and the
stellar lifetimes are those of Ref.~\cite{LBS16}, Eqs.~2.2 and 2.3. The occurrence of habitable planets is taken
constant within a class and cancels in the normalization, as in Ref.~\cite{LBS16}; the Kepler rates of Bryson and
others \cite{Bryson21} show no compelling dependence on stellar temperature over the G and K range. The observation
window is the hundred-billion-year horizon of Krauss and Scherrer \cite{KS07}. The diamond's volume peaks at $0.231$ of
the dark-energy timescale against the $0.23$ of Ref.~\cite{BHKP07}, Eq.~20, at 4.05~Gyr for our constant.

\section{Reference classes, declared first}

Four classes were fixed before any number (PREREGISTRATION T72 \cite{repo}). \emph{A}: sun-like stars, 0.8 to 1.2
solar masses, the star's lifetime as the window, a fixed delay of 4.5~Gyr \cite{HKW18}. \emph{B}: K and G stars,
0.5 to 1.4 solar masses, with the lower cut of Lingam and Loeb \cite{LL18} and Kipping \cite{Kipping25}, a fixed
10~Gyr window per planet \cite{Kipping25}, a 3~Gyr delay. \emph{C}: all stars, 0.08 to 3 solar masses, the control.
\emph{D}: the author's composite: sun-like stars, the hard-steps delay of Carter \cite{Carter83}, Hanson
\cite{Hanson98} and Snyder-Beattie and others \cite{SB21} in the form ``the observer appears in the last $1/(n+1)$ of
its planet's window'' with $n = 5$ ($3$ and $7$ reported), and the observation cap. The reproduction gate was class C
under Ref.~\cite{LBS16}'s own planet-years unit: the share before our age came out at $0.17\,\%$ against their
$0.1\,\%$, inside the registered factor of two; the peak of $dP/d\ln t$ came out at $4.4 \times 10^{12}$~yr against a
registered window of $5 \times 10^{12}$ to $2 \times 10^{13}$ around their ``around $10^{13}$'', a miss by the letter.
The registration said the classes are not read on a failed gate; the author took the gate as passed on the share and
the 2016 paper's loose wording, and the classes below were read on that decision (PREREGISTRATION T72, ASSUMPTIONS
O138 \cite{repo}). The verdict words were fixed in advance: TYPICAL for $0.05 \le F_R \le 0.95$, EARLY below, LATE
above, and INCONCLUSIVE when a class's $F_R$ crosses a boundary under the registered sensitivity variations.

\emph{The unit decides, not the stars (exact).} Under the once-per-civilization unit with a fixed delay $d$, the mass
integral in Eq.~\eqref{eq:measure} is a constant for every class whose stars outlive $d$, so $p_R(t) \propto
\psi(t-d)$: classes A and C are the same curve to the last digit, and B differs from A only through its delay. Of the
units tried, the published ``early'' result of Ref.~\cite{LBS16} returns only under the two that weight each planet by
how long it lives: the planet-years gate ($F = 0.0017$) and the observer-moment variant, reported beside the scored
count and not scored (class C $F = 0.0009$; A gives 0.44 and B 0.67, so there the sun-like restriction does its work;
Ref.~\cite{repo}). It does not survive the diamond weight. The disagreement in the literature over red dwarfs is,
under this measure, a disagreement about the counting unit as much as about habitability.

\emph{The hard-steps delay is the one input that moves the peak.} Class D's peak sits at 17.3~Gyr with a quarter of
its observers before our age.

\emph{The diamond weight.} $V_c$ is already about half its peak at our age and keeps falling, so under it most weighted
observers of classes A, B and C precede us (B LATE; A and C LATE in the scored row and INCONCLUSIVE by the registered
sensitivity rule, their rows spreading over LATE and TYPICAL), the planet-years gate flips to TYPICAL, and class D
moves to $F = 0.53$ with its peak at 11.3~Gyr. The author predicted, before the run, that the diamond would move the
peaks (it did), that the shares before our age would rise (they did in every class) and that no verdict word would
change (the gate's and B's changed, and A's and C's became a spread); our own prediction that A, B and C would stay
typical failed as well.

\section{Two pictures of the end, and what the light cones do}

The diamond's far edge is the past light cone of the universe's end. It exists only if the universe has a future event
horizon, that is, only if the conformal time
\begin{equation}
\eta_\infty = \int_0^\infty \frac{dt}{a(t)}
\label{eq:etainf}
\end{equation}
is finite. For a dark energy with a constant equation of state $w$, which eventually dominates whenever $w < 0$, the
integral converges if and only if $w < -1/3$, the condition for the expansion to keep accelerating (textbook; to verify).
Where it diverges, $r(t) = \eta(t)$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:measure}'s weight, $V_c$ grows without bound, and the observation
window of Ref.~\cite{KS07}, which rests on dark energy carrying other galaxies out of view, never closes. The weight then
rises with age, and only the decline of star formation limits how late a typical observer is.

\emph{The author's picture.} Dark energy is the network's density of defects at a coupling fixed by the loop's total
energy, and space grows by gaining points that carry that density, so the density per volume never thins: a true
cosmological constant, $w = -1$, and an end in empty de Sitter space (VISION Updates 70, 72 and 76 \cite{repo}). The
horizon exists, the diamond closes, and the results above are her picture's.

\emph{The published model's picture.} In the model whose network the companion papers use, the finite-coupling ground
state is a timeless, negatively curved geometry \cite{T22,T23}. As a closed surface such a geometry needs at least two
handles (Gauss--Bonnet; ours). That geometry is read, far from the beginning, as de Sitter space with a positive
cosmological constant. Ref.~\cite{T24} (its Sec.~IV) then lets the coupling run, $1/g$ being universal time, so that dark
energy falls: the expansion ``will accelerate again, albeit at a much smaller rate, which actually tends to zero as the
ground state curvature becomes smaller and smaller at tiny couplings.'' If the acceleration ends, Eq.~\eqref{eq:etainf}
diverges, the diamond never closes, and the measure of Sec.~I would put most observers later than us: under that model
our age would be early. Ref.~\cite{T24} gives no law for the fall, so this is the limit, not a number; and the same
passage has matter turning into spacetime as the coupling runs, which would thin the late tail of star formation and
pull the other way. Neither paper computes an observer measure; the comparison is ours.

\emph{What separates them.} Under the measure, the two pictures predict different typical ages. Without any observer,
they predict different equations of state: $w = -1$ for hers, and $w$ rising above $-1$ for his. The measured value is
$w = -1.03 \pm 0.03$ (Ref.~\cite{Planck18}, with supernovae and baryon acoustic oscillations; to verify); recent baryon-acoustic analyses prefer a dark energy that changes with time (to be
cited after reading). The classes of Table~\ref{tab:results} under declared stand-ins for a fading dark energy
(constant $w = -0.9$, $-0.7$ and $-1/3$, the last at the edge where the horizon disappears) are the next calculation,
registered as T77 with the author's prediction before any number \cite{repo}. A third difference does not enter: his
picture has one universal time and one universe, hers one clock per universe and many, and for one universe counted by
its own age the two agree.

\section{What this does and does not show}

Class D is the only class that keeps our age typical under both weights, and lands near its median under the diamond
($F = 0.53$). This is a declared assumption holding together, not a finding, for three reasons (CLAUDE.md rule 13
\cite{repo}): the class was built from our own case (we orbit a sun-like star; the hard-steps delay is calibrated on
Earth); thirteen combinations of class and weighting were computed (the four classes and the gate under each of the
two weights, and three classes under the observer-moment variant), and among that many one landing within 0.05 of the
median is about what chance gives (ours: each would with probability about 0.1 if the shares were spread evenly); and
the measure's two remaining definitions were chosen by the author with these numbers known, for reasons of principle
that are recorded as such. Accordingly our own age is not scored under the measure from here.
Two things are shown: the counting unit decides more than the stars do, and the diamond and the observation cap are
different weights with very different consequences: the cap changes nothing in class D, because it bites only after
that class's planets are gone (on the planet-years gate alone it lifts $F$ from 0.0017 to 0.08), while the diamond
changes every class.

The measure's first genuine test must be a quantity it predicts that was not put in. Candidates, none chosen: the
share of observers who find dark energy already dominant in their universe; the distribution among observers of a
universe's age over its dark-energy timescale; and, from the toy, the one term it supplies, the daughters born per unit
of energy fed.
