\title{What the change leaves behind: relic defects of fixed size in a graph model of emergent geometry, their
kinds, their number, and how long they last}

\author{Emily Smith}
\email{emilysmithcreate@gmail.com}
\affiliation{Independent researcher, Charlottesville, Virginia, USA}

\date{DRAFT, 1 November 2026}

\begin{abstract}
In a companion paper a $4 \times L$ torus with one direction curled was shown to be metastable, and to convert
after activation with a release of energy, in the family of graph energies obtained from combinatorial quantum
gravity (CQG) by raising the coefficient $\lambda$ of its local term above 1 (only $\lambda = 1$ is CQG; every
result here is at $\lambda = 1.25$, next to CQG and not in it). Here we study the defects the conversion leaves.
Read from the saved wiring there are three kinds, each of fixed size and each a local minimum of the energy: a
column (four vertices in a loop that still closes a square, $24\lambda - 16 = 14$ units), a double column (eight
vertices, 8 units), and a knot of eight vertices of two kinds (20 to 30 units). Isolated, with a reservoir large
enough to stay cold, a conversion started at one place leaves about one relic at every size from 64 to 288
vertices, at a position indistinguishable from uniform. The number rises with the number of nucleation events
by less than one per event ($1.10, 2.15, 2.50, 3.40$ relics for $1, 2, 4, 8$ planted seeds), and in proportion
to length when a long torus nucleates spontaneously. In an external bath at fixed coupling a lone column anneals
within $500$ to $80{,}500$ sweeps; under cooling it is arrested. When products of spontaneous nucleation at
1,024 vertices are cooled over 0 to 300,000 size-fair sweeps, the columns fall from about 2 to 0.1 per product while
the other two kinds do not change, so that the excess energy falls only from 4.3\% to 1.3\% of the release. Two
predictions of a gentler fall, the author's and ours, failed. Every verdict quoted was pre-registered before
its run; the readings from wiring are exact.
\end{abstract}

\maketitle

\section{Introduction}

A change from one ordered arrangement to another releases energy and, in an isolated system, must keep the
released energy in the graph or its reservoir. Conservation alone does not require a residual defect. In the
companion paper \cite{paper1} the starting arrangement is a $4 \times L$ torus, one direction curled to a single
square, which is metastable once the local term of CQG \cite{T17,KTB19,T25} is weighted by a coefficient
$\lambda > 1$ and which converts, after activation, to arrangements with two squares on every edge. That paper
measured the escape and conversion clocks, the cost of the first exit and the release. This one measures what
is left: its kinds, its number, where it sits, and how long it lasts.

The question is narrow and the answer is measurable. The reason for asking is the author's hypothesis that
space emerged from an ordered arrangement by a change of this kind, and that such a change leaves a remainder;
she wants tested whether a remainder can last. Ref.~\cite{T24} describes something in the same family for CQG
itself: allotropes, long-lived metastable domains of the network in a different discrete arrangement. The
relics studied here are not allotropes in that sense: they are small objects of fixed size, made by the
conversion, at $\lambda > 1$.

The process has a second set of relatives, in the nonequilibrium dynamics of ordered phases in condensed-matter
models, and we use that field's words where the concept is the same. There, order that forms in an isolated
system does so by nucleation and growth and can end in an arrested state whose defects no longer move
\cite{JC26}; in contact with a thermal bath the defects diffuse and annihilate in pairs \cite{JYC25}. Those
works study other models and are context, not validation. Their defects are kinks, walls between two
symmetry-related versions of one order. A relic is a small piece of the parent arrangement left in place, and
we do not call it a kink (Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion}).

Every kinetic statement below concerns one Markov chain, the local edge switch used to sample the model
\cite{KTB19}, with Metropolis acceptance, and the sealed (isolated, microcanonical) version of it built from
Creutz demons \cite{Creutz83}. Energies, the relics' costs and the classification of end states do not depend
on the chain; waiting times, annealing times and counts per seed do.

\section{Model, dynamics, and how a relic is read}
\label{sec:model}

The state is a four-regular bipartite graph on $N$ vertices obeying the hard-core rule of CQG (no two vertices
share more than two neighbors). With $S$ the number of squares and $X = \sum_e (S_e - 2)_+$ the number of
surplus squares on edges carrying more than two, the energy is
\begin{equation}
  H = 16(N - S) + 4\lambda X,
  \label{eq:H}
\end{equation}
which for $\lambda = 1$ is the CQG action \cite{T25} and for $\lambda \ne 1$ is not a curvature. Any
arrangement with two squares on every edge has $H = 0$ and is a ground state for $\lambda \ge 1$; the curled
torus sits $\varepsilon = 4(\lambda - 1)$ per vertex above it, 1 at $\lambda = 1.25$.

Two letters need a warning for a reader from condensed matter, where both name interaction strengths. Here
$\lambda$ weights the local term. The coupling $g$ of CQG plays the role of a temperature: at fixed $g$ a
proposed switch is accepted with probability $\min[1,\exp(-\Delta H/g)]$, which puts the graph in contact with
an external thermal bath.

\emph{Reading a relic.} The square-count signature $d(v)$ of a vertex is the number of its six edge pairs that
close no square: 2 where the graph is locally a square lattice, 1 on the curled torus, 0 in a 4-cube. It serves
as a local order parameter and does not certify geometry. A relic is a localized defect: a connected piece of
vertices not at $d = 2$ in a product that is otherwise at $d = 2$. Its excess energy is read exactly from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:H} on the saved wiring.

\emph{What the product is.} ``Flat'' below means $d = 2$, a local statement. The companion paper reconstructed
3,521 of its zero-energy end states: 3,446 are connected tori whose shortest noncontractible loop is six edges,
the parent torus re-glued with its seam shifted by two sites; 73 are disconnected and two are Klein bottles
\cite{paper1}. The product is therefore not a wide sheet, and nothing here measures a width unless it says so.

\emph{Isolated (sealed) runs} keep a reservoir of $C$ stores beside the graph and exchange energy with nothing
else. The stores play the part of the internal bath of Ref.~\cite{JC26}, degrees of freedom inside the isolated
system that take up and give back energy without dissipating it; here the reservoir is put in by hand and $C$ is
a declared parameter. A proposed switch picks one store at random, is refused if that store cannot pay for it,
and otherwise pays or is paid; $H + \sum \text{stores}$ is conserved to the last unit in every run reported.
The stores' mean energy is not a calibrated temperature \cite{paper1}. The isolated runs below use $C = 2N$,
large enough that the release leaves the reservoir cold and the product at $d = 2$ \cite{paper1}: in T10 and T11
one store starts with a 12-unit seed and the rest are empty; in T17, T19 and T25 every store starts empty and the
change is started by planted seeds. A seed is a nucleus of the product arrangement, planted or spontaneous; a
front is the moving boundary of the domain that grows from it; where two fronts meet, two domains coalesce. T37
and T51 run in an external bath.

\section{Three kinds of relic, read from the wiring}
\label{sec:kinds}

\emph{Exact for each graph read.} A leftover has been named from a count and been wrong before in this project,
so each kind below was read from saved adjacency lists.

\emph{The column.} A closed loop of four vertices at $d = 1$ that closes a square, as each column of the
parent torus does. It has one extra square, and holds 4, 9 or 14 units at $\lambda = 1.25$ according to how
many edges at its vertices carry a third square ($S = N + 1$ and $X = 4$, 5 or 6). The 14-unit form,
$24\lambda - 16$, is the common one: 1,070 of the 1,142 single columns in the end states of
Sec.~\ref{sec:cooling}. In it two opposite edges of the loop and one edge leaving each vertex carry the
third square; in the 4-unit form all four edges of the loop do and no edge leaving it does. In none of the
2,045 columns of the saved end states read so far does the loop wind around the product: removing its four
vertices never separates their neighbors locally (the project record, entry O126). Its cheapest single switch
out costs 12.

\emph{The double column.} Eight vertices at $d = 1$ with twelve edges among them, each vertex keeping three
edges inside the piece and one out: a $4 \times 2$ segment of the parent torus. It holds 8 units.

\emph{The knot of two kinds.} Eight vertices: two joined pairs at $d = 1$, each vertex's four edges carrying 2,
2, 3 and 3 squares, and two joined pairs at $d = 3$, each vertex's edges carrying 2, 1, 1 and 2. It holds 20
units at its own vertices and 30 within one step. Counted by connected pieces it appears as four pieces of two vertices, two steps apart, which is how the
registered analysis first counted it. A 20-unit ``twist'' seen in 9 of 80 isolated runs (Sec.~\ref{sec:one}) is
of the same kind; whether it is the same arrangement was not settled.

\emph{Pre-registered as T55}, on the 600 end states of Sec.~\ref{sec:cooling}, before any of them was read
this way. Three readings of the pieces that cooling does not remove were registered: SCAR (a single switch
removes the piece and lowers the energy), SEAM (the piece carries an edge joining places at least seven steps
apart by any other route, the stitching between domains that grew from different seeds) and KNOT (neither). The
verdict was KNOT for both piece sizes scored: all 652 pieces of two and all 103 pieces of eight at $L = 256$ are local
minima, with a cheapest exit of 11 in nearly all, and 4 of 2,121 pieces of any size carry such an edge. The
author's prediction, SEAM, failed; ours held in part (not SCAR; the pieces of eight are not SEAM as we had
guessed).

Rarer resting states, 8 to 45 units, were read from twelve of the companion paper's decays: combinations of
small defects, among them a two-vertex twist (20 units), a 3-cube, an open line of three vertices, single edges
and single vertices; eleven matched their recorded release exactly, and the twelfth changed state inside its
last measuring window. Whether the list of
kinds is complete is not known.

The share of the release kept by one 14-unit column is exact, $(24\lambda - 16)/[4(\lambda - 1)N]$: 22\% at
$N = 64$, falling as $1/N$.

\section{One seed, about one relic, at every size}
\label{sec:one}

\emph{Pre-registered as T10.} Twenty isolated conversions at each of $N = 64, 96, 192, 288$ ($C = 2N$, a
12-unit seed, 30,000 sweeps). The prediction on record was that the mean number of relics rises with $N$. The
verdict was ONE RING, HOWEVER LARGE: at every size the product holds about one column, the mean count $0.85$
to $1.05$, with a slope of $0.0003 \pm 0.0003$ per vertex. (The registered label keeps the word ``ring''; the
four vertices lie along the torus, spanning one to four of its original columns, and do not wind around it.) A second check, that every
relic holds 14 units, failed in 9 of the 80 runs, which held the 20-unit twist instead.

\emph{Pre-registered as T11}, at $N = 64$ and 96. Two readings were possible: the relic marks the seed, or it
marks the place opposite the seed, where the two fronts from one nucleus would meet. In the 36 and 28 runs
holding one relic its position is indistinguishable from uniform along the torus: NEITHER. Where the fronts
actually met was not measured.

\section{How many}
\label{sec:count}

\emph{Planted seeds; pre-registered as T17.} A $96 \times 4$ torus ($N = 384$), isolated and cold, with $k$
seeds planted as the cheapest move out of the torus at evenly spaced columns; twenty runs per $k$, all
converting. The author's hypothesis corresponds to one relic per seed, though she did not pick among the
registered options; ours leaned toward one per torus whatever $k$. Neither held: $1.10$, $2.15$, $2.50$ and
$3.40$ relics per torus for $k = 1, 2, 4, 8$, a slope of $0.29 \pm 0.04$ per extra seed, verdict BETWEEN. The
slope is only about one standard error above the boundary with one per torus, and the rises (1.05, then 0.35,
then 0.90) are poorly described by a line. The counts are more regular than independent events would give
(sub-Poissonian: variance over mean 0.09 to 0.50). Where domains grown from neighboring seeds coalesce, relics
may merge or anneal; that was not measured.

\emph{Spontaneous nucleation; pre-registered as T37.} The chance of a seed per sweep does not grow with the
torus while a front's speed per sweep falls with its size, so a long enough torus nucleates in several places
before any one domain has grown across it. Tori $4 \times L$ were started in an external bath at $g = 1.25$
($L = 1024$) and at $g = 1.5$ and $1.75$ ($L = 64$ to $1024$), 12 to 40 replicas a cell. The registered
predictions were ours, with the same verdict inferred for the author: that the seed count grows with length,
that the converted fraction follows the one-dimensional law of nucleation and growth \cite{KJMA} (Avrami
exponent 2), and that the energy left grows at least threefold from $L = 64$ to the largest $L$. All three held:
the seed count grows as $L^{0.92}$ and $L^{0.78}$ at $g = 1.5$ and 1.75, and the Avrami exponent is $2.02$,
$2.14$ and $2.03$ at the three couplings. A fourth, the seed count computed from the measured nucleation rate
and front speed with nothing fitted, holds at $g = 1.25$ (0.71 of the observed) and fails at the warmer
couplings (0.41 and 0.16 at $L = 1024$).

Read afterwards from the saved graphs, the cells divide in two. Where the product stays at $d = 2$ (about 95\%
of vertices), columns are left in proportion to length: one per 57 to 72 columns of torus at $g = 1.5$ for
$L \le 256$, and at $g = 1.25$ about one for every three seeds, close to the $0.29$ per planted seed above. At
$L = 1024$ and $g = 1.5$ or $1.75$, 55\% and 74\% of vertices ended at $d > 2$, the squares per vertex fell to
$0.90$ and $0.83$, and the graph had absorbed $1.75$ and $2.49$ per vertex from the bath: thermal melting, with
energy supplied from outside. At this $\lambda$ an isolated torus's own release is too small to do that
\cite{paper1}. The coupling at which the product gives way to the random phase falls as the system grows
(the published curves for $N = 100$ to 200 in the parent model do not collapse either; Ref.~\cite{KTB19},
Fig.~8a, our reading), so the test of Sec.~\ref{sec:cooling} nucleates at the coldest of these couplings.

\section{Lifetime in an external bath}
\label{sec:lifetime}

\emph{Pre-registered as T19.} Products with exactly one column, made as in T17 at $k = 1$ from a $24 \times 4$
torus ($N = 96$), were held at fixed coupling $g = 1.0$, $1.25$ and $1.5$ for 100,000 sweeps, the relic's
position read every 500. The options on record were that it moves, that it anneals at the warmer couplings and
stays at the colder, or that it stays. It anneals at every coupling: at $g = 1.0$ all nine were gone between
4,000 and 80,500 sweeps; at 1.25 all ten within 500 to 13,000; at 1.5 all ten within 11,000, two after moving
first. Our prediction, that it would stay at $g = 1.0$, was wrong. In T10's cold isolated runs the relic lasted
the whole 30,000 sweeps.

In the terms of defect kinetics: each product held one relic, so each annealed singly, with no partner to
meet, and 27 of the 29 did so without first being seen to move. A column has low mobility and is not removed
by pair annihilation, which is how the kinks of Refs.~\cite{JC26,JYC25} are removed.

\section{Cooling}
\label{sec:cooling}

Reality cools as it expands, so what matters for a long-lived relic is a race between annealing and cooling.

\emph{One relic; pre-registered as T25.} A product with one column ($N = 96$) was cooled from $g = 1.25$, where
every column anneals within 13,000 sweeps, to $g = 0.25$, where no move is accepted, by the same factor each
block of 500 sweeps over $t_{\rm cool} = 300$ to $100{,}000$ sweeps, then held cold for 20,000 sweeps. The
registered verdict was FREEZES IN if some cooling time keeps a majority of relics and the fastest keeps the
most. The prediction recorded for the author was FREEZES IN; it was inferred from her stated positions, and
she confirmed it only after the reading. Ours was FREEZES IN with a threshold between 1,000 and 10,000 sweeps.
The relics surviving were 20/20, 17/17, 19/19, 15/19, 14/18 and 7/19 at $t_{\rm cool} = 300$, 1,000, 3,000,
10,000, 30,000 and 100,000: FREEZES IN, with the registered ``freeze-out time'', the longest cooling that keeps
a majority, at 30,000 sweeps, three to thirty times longer than we predicted. (That label is a threshold
cooling duration. It is not the freeze-out time of the Kibble--Zurek literature, the instant at which a system
can no longer keep up with a cooling of finite rate \cite{dCZ14}.) A relic that anneals at every fixed coupling
tried is therefore kept by cooling. This is dynamical arrest in the sense of Ref.~\cite{JC26}, where defects
become immobile once the energy available to them falls below the local barrier; there the arrest follows a
sudden change in an isolated system, and here it follows cooling by an external bath (our reading).

\emph{Many relics; pre-registered as T51.} Tori $4 \times L$ were started at $g = 1.25$ and left to nucleate
spontaneously until at least 90\% of vertices were at $d = 2$ and no stretch of unconverted torus remained.
Each product was then cooled to $g = 0.25$ over $t_{\rm cool}$ and held, every cooling time starting from the
same product. Times are in size-fair sweeps, $N/96$ sweeps of the chain, so that each local pair of edges is
offered as often per unit time as at T25's 96 vertices. At $L = 256$ ($N = 1{,}024$) there were 80 products
for $t_{\rm cool} = 0$ to 100,000 and 40 for 300,000; at $L = 128$ and 512, 40 each at 10,000. All 160 tori
converted, and at the end of the nucleation stage they held $1.27 \pm 0.21$, $2.14 \pm 0.15$ and $4.90 \pm 0.33$
columns at $L = 128$, 256 and 512, one per 100 to 120 columns of torus, with 1.75, 2.67 and 3.90 pieces of
other kinds.

The registered question was the shape of the fall of the column count $C$ with cooling time, scored on
$R(t) = C(10t)/C(t)$ at $t = 10{,}000$ and $30{,}000$: GENTLE if both lie between 0.25 and 0.85, CLIFF if
either is below 0.25. The author predicted GENTLE and so did we. The result is $R(10{,}000) = 0.347 \pm 0.040$
and $R(30{,}000) = 0.079 \pm 0.035$: CLIFF. Both predictions failed.

Three further results are in Table~\ref{tab:t51} and beside it. \emph{The energy that stays is not in the
columns.} The columns fall seventeenfold while the excess energy falls threefold, because the knots and
double columns do not change with cooling time at all over the range run. Fitted from 10,000 upward, as
registered before the reading, the columns fall as $t_{\rm cool}^{-0.82 \pm 0.13}$ and the excess energy as
$t_{\rm cool}^{-0.34 \pm 0.06}$. These are apparent exponents over a decade and a half \cite{BBJC26}, and a
constant population beside a vanishing one implies a floor that a power law does not have; a form with a floor
has not been tested on registered data. \emph{The size-fair clock missed one of its two points.} Its
registered prediction was that the survival at 1,024 vertices would repeat T25's at 96: against T25's
$C(30{,}000)/C(10{,}000) = 0.99$ and $C(100{,}000)/C(10{,}000) = 0.47$ we find $0.78 \pm 0.05$ and
$0.35 \pm 0.04$, the first outside the registered window of 0.20. On that clock the larger product loses its
columns sooner. \emph{The count per length does not depend on size} at the one cooling time tested: $0.0107$,
$0.0083$ and $0.0093$ columns per column of torus at $L = 128$, 256 and 512, each pair within two standard
errors. At $L = 512$, 7 of 40 products ended as two separate pieces.

\section{Completion statistics in the form used for gravitational waves from a first-order transition}
\label{sec:gw}

Predictions of the gravitational-wave spectrum of a cosmological first-order transition are written in terms of
four numbers evaluated at the transition \cite{CapriniEtAl16,CapriniEtAl20,HLLP21,Athron23}: the temperature $T_*$;
the strength $\alpha$, the energy released over the thermal energy of the old phase (Ref.~\cite{CapriniEtAl16},
Eq.~(4); Ref.~\cite{Athron23}, Eq.~(5.23)); the inverse duration $\beta/H_*$, which fixes the mean bubble separation
through what Ref.~\cite{HLLP21} (its Eq.~(7.21)) writes as
\begin{equation}
  n_{\rm bubble} = \frac{\beta^3}{8\pi v_w^3} = \frac{1}{R_*^3};
  \label{eq:nbubble}
\end{equation}
and the wall speed $v_w$; with efficiency factors $\kappa$ that split the release among the walls, bulk motion of
the fluid and heat (Ref.~\cite{CapriniEtAl16}, Sec.~2.2). That literature is a target for the form of this report,
not evidence for anything in it. This section recasts what the paper already measures as pure numbers in the model's
own units, so that a reader from those groups sees at once what could be used and what could not. No run was made
for it, no number elsewhere in the paper changes, and nothing here predicts a signal: the model has no fluid, and
the conversion is the decay of a metastable arrangement under fixed rules, not a thermal transition in a cooling
bath.

\emph{(a) Completion mode (measured; four links, $\lambda = 1.25$, $4 \times L$ tori unless said).} Started at one
place, isolated, the conversion completes as one domain with two fronts, at $N = 64$ to 288 (Sec.~\ref{sec:one}) and
at $L = 96$, 192 and 384 (T47, every replica of 48 converting fully). In an external bath at fixed $g$ it completes
by several seeds whose domains coalesce, on average in every cell of T37 (Sec.~\ref{sec:count}; the counts are in
item (b)) and of T51 (Sec.~\ref{sec:cooling}); at $L = 512$, 7 of 40 products of T51 ended as two separate pieces, and the warm long tubes
of T37 melted instead of completing. Two results from the six-link version of the model (three directions; a
different setting, carrying the open reproduction-gate caveat of the project record, VISION Update 24) read the
same distinction: a connected $4 \times 4 \times L$ torus with two directions curled, in a bath at fixed $g$, opened
its first direction from several places, a median of 2 open regions (1 to 6) at $L = 72$ and 1 at $L = 18$ when a
tenth of its vertices had opened (T53, SEVERAL); the same torus under the ``all at the last'' tie, isolated and
fed at one end with 30 units in each of eight stores, opened as one front from the fed end at $L = 8$, 12 and 16,
and from several places at 60 units a store from $L = 12$ up (T88, ONE FRONT and SEVERAL), with nothing opening at 15
a store, below the exact wall of 24 to 25.6. In T88 the front was paid by the fed stores and never by its own release
(exact: one opened vertex releases 2.4 or 3.0 against that wall), and the opened region was heavily damaged, so
``front'' there names where the opening started and spread, not a clean product.

\emph{(b) Seed density per unit length (measured; the model's one-dimensional form of Eq.~\eqref{eq:nbubble}).}
Means of the registered seed count over the saved T37 rows: at $g = 1.25$, $20.6 \pm 0.9$ seeds in a torus of 1,024
columns, one per 50 columns; at $g = 1.5$, $1.93$, $4.50$, $9.20$, $19.0$ and $33.1$ seeds at $L = 64$, 128, 256,
512 and 1,024, one per 27 to 33 columns; at $g = 1.75$, $3.05$, $6.78$, $13.1$, $22.3$ and $38.5$, one per 19 to 27
columns (standard errors 0.14 to 0.98). The growth exponents 0.92 and 0.78 are in Sec.~\ref{sec:count}. The
nucleation rate by T37's registered definition, $I = 1/(L\,\bar t_1)$ with $\bar t_1$ the mean sweep of the first
seed, is about $7 \times 10^{-6}$, $3$ to $5 \times 10^{-5}$ and $1.0$ to $1.5 \times 10^{-4}$ seeds per column per
size-fair sweep at $g = 1.25$, 1.5 and 1.75 (arithmetic on the saved first-seed times; on the chain's own sweep
it falls as $1/L$, Sec.~\ref{sec:cooling}). At fixed $g$ this rate is constant in time, and with a front speed $v$ the
one-dimensional count is $k = (L/2)\sqrt{\pi I / v}$ \cite{KJMA}, the form T37's third prediction used; the
cosmological rate instead grows as $e^{\beta t}$ as the universe cools (Ref.~\cite{CapriniEtAl16}, Eqs.~(1) to
(3)), so a bath at fixed $g$ has no $\beta$. The relics follow the seeds: one column per 100 to 120 columns of torus
at $g = 1.25$ (T51), and in T37's cell at that coupling about one column per three seeds (Sec.~\ref{sec:count}).

\emph{(c) The share of the release left in defects (measured; exact for each graph read).} The release is
$4(\lambda - 1) = 1$ per vertex. After the opening and a quench, $4.3\%$ of it is held in defects; after cooling
over 300,000 size-fair sweeps, $1.3 \pm 0.3\%$, falling as $t_{\rm cool}^{-0.34 \pm 0.06}$ from 10,000 upward
(Table~\ref{tab:t51}). What holds it at the slowest cooling is a fixed population: per 1,024-vertex product about
0.33 knots of two kinds (20 to 30 units), 0.2 double columns (8 units) and 0.13 columns (14 units), about one knot
and one double column per three to five products and one column per eight, each a local minimum
(Sec.~\ref{sec:kinds}), the number of knots and double columns set at the opening and not by the cooling. A single 14-unit column holds $(24\lambda - 16)/[4(\lambda - 1)N]$ of the
release, 22\% at $N = 64$ and falling as $1/N$ (exact).

\emph{(d) The front's speed (measured, isolated, four links).} The front's pace is steady: the pace ratio of T47
(1 for a fixed speed, 2 for diffusive spreading) has medians 0.85 to 1.28 at $L = 96$, 192 and 384 in both reservoirs.
Its speed per chain sweep halves as $L$ doubles (0.0078, 0.0041, 0.0021 columns per sweep with a shared reservoir
of $2N$ stores; 0.0054, 0.0028, 0.0013 with one store per vertex) because the chain offers any one local pair of
edges about $2/N$ times a sweep; on the size-fair clock the speed is 0.031 to 0.034 columns per fair sweep with the
shared reservoir and 0.020 to 0.022 with local stores, within 8\% of their means across sizes. The speed depends on
the energy stored ahead of the front: in a $4 \times 192$ tube with one store per vertex, a band of 16 columns given
$e$ units per vertex (all below the 12 that would let the band open by itself) was crossed in a median 6,170 sweeps
at $e = 0$, 3,310 at $e = 6$ and 2,750 at $e = 10$; the crossing time relative to the bare stretch on the other side
of the seed was 1.13, 0.97, 0.80 and 0.71 at $e = 1$, 3, 6 and 10 (T50: no effect at 1 and 3, faster at 6 and 10).

\emph{What each stands in for, and what cannot be supplied (our reading, unverified).} $T_*$: nothing. The coupling
$g$ plays the part of a temperature in the acceptance rule, in the model's own units, and the openings above
happened at $g = 1.25$ to 1.75; there is no kelvin and no $g_*$. $\alpha$: the numerator is exact, $4(\lambda - 1)$
per vertex; the denominator, the thermal excess energy of the curled phase at the opening coupling, is not measured
in this paper, so $\alpha$ is not supplied here, and in the formulas it enters only through $\kappa\alpha/(1 +
\alpha)$, the share that becomes bulk motion, which has no counterpart in a model without a fluid. $\beta/H_*$: item
(b) is the model's $n_{\rm bubble}$ in one dimension, seeds per column and a rate per column per fair sweep; it
becomes $\beta/H_*$ only with a length for a column and a Hubble time, neither of which the model has, and at fixed
$g$ the rate has no $\beta$ at all. $v_w$: item (d) is a fixed speed in columns per fair sweep, with a measured
dependence on stored energy; there is no speed of light to divide by and no sound speed has been defined for the
open phase, so $v_w$ is not supplied. $\kappa$: item (c) is a defect-energy fraction of the release, about 1\% at
the slowest cooling and set by the seeds; the walls' share $\kappa_\phi$ radiates during collisions and the heat
share thins with expansion, while these knots neither move nor radiate and, being a fixed number of objects, would
thin as matter does, so the fraction has no place in that energy budget and is reported as what it is. There is no
$K$, no $\bar U_f$ and nothing for turbulence. Of the four inputs, then, the model offers a qualitative version of
one (the completion mode and seed density behind $\beta/H_*$), a shape without a scale for another ($v_w$), the
numerator of a third ($\alpha$) and nothing for the fourth ($T_*$); every amplitude in the literature needs the
kinetic energy fraction of a plasma, and the model has no plasma. A map from the sweep to a unit of time, if one is
ever decided, is what would be needed before any of these numbers became a frequency or an amplitude.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\emph{What is on the record for this model.} The conversion leaves three kinds of small defect of fixed size,
each a local minimum. Started at one place it leaves about one, whatever the size. Their number is set by how
the conversion started: it grows with the number of nucleation events by less than one per event, and in
proportion to the length of a torus that nucleates spontaneously. A lone column anneals at each fixed coupling
tried and is arrested by cooling. Among many, slow cooling removes the columns and leaves the knots and double
columns, which hold most of the energy that stays.

\emph{For the author's hypothesis}, which asks whether a remainder of such a change can last: in this model
the remainder that lasts is not the common relic. It is the two rarer kinds, whose number over the range run
depends on how the change started and not on how slowly its product cooled. Whether they anneal on longer
clocks is not known: the range ends at 300,000 size-fair sweeps. A first exit of nearly the same cost separates
the kind that anneals (12 units) from the kinds that do not (11), so the first exit is not what decides; the
barrier that would is the highest point on the lowest route to the product, which has not been computed for
any of the three.

\emph{In the vocabulary of nonequilibrium defect kinetics} \cite{JC26,JYC25,FZC26} (our reading; those works
study other models): the conversion is the decay of a metastable ordered state by nucleation and growth; a
relic is a localized defect trapped in a local minimum; its number is set by the nucleation events, with a
yield below one per event; in an external bath a column anneals singly and has low mobility; under cooling it
is dynamically arrested. Two differences from the kinks studied there matter to anyone carrying results
across. A kink separates two symmetry-related versions of one order and is removed only by meeting another,
while a lone column anneals by itself. And the dynamics there is a physical equation of motion, while ours is
a sampling chain whose rates are its own.

\emph{Is a relic a wall between two regions? (exploratory).} One point cuts the other way. The product is not
unique: every arrangement with two squares on every edge has zero energy, whatever its short period. A reading
of the companion paper's 2,014 saved end states that keep a defect (the project record, entry O116) finds that
about half hold a stretch wider than six edges around, and that in the 440 where a stretch six around and a
wider one are both present the two never meet directly: a defect always lies between them. A relic of that
sort is the joint between two regions of the product, the nearer relative of a domain wall, and T55's SEAM did
not test for it, since a joint need carry no long-range edge. Relics inside a region of one width we call free:
in about three in five of those end states every relic is free (774 six around throughout, 473 wider
throughout), and about one in five holds a joint. Which of the three kinds are joints, and whether
the relics followed in Secs.~\ref{sec:lifetime} and \ref{sec:cooling} were, has not been read. Regions of
differing short period joined at defects are also the majority outcome of the companion paper's isolated runs
when the reservoir is small or the curling cost larger \cite{paper1}.

\emph{Later results, and what they mean for this paper.} After the measurements above, the six-link rod fed at one
end was found to open as one front from the fed end at 30 units a store and from several places at 60 (T88,
1 November 2026; Sec.~\ref{sec:gw}), and the completion statistics of this paper were cast in the form the
gravitational-wave groups use (Sec.~\ref{sec:gw}). Neither changes a number here. The second says what a reader
from those groups could take (the completion mode, the seed density per length, the defect fraction of the release,
a front of fixed speed that runs faster where energy is stored) and what no result of this model can give them (any
quantity with a unit, and so any frequency or amplitude).

\emph{Not claimed:} anything about the real universe; a relation between the model's sweep and a cosmological
clock; a gravitational-wave signal of any kind; that the knots and double columns never anneal; that the list of kinds is complete; a law for the fall
with cooling time; or that a relic in three dimensions, or with the model's vertices treated as
interchangeable, behaves the same way. Under a rule that also charges for loops of six, tested since, the
product widens and holds flaws throughout; there the defects are the main obstacle to a clean product and not
a rare remainder, and that rule is the subject of another paper.
