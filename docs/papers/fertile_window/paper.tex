\title{The rule, the room, and which settings give a birth: how a curled torus's change into a flat one depends
on the coefficient of the local term}

\author{Emily Smith}
\email{emilysmithcreate@gmail.com}
\affiliation{Independent researcher, Charlottesville, Virginia, USA}

\date{DRAFT, 11 October 2026}

\begin{abstract}
In graph energies next to combinatorial quantum gravity (CQG), a coefficient $\lambda$ multiplies the price of
a surplus square. Only $\lambda=1$ is CQG; everything here is at $\lambda>1$, in our family. There a torus with
one direction curled lies $4(\lambda-1)$ per vertex above flat and gives way after an activated first exit. We map each ingredient of that change against $\lambda$.
Exact: the wall, the cheapest single move out, costs $32-16\lambda$ below $\lambda=4/3$ and $64-40\lambda$ above,
and counting the exits offered gives a first-exit time of 8330 to 183 sweeps for $\lambda=1.05$ to 1.35 at
coupling $g=1.5$. Measured: first exits counted by the move are on that count at $\lambda=1.05$ and 1.30. Three
registered maps of the conversion were inconclusive; a fourth, timed without a detector, met its criteria in
every cell from $\lambda=1.05$ to 1.30 at 64 to 192 vertices, with a held-out size not yet read. Read from
their wiring, 9,208 of 9,215 flat end states are the tube with its seam shifted by two, a flat torus six edges
around: the product does not widen. Sealed, the
reservoir a clean conversion needs grows at least in proportion to the release, at one size. With six and eight
links no setting opened a curled state into one flat space by its registered criterion, under an open
reproduction gate. A larger $\lambda$ gives a larger release, a shorter-lived curled state and a harder birth.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

In graph energies next to combinatorial quantum gravity (CQG) \cite{T17,KTB19,T25}, a coefficient $\lambda$
multiplies the price of a surplus square, and only $\lambda=1$ is CQG. The companion paper \cite{paper1} studied
a tube at $\lambda>1$: a $4\times L$ torus with one direction curled to a period of four edges. The tube is
stable for now: no single move out of it lowers its energy, yet lower states exist. After an activated first
exit it converts toward a flat torus and releases energy, a change that paper calls a burp. Most of that work is at one
setting, $\lambda=1.25$, and one setting could be a lucky point. This paper asks how each ingredient of the change
depends on $\lambda$: the wall (the cost of the cheapest single move out), the wait, the release, the room the
product needs when the system is sealed, and the scrap left behind. It also records, compactly, what the same
price list gives with more directions, which a companion paper treats in full \cite{paper5}.

\emph{Why a coefficient at all.} At $\lambda=1$ an edge carrying three squares costs what an edge
carrying two costs, so the flat torus, the tube and the 4-cube have the same energy and a change between them
releases nothing. For $\lambda>1$ the zero-energy states are the graphs with two squares on every edge, and each
curled direction costs a known energy. The states, the moves and the hard-core rule are those of CQG; the energy
at $\lambda\neq1$ is not its curvature action. We call the $\lambda\neq1$ energies our family, or the toy. We
have not found published work that sets the surplus price to any finite value above its curvature value: in
print, as far as we have found, are $\lambda=0$, $\lambda=1$ and, in the text of two papers \cite{KTB19,KT19}, a
version in which surplus squares are forbidden outright \cite{search}.

\emph{Four kinds of statement} are kept apart and labeled. \emph{Exact}: arithmetic or complete enumeration.
\emph{Measured}: a run registered before it ran, quoted with its registered verdict in capitals and with whose
prediction held or failed. \emph{Interpretation}: ours, unverified. \emph{The author's}: the hypothesis that
motivates the work. In that hypothesis the extra term is a curling cost, a constant whose value is whatever
reality has, and among possible settings the one observed would be the one whose cycle yields the most, counted
by her in observers who evolved. That framing is the author's and is not a claim of this paper. Readings made after a result was known are labeled
retrospective. Test numbers such as T60 refer to registrations in the project's record \cite{repo}. Every kinetic statement is about one Markov chain, the local edge switch with Metropolis
acceptance \cite{paper1}; we do not claim it is the physical time evolution of anything.

\section{The price list: what is exact}
\label{sec:exact}

A state is a simple four-regular graph on a fixed labeled bipartition of $N$ vertices obeying the hard-core rule:
no two vertices share more than two neighbors \cite{KTB19,T25}. With $S$ the number of squares (four-cycles),
$S_e$ the number on edge $e$ and $X=\sum_e(S_e-2)_+$ the surplus squares,
\begin{equation}
  H = 16(N - S) + 4\lambda X = 4 \sum_e \big[(2 - S_e) + \lambda\,(S_e - 2)_+\big],
  \label{eq:H}
\end{equation}
so a missing square costs 16 and a surplus square $4\lambda$. For $\lambda\ge1$ no state lies below zero, and for
$\lambda>1$, $H=0$ if and only if every edge carries exactly two squares \cite{paper1}.

\emph{The ladder.} Curling one direction of a torus to period four puts a third square on every edge along it.
The tube (even $L>4$) has $S=5N/4$, $X=N$ and lies $\varepsilon N$ above zero, with
$\varepsilon=4(\lambda-1)$; the 4-cube, both directions curled, lies $2\varepsilon$ per vertex above. A
conversion that ends with graph energy $H_f$ releases $Q=\varepsilon N-H_f$, the full gap only when $H_f=0$.

\emph{The wall and the count.} Every switch the chain can propose out of the tube was listed and priced. Two
kinds are cheapest: move A ($\Delta S=-2$, $\Delta X=-4$), costing $32-16\lambda$, in $3N$ ways; and move B
($\Delta S=-4$, $\Delta X=-10$), costing $64-40\lambda$, in $2N$ ways. The cheaper of the two is the wall: A below
$\lambda=4/3$, B above, both positive up to $\lambda=1.6$. A proposal is an ordered pair of edges, $4N^2$ in all,
and each switch arises from two of them; a sweep is $2N$ attempts. So a sweep offers A three times and B twice at
every size. Weighting each offer by its acceptance gives the two-channel first-exit time at coupling $g$, the
count:
\begin{equation}
  \tau_{AB}(\lambda,g) = \Big[\,3\,e^{-(32 - 16\lambda)/g} + 2\,e^{-(64 - 40\lambda)/g}\Big]^{-1}
  \label{eq:tau}
\end{equation}
(Table~\ref{tab:exact}). Dearer exits add at most 2\% to the rate at the $g=1.5$ conditions of the maps
\cite{paper1}. The count is the time to the first exit, not every clock of the conversion, and the wall is a first-exit cost, not a proven saddle of a
complete conversion path. That $\tau_{AB}$ does not depend on $N$ is a property of the proposal, which draws its
two edges from the whole graph. Durations in the maps below are in these chain sweeps, as in the earlier maps,
because the fourth map repeats them and the first exit's rate per chain sweep is the same at every size. Where
local rates are compared across sizes (the cooling times of Sec.~\ref{sec:scrap}) we use a fair clock, on
which one fair sweep is $N/96$ chain sweeps and every local pair of edges is offered equally often at every size.

\emph{The scrap's share.} The commonest relic of the cold sealed boxes of Ref.~\cite{paper1} is one column of
the tube left curled: a closed loop of four vertices, costing $24\lambda-16$ above flat. It does not vanish as
$\lambda\to1$, while the gap does. Its share of the gap is $(24\lambda-16)/[4(\lambda-1)N]$: 22\% at
$\lambda=1.25$ and 72\% at 1.05 for $N=64$ (Table~\ref{tab:exact}), 7\% and 24\% for $N=192$. Below
$\lambda=1+8/(4N-24)$, which is 1.035 at $N=64$, one such column costs more than the whole gap.

\section{The wait}
\label{sec:wait}

\emph{Measured, counted by the move.} T22 counted every accepted move in forty runs per cell
($\lambda=1.05,1.10,1.25$; $N=64,96$; $g=1.5$). All 240 first exits were observed, and their means are consistent
with Eq.~(\ref{eq:tau}) within model-conditional 95\% intervals \cite{paper1}. (At $N=96$, $\lambda=1.05$ the mean
over all forty is 9302 sweeps; over the 39 that went on to convert, 7485.) Its registered verdict at
$\lambda=1.05$ was FALL-BACKS: exits return to the tube, and the first exit is not late. Our predicted verdict
held and our predicted numbers did not: we expected 70 to 87\% of exits to go through at $\lambda=1.05$ and nearly
all at 1.25. The author had given no prediction. For the 239 runs that reach a quarter conversion, the reciprocal
of the mean number of exits per run is 0.56 to 0.68, with no trend in $\lambda$. This is a diagnostic, not a
derived transmission coefficient. Returns are a feature of the chain at every $\lambda$ tested, not a
near-$\lambda=1$ effect.

T59 counted fresh first exits by the move at the two ends of the window. At $\lambda=1.05$
($\tau_{AB}=8329.7$ sweeps), 2000 runs at $N=64$ and 1000 at $N=96$ have means $0.992\pm0.023$ and
$0.946\pm0.031$ of $\tau_{AB}$, with one first exit beyond $8\tau_{AB}$ where 1.0 is expected: ON THE COUNT at
both sizes. At $\lambda=1.30$, $N=64$ ($\tau_{AB}=419.0$), 4000 runs have mean $1.005\pm0.016$. Read by a look
every five sweeps, the same runs give a mean later by $0.096\pm0.007\,\tau_{AB}$, and 94\% of that comes from
first exits that return before the look, about one first exit in thirteen: HIDDEN EXITS. The author's two
predictions (chance; exits hidden between two looks) held, as did ours. Described, not scored: a mean wait read
by five-sweep looks sits about 8 to 10\% above the count for this reason alone, and the look leaves the wait's
CV unchanged (0.993 against 0.990 by the move). The same registration replayed, from
their seeds, the long waits recorded earlier at $\lambda=1.05$. A detected wait of 78,205 sweeps read as the tube
at every look (TRUE WAIT). Seven long first exits were offered moves A and B at 2.94 to 3.03 and 1.94 to 2.04 per
sweep throughout (OFFERED AS COUNTED). We read those long waits as chance.

\emph{Exact, behind that reading.} At the sizes enumerated ($N=48$ to 288, at $\lambda=1.25$) the graphs a
waiting tube reaches without changing its squares form three classes with one census of moves \cite{paper1}. The
chance of leaving is then the same at every attempt, so the first exit is exactly memoryless with the census's
mean: 8326 and 417.5 sweeps at $\lambda=1.05$ and 1.30, against $\tau_{AB}$'s 8329.7 and 419.0. This is our
arithmetic on a complete enumeration, unreviewed.

\emph{Interpretation (ours, unverified).} Across the window, what $\lambda$ changes about the first exit is the
two exponents of Eq.~(\ref{eq:tau}), and nothing else we have measured.

\section{The conversion across the window}
\label{sec:window}

\emph{Protocol and the registered bar.} The tube at $g=1.5$, $N=64,96,144,192$, is followed in blocks of five
sweeps until 98\% converted or 100,000 sweeps, and the final graph is saved. Let $d(v)$ be the number of pairs
of edges at $v$ that close no square: 1 on the tube (curled-like), 2 on the flat torus (flat-like). It is a
square count, not a dimension. Three criteria are registered per cell $(\lambda,N)$: (a) the coefficient of
variation (CV) of the wait lies in a stated band, as a memoryless wait's would; (b) at least 80\% of vertices
have the square-count signature $d\in\{1,2\}$ at half conversion; (c) the largest connected piece of flat-like
vertices holds at least 70\% of them at half conversion. A cell meeting all three is called sharp. The window
verdict asks for a sharp change at every $\lambda$ from 1.05 to 1.30, at every size that passes its gates;
$\lambda=1.35$ is scored separately, as the edge. These are counting and connectivity statistics. They do not certify two geometric
orders, a unique seed or a single moving front.

\emph{Where the tube persists.} A tube persists in a cell when more than half its runs are under a quarter
converted at sweep 200. In T8 that holds at every size from $\lambda=1.05$ to 1.35 and fails at 1.40 and 1.45;
our registered prediction for this edge, between 1.3 and 1.4, held, and the author had made none.
(In T23, $\lambda=1.35$ sat on the line at $N=64$ and 96.) The persistence edge is operational, not an energetic
spinodal: the walls there are still 8 to 10 units, and move B costs energy up to $\lambda=1.6$.
Equation~(\ref{eq:tau}) gives a mean of 200 sweeps near $\lambda=1.345$ but does not derive the edge
\cite{paper1}.

\emph{Three maps, each inconclusive.} T8 (thirty runs per cell, $\lambda=1.05$ to 1.45, its $\lambda=1.25$ cells taken from an earlier registered
run) and T23 and T24 (120
runs per cell, $\lambda=1.05$ to 1.35, fresh seeds) each returned INCONCLUSIVE by the letter of its own rules,
for a different reason. T8's CV band was too tight for thirty waits and failed in nine of 24 persistent cells,
and its endpoint-energy criterion failed at $\lambda=1.35$. T23 sized the band to the sample; its energy
criterion, which compared a window average with a saved endpoint, then failed at every size at $\lambda=1.30$.
T24 replaced that energy criterion with a check that each saved graph is valid, which held in all 28 cells; its
CV criterion failed in four,
$(N,\lambda)=(64,1.25)$, $(64,1.30)$, $(192,1.30)$ and $(144,1.35)$. In all three maps criteria (b) and (c)
held in every cell through $\lambda=1.30$. The author's prediction for T23 and T24, SHARP ACROSS THE WINDOW, was
neither confirmed nor refuted by them.

\emph{The detector.} The wait in those maps was a detector's. It rests for 200 sweeps, sets a threshold from the
run's own spread of $S/N$ in that window, and then looks every five sweeps. A retrospective, exploratory reading
of the saved rows (no new run, no verdict changed) shows that the detector's clock, not the chain, produced most
of the long waits. The two waits that failed two of T24's cells, 24 and 76 times $\tau_{AB}$ at $N=64$, were not
waits: both tubes were three quarters or more converted when the window ended, and then rested on defective
sheets until the lowered threshold was crossed. A registered tail test (T38; 10,000 runs at $\lambda=1.25$ and
1.30) had returned TWO POPULATIONS, as the author's inferred prediction and ours had it; that verdict stands as
scored. Read the same way, all 190 of its runs with no
detector crossing had converted inside the window, and most of its fastest detected waits were exits inside the
window. A
registered replay of one T38 cell from its seeds (T58) returned every saved row unchanged. Its verdict on the
three long waits left was MIXED, and neither registered prediction for them held (the author's, a hidden second
curl; ours, a lowered threshold in all three). Timed with no detector, that cell's 4000 first exits have mean
$1.071\pm0.017\,\tau_{AB}$ by five-sweep looks, with one beyond $10\tau_{AB}$ where 0.18 is expected. One long
wait remains, of a length chance allows about one time in six \cite{paper1}.

\section{The product}
\label{sec:product}

\emph{A retrospective reading of saved final graphs: exploratory; each graph's width is exact} \cite{repo}. A run
that ends at zero energy ends on a flat torus: every edge on exactly two squares. That is a count at each edge, and
a torus six edges around already satisfies it; Ref.~\cite{paper1} says that growth of both periods had not been
measured. We measured it. The \emph{width} of a flat torus is the length of its shortest loop that cannot be shrunk
to a point. It can be read exactly from the number of vertices within $r$ steps of a vertex, which equals the
endless flat sheet's $2r^2+2r+1$ until $2r$ reaches the width. The tube's width is 4, and a flat torus needs at
least 6.

Of the saved end states of the four maps ($N=64$ to 192, $\lambda=1.05$ to 1.45), 9,215 are exactly flat, and 9,208
of them have width 6: every one at $N=144$ and 192, and all but seven at 64 and 96 (six of width 8, one of width
12). Sampled against a graph built for the purpose, 41 of 41 at $N=64$ and 96 are isomorphic to the tube with its
seam shifted by two: four vertices to a ring as before, but a walk around the ring arrives two steps along the tube,
so no four steps close and the shortest way around is six. It has eight vertices at each distance, as the tube has.

So in these maps a birth is the birth of a flat torus six around, at every $\lambda$ and every size, and its width
does not grow with $N$. The wall, the wait, the release and every registered verdict above are unaffected: they are
energies and counts, and the energy cannot tell a flat torus six around from a wide one. What is not shown, here or
in Ref.~\cite{paper1}, is a small direction becoming large. \emph{Interpretation (ours, unverified):} every flat
torus has zero energy, and at these couplings a flat torus accepts almost no move (leaving it costs 32), so the
chain stops at the first flat state it reaches, and the nearest to the tube is the tube twisted by two. Where
released energy stays in place the product can be wider: in a sealed control with one store per vertex (T48,
$\lambda=1.25$), 6 of the 15 tubes that converted are wider by the same kind of reading, and 9 are not. What makes a
flat torus widen is the next thing to measure.

\section{The room}
\label{sec:room}

Sealed, the released energy has only a reservoir to go to: $C$ stores of nonnegative energy, each move paid from
or into one store chosen uniformly, so that graph plus stores conserve energy \cite{paper1}. We call $C$ the
room. It is a number of stores, not a heat capacity in temperature units, and conservation alone does not
establish equilibrium. A conversion is called a birth here when its end state meets the sheet criterion of
Ref.~\cite{paper1}, a count ($f\ge0.9$ with $f=\overline d-1$, at least 90\% of vertices at $d=2$, under 5\% at
$d=0$ or $d\ge3$), not a topology test.

\emph{Measured at $\lambda=1.25$} (T9, registered verdict BONFIRE WITH A THRESHOLD, as we had predicted;
$N=64,96,192$): for
$C\le N/16$ the converted region melts into the random phase in 17 to 20 of 20 runs, and for $C\ge N/2$ the runs
are births in 18 to 20 of 20, the crossover lying between $N/4$ and $N/2$ at each size \cite{paper1}.

\emph{Measured against $\lambda$} (T18). One seed is planted in a sealed $24\times4$ tube ($N=96$) with every
store empty, at ten values of $C$ from $N/32$ to $2N$, twenty runs each, at $\lambda=1.10$, 1.25 and 1.40. The
smallest room giving a majority of births is $C^*=12$, 36 and 72 stores. The registered reading is the ratio
$R=C^*(1.40)/C^*(1.10)=6$: PROPORTIONAL (the rule was $R\ge2.5$). Our prediction held on the rule and undershot
the size, since we expected $R\approx4$; the author had given no prediction. Per unit of release,
$C^*/(\varepsilon N)=0.31$, 0.375 and 0.47, but the grid of $C$ steps by factors of 4/3 to 2 and cannot tell
faster than proportional from proportional. What the data show is a room that grows with $\lambda$ at least as
fast as the release. At $\lambda=1.40$ even the largest reservoir, $C=2N$, gave a birth in only 16 of 20 runs.
This is one size. The melting coupling of the new sheet at $\lambda=1.10$ and 1.40 was assumed close to that at
1.25, not measured; that it falls as $\lambda$ rises is our guess from $R=6$, unverified.

\emph{Melting moves with size (unfinished).} Two things on the record limit how far a statement at a fixed warm
coupling carries. First, a retrospective reading of saved wiring (T37; $\lambda=1.25$; tubes long enough to
start in several places; its registered verdict, MANY SEEDS, MANY SCRAPS, stands by the letter): at $g=1.5$, tubes of $4\times64$ to $4\times256$ vertices end with 94 to 97\% of
vertices flat-like, while at $4\times1024$ only 28\% are flat-like, 55\% have opened past flat ($d>2$, the
direction of the random phase), and the graphs took energy from the bath. At $g=1.25$ the same length ends 95\%
flat-like. Whether the long warm runs had equilibrated is not known. Second, an early exploratory scan (one
replica, under a variant of the energy that forbids surplus squares) put the coupling at which $S/N=0.5$ at
$g=7.64$, 5.51, 4.34 and 3.51 for $N=100$, 196, 400 and 900; repeating it at $\lambda=1$ with several replicas
and errors is not finished. Both point to a larger network melting at a lower coupling. A published entropy
argument for a related graph model, in which the disordered phase's entropy grows as $N\ln N$
\cite{K08,CP12}, points the same way; applying it to this energy is ours, unverified, and it is a target to
test against, not support. The maps of Sec.~\ref{sec:window} are at $N\le192$ and $g=1.5$, and carry this
caveat.

\section{The scrap along the window}
\label{sec:scrap}

At fixed coupling the share of runs that do not end on a zero-energy graph rises with $\lambda$
(Table~\ref{tab:t60}). In T8 every run at $\lambda=1.05$ ended at zero energy, and all 120 saved endpoints are
certified tori \cite{paper1}; near the edge about half do not. The endpoints that hold energy rest on one curled
column or on combinations of small defects.

After cooling, the scrap has been read only at $\lambda=1.25$ \cite{paper2}. In 1024-vertex sheets that opened
from several starting places, the columns heal as the cooling slows, falling from 2.1 to 0.1 per sheet between
cooling times of 10,000 and 300,000 fair sweeps (T51, registered verdict CLIFF; the author's prediction and ours,
a gentle fall, both failed). The counts of two other kinds of piece do not change over that range. Read exactly
from their wiring (T55, registered verdict KNOT; the author's prediction, a seam between patches, failed), those
pieces are stuck arrangements of their own, so the scrap is three kinds of knot of fixed energy: the column
(14 units), an eight-vertex knot of two kinds of vertex (20 to 30) and a double column (8). The exact share of
Sec.~\ref{sec:exact} is the single column's. T51 and T55 are at one $\lambda$; how the three kinds divide the
scrap at other $\lambda$ has not been read.

\section{The same price list with more directions}
\label{sec:directions}

In the author's hypothesis the space directions are tied: they curl and open together. That makes the number of
directions a second axis of the map. With $2D$ links per vertex, $D=3$ or 4, the same price list (16 per
missing square, $4\lambda$ per surplus square, an edge full at $2D-2$ squares) gives a ladder that stays
additive: each curled direction costs $\varepsilon$ per vertex. Every rung's cheapest single move was priced by
listing switches (Table~\ref{tab:dmap}).

\emph{Exact.} (i) Each rung has its own wall, and the walls differ from rung to rung at every $\lambda>1$,
so nothing in this energy ties the directions together. (ii) The ratio of flat
space's wall to the last rung's wall at $\lambda=1.25$ is 2.7, 1.8 and 1.6 with two, three and four directions.
Read as a margin between a bath that pays the last rung and one that damages flat space (our reading), more
directions leave less, not more; a rule of ours built on this ratio, a window wherever it is at least 2, failed
in T39. (iii) A state curled in every direction at every vertex is connected only up to $4^D$ vertices, where it
is the hypercube, so a fully curled state larger than one cube is a gas of separate pieces. This last applies a
published theorem \cite{Mul79} whose proof we have not read.

\emph{Measured.} By the registered verdicts, which rest on counts of flat-like vertices and their largest
connected piece, no setting with six or eight links opened a curled state into one flat space. The price of the first move out of the two-curled six-link torus is fixed with size (T32; the
author's prediction and ours held). Given that push at $\lambda=1.25$, its first curled direction opened fully
in 28 of 84 runs and its second never in a cold reservoir (T30; both predictions failed). The one-curled six-link torus, the direct counterpart of
Sec.~\ref{sec:room}, never opened at any reservoir from $N/8$ to $4N$ (T41), so the room it would need could not
be measured. With eight links a push equal to the first move stalled after that move (T33), and pushes up to
eight times it moved no run off its starting rung by the registered measure (T40). The author's predictions,
some of them inferred from her stated positions, failed in T30, T39 and T41 and were not reached in T33 and T40,
where nothing opened. Ours were right that nothing opens all and wrong in particulars: the windows we expected
at larger $\lambda$ did not appear (T39), and our estimate of the push that starts four directions was too low.

Three results cut the other way and are reported as prominently. In a gas of 6-cubes at $\lambda=1.10$, two of
three directions opened in 21 of 24 runs, in many small flat-like patches and never one space (T30-gas). With
released energy kept at the vertex that releases it (T48), the two-curled torus at $\lambda=1.25$ advanced one
rung and stopped, as the author and we had predicted (ADVANCES ONLY). At $\lambda=1.40$ that prediction failed:
the setting's registered verdict is DAMAGED, with damage in 7 of 16 runs, and in 5 of the 16 the torus reached a
connected flat-like region holding at least half its vertices. And in a warm bath at fixed coupling the same torus returned ADVANCES ONLY at every length and both $\lambda$ (T53): its
first direction opens and it rests there. Its second opened only in a minority of runs, at the two shorter lengths and
$\lambda=1.40$, in baths that then damaged the space. The author's prediction of a window failed, and her
prediction of several starting places held. Ours held at $\lambda=1.25$ and failed at 1.40, where we had
expected a window at the shortest length.

The verdict words OPENS and ONE SPACE are counts too. By the distance profiles of their saved graphs, about a
third of the six-link runs that met the count's criterion for an opening are the starting torus glued together
differently, not a wider space \cite{paper5}.

\emph{Interpretation (ours, unverified).} The bath that crosses the last wall is close to the bath that takes
flat space apart, and a single move does not price the barrier of a growing patch in three directions.

\emph{With a tie.} A term that ties the directions is an ingredient the author has added, a further step from
CQG. The tied runs read so far returned NOT ALL (T44 to T46), as we had predicted in outline; the author's
prediction, that one push opens all, failed. Under the tie the author chose afterwards, in which nothing is
released until a vertex's last direction opens, the single-move wall of the one-curled torus falls from 39.2 at
$\lambda=1.05$ to 4.0 at 1.25 while flat space's stays 64 (exact). Its first registered test held that torus and
the two-curled one in a warm bath (T56). No cell has an OPENS majority: the one-curled torus STAYS at
$\lambda=1.10$ to 1.20 and ADVANCES ONLY at 1.25, the two-curled one STAYS, and an untied control shows NO
DIFFERENCE. The author's prediction, that it opens into one flat space, failed, and so did ours, a window at 1.15
and 1.20. The tie as written charges a damaged vertex nothing, and the tied torus broke where the untied one was
untouched. The test that prices the barrier of a growing patch has no verdict yet. Reference~\cite{paper5} treats
both.

\emph{The caveat on every six- and eight-link statement.} These are statements about an energy we derived from
the published definitions and a kernel validated on its own terms (at four links it reproduces the four-link
chain draw for draw, and its energies and moves are checked by brute force). The published six-link curve
\cite{T22} has not been reproduced: the gate failed and stays open. A factor of two in the coupling is accounted
for by the published equation; whether the remaining difference is the published figure's protocol or its graph
ensemble is not settled. There is no published eight-link curve.

\section{Discussion}

\emph{The trade-off.} In two directions the map has one shape. As $\lambda$ rises from 1.05 to 1.35 at $g=1.5$,
the gap per vertex, the most a conversion can release, grows linearly (0.2 to 1.4), the wall falls (15.2 to
10), the counted wait falls from
8330 to 183 sweeps, the largest flat-like component at half conversion shrinks, fewer runs end at zero energy,
and a sealed conversion needs at least proportionally more room. As $\lambda$ falls toward 1 everything the
change produces shrinks to nothing and the tube lasts longer. No setting at $g=1.5$ gives a large release, a
long-lived tube and a product that needs little room.

\emph{Which settings give a birth.} Within what was run: in a bath at $g=1.5$, the tube at $\lambda=1.05$ to 1.30
and 64 to 192 vertices converts with the fourth map's count criteria met in every cell, and 82 to 120 of 120 runs
per cell end on a zero-energy graph, a flat torus six edges around (Sec.~\ref{sec:product}). Sealed, at 96 vertices, a majority of runs are births when the room is at
least 12, 36 and 72 stores at $\lambda=1.10$, 1.25 and 1.40. Outside that: break-up from $\lambda=1.35$ and no persistence from 1.40; in long warm tubes, melting;
with six and eight links, no setting with a registered verdict of one flat space.

\emph{The author's framing.} In the author's hypothesis this trade-off is what a selection among settings would
act on. The toy can map the ingredients; it cannot count what a cycle yields, and this paper claims only the
map.

\emph{What this cannot show.} Anything at exactly $\lambda=1$, where nothing is released. Anything in the maps at
couplings other than $g=1.5$, or at sizes above 192 until the held-out size is read. The room at any size but 96.
Anything with interchangeable vertices: all runs treat vertices as named, and weighting each unlabeled class
once is a different ensemble, with acceptance $\min\{1,[A(G')/A(G)]\,e^{-\Delta H/g}\}$ for symmetry counts $A$.
It leaves every energy here unchanged and changes the rates; a first-move suppression by a factor $cN$
corresponds to an increment $g\ln(cN)$ in an effective barrier, not to a derived lifetime. And anything about
reality beyond whether a mechanism is possible in a class of toys.
