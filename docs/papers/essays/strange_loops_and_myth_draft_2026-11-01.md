# The Loop You Already Know

*Working title. Alternatives: "Myth as a Priming Coat"; "Every American Child Knows a Strange Loop".*

**Abstract.** Self-reference is the hardest shape in physics to explain to a general reader, and it sits at the
center of the measurement problem, the anthropic arguments, the cosmological measure problem and Wheeler's
participatory universe. Yet one self-referential loop is grasped by most American children before they can read:
belief makes good, and good is what the belief is about. I argue that a culture's foundational myths install one such
loop early, that the loop is understood long before it can be stated, and that this early priming is an unused
resource for public understanding of loop-shaped physics. I raise, and partly test, the question of whether cultures
that install the loop less firmly produce less work about loops; a bounded bibliometric look does not support that
guess, and I say what it cannot show. I close with the loop in my own hypothesis about where spacetime comes from,
stated as a hypothesis.

## 1. A shape children learn first

Ask a physicist to explain why the observer is a problem in quantum mechanics, and watch what happens to the
sentences. They get longer. The physicist starts drawing. Somewhere in the second minute the word "itself" appears,
and from then on the listener is either with them or lost, and usually lost. The thing being described is a loop:
a system whose description includes the describer, a rule that applies to the rule, a measurement that is part of
what it measures. Loops are hard. They are hard for graduate students. Hofstadter wrote two long books to get one
loop across, and he is the best explainer the subject has had.

Now ask a nine-year-old in Virginia what it means to be saved. The answer comes without drawing. God made you; you
fall short; someone who was God took the punishment for the falling short; if you believe that, you are made good,
and being made good is what lets you believe it properly. The child does not say the last clause. The child does not
need to. The loop is installed, working, and invisible, in the way a native speaker's grammar is installed and
invisible. The child can run it forward and backward, can feel when a story breaks it, can tell you that someone who
is "good" only to be saved has missed the point, which is exactly the self-referential subtlety that makes the
structure a loop rather than a ladder.

I am a software engineer, not a theologian or a physicist, and I am not going to argue about whether the loop is
true. I am going to argue about what it does to the mind it is installed in. My claim is this: the foundational myths
of a culture put one self-referential loop into almost every child, early, as a shared and working object; that this
loop is a piece of cognitive equipment, not only a belief; and that the people who want the public to understand
loop-shaped physics have been trying to build this equipment from scratch in adults when most of their audience
already owns a copy.

## 2. What a myth does

By myth I mean a story a culture tells so often, and so early, that its structure becomes a way of seeing rather
than a thing seen. Good and evil are the clearest example. Nobody is taught good and evil as a definition. They are
given a hundred stories in which someone is one or the other, and the categories assemble themselves. By the time a
child can argue about whether a character is really good, the words have become instruments the child thinks with.
The same is true of fairness, of promise and betrayal, of sacrifice. These are not easy ideas. Philosophers have
spent careers failing to define them. Children handle them fluently at six, because the stories came first and the
definitions were never needed.

Myth, in this sense, is a universal language. It is what lets a complicated idea become mainstream without anyone
having to understand it the way a specialist does. A story is a compressed, runnable model. You cannot hand a child
a theory of moral responsibility, but you can hand them the story of a man who gave something up for someone who
did not deserve it, and the theory arrives inside the story, pre-installed, and runs.

Some structures are harder than good and evil, and self-reference is the hardest I know. Most stories are ladders:
this happened, then that, because of this. A self-referential story is one where the ending reaches back and changes
what the beginning meant, or where the teller is inside the tale. Children's literature mostly avoids this, and when
it uses it (the dream that was real, the book that is about itself) it is for a shiver, not for understanding.

Religious mythology does not avoid it. It is built out of it.

## 3. Key loops in religious mythology, and the physics each one points at

I will take four loops, each from a tradition I know well enough to describe without embarrassment, and beside each
I will put the piece of physics that has the same shape. The physics summaries are from general reading, and a
physicist should check them before anyone relies on them. The pairings are mine.

**The loop of belief and grace.** In the Christian story, you are made good by believing in an act of sacrifice,
and the act of sacrifice is what makes you able to believe. Faith produces the change; the change produces the
faith. Protestant theology spent centuries on the order of operations here, because there is no order of operations:
it is a fixed point, a state that is its own cause. The physics with this shape is the observer in the measurement
problem. In the standard account, a measurement produces a definite outcome, but a measuring device is itself a
physical system that should be described by the same quantum rules, which do not produce definite outcomes. The
observer is inside the thing being observed. Everett's answer, that the observer is simply another part of the
wavefunction and the definiteness is relative to the observer's own state, is a fixed point in the same sense: the
observer's state is defined by the measurement that the observer's state defines. People find this baffling. They
find grace baffling too, in the same place, for the same reason, and most of them already live inside it.

**The loop of the covenant.** In the Hebrew Bible, God chooses a people, and the people's story is the record of
having been chosen, and the record is what makes them a people. Identity and election are each other's cause. The
law is given by the God the law describes. The physics with this shape is Wheeler's self-excited circuit. Wheeler
drew a U with an eye at one end looking back at the other: the universe, through observers it has produced, brings
about its own past. He called it the participatory universe. Whether or not the idea is right, and most physicists
treat it as a provocation rather than a theory, its shape is the covenant's shape: a thing brought into existence by
the witness it brings into existence.

**The loop of karma and rebirth.** In Hindu and Buddhist traditions, your actions make the conditions of your next
life, and those conditions are what you act within; the self that is reborn is both the author and the product of
the chain, and the final move, in the Buddhist version, is to see that the self doing the seeing was never a fixed
thing. The physics with this shape is the anthropic and observer-measure argument. Why are the constants of nature
such that observers exist? One answer is that observers can only find themselves where observers can exist, so the
population of observers selects, from among possible worlds, the ones where the question gets asked. The observer is
both the thing explained and the thing doing the selecting. Cosmologists who try to make this quantitative run into
the measure problem: in an infinite collection of universes, the relative numbers of observers depend on how you
count, and the counting method is itself a choice an observer makes. That is karma's loop in the vocabulary of
probability.

**The loop of the eternal return.** In several traditions, Norse, Stoic, Hindu, the world ends and begins again,
and the beginning is caused by the ending, and the cycle has no first turn. The physics with this shape is any
cyclic cosmology, and, in a more exact way, Hofstadter's strange loop itself: a hierarchy of levels in which moving
up through the levels brings you back to where you started. Hofstadter's examples are Escher's staircase, Bach's
endlessly rising canon, Gödel's sentence that talks about itself by encoding its own arithmetic, and, in his second
book, the "I" of a mind, which he argues is a strange loop the brain tells itself.

These pairings are not meant to say that the myths anticipated the physics. They did not. They are meant to say that
the *shape* was in circulation, shared, and handled fluently by ordinary people, for thousands of years before the
physics needed it. When a physicist reaches for a loop and finds that the audience cannot hold it, the audience is
holding one already. It is just labeled differently.

## 4. My own loop, stated as a hypothesis

I will put my cards on the table, because the whole argument of this essay is that loops are easier to see when
someone shows you theirs.

I have a hobby research programme, run with AI assistants on toy models, built around a hypothesis I hold and have not
shown. Our spacetime, in this hypothesis, is one stable phase of something deeper; it came about as a sharp change,
with a bounded release of energy, out of a specific earlier arrangement that was stable for the time being but not
the most stable available; what did not convert remains as a leftover that accounts for the rest of the books; and
the whole thing is one turn of a loop, in which matter gathers into black holes, black holes re-curl what space
uncurled, and the re-curled stuff is the seed of the next turn. The loop is strange in Hofstadter's sense because the
measure of what counts as a good turn, the thing that would explain why we see this reality rather than another, is
the observers the turn produces, and the observers are the ones doing the measuring. I have toy results about the
first step (a specific arrangement in a published graph model does open sharply, from a seed, with an exact release)
and a long list of pre-registered predictions that did not hold, each recorded. None of it is evidence for the loop.
It is a hypothesis with a stated way to be wrong, and I say so wherever I write about it.

What I noticed, writing it up for a general audience, is that the loop was the easy part to explain. People got it
immediately. They had trouble with the graph model, with the energy function, with what a torus is, but "the end of
the turn is the beginning of the next turn, and what counts is who is there to see it" landed without effort. I
did not understand why until I thought about where my readers, mostly Americans, had learned to hold a loop.

## 5. A question I could partly test, and what the look found

If the argument above is right, a culture that installs a self-referential loop early and firmly might be more at
home with loop-shaped ideas, and might produce more of them. Europe and China are, on the standard surveys, much
less religious than the United States. The Pew Research Center's 2018 comparison has 53 percent of Americans saying
religion is very important to them, against 3 percent in China and an average of 23 percent across European
countries. So one could ask: do Europe and China produce fewer publications about self-reference?

I asked an assistant to take a bounded look, with the queries written down so that anyone can rerun them. The
database was OpenAlex, an open index of about 329 million scholarly works. The search was for works whose title or
abstract contains "self-reference", "self-referential" or "strange loop". It found 27,004. Grouped by the countries
of the authors and divided by each country's total output, the United States has about 12 such works per 100,000;
the United Kingdom 16; Germany 15; the Netherlands 15; Switzerland 17; Austria 19; China about 10; Japan about 2.
Among countries with at least a hundred hits, the United States is below every one of the fifteen with the highest
normalized share, nearly all of them in northern and central Europe, plus Israel, Hong Kong and Singapore.

So the look does not support the guess. The less religious research cultures of Europe publish, by this measure, a
little more about self-reference per paper than the United States does, not less.

Here is what the look cannot show, and it is most of what matters. The search terms are English, and 93 percent of
the hits are in English; China's and Japan's low numbers are at least partly an artifact of that. The term is not the
idea: "self-referential" in a title catches narcissism questionnaires, memory experiments and laser metrology, and the
field breakdown has the arts and humanities and psychology at the top, not mathematics or physics. Nothing about
religiosity is in the data at all; I have set a bibliometric number beside a survey number and noted that they do not
move together, which is suggestive and no more. A real study would need a corpus coded by concept rather than by
word, searches in several languages, normalization by field, and a religiosity measure matched to the authors'
upbringing rather than today's national average. I have written none of that, and the essay would be dishonest if it
pretended the look was more than it is.

The assistant also tabulated sixteen people most associated with self-reference in logic, mathematics, biology,
physics and the arts, from Gödel, Turing, Tarski and Russell through Smullyan, Hofstadter, Escher, Lawvere,
Spencer-Brown, Luhmann, Maturana, Varela, Bateson, Barwise, Etchemendy and Wheeler, with birthplace, childhood home,
places of work and religious upbringing where an encyclopedia page states it. Seven were born in the United States,
four in Britain, two in Chile, and one each in Austria-Hungary, Poland, the Netherlands and Germany; nearly all
worked in the United States or Britain, which is where the century's logic departments were. Religious upbringing is
stated for only five, and the five cover the range: a Lutheran theist, a Jewish family that converted to Catholicism
and raised an atheist, a Presbyterian grandmother overriding an agnostic will, a family atheist for generations, a
Jewish family and a Taoist. Five is not a sample. It is a reminder that the question is answerable only with real
biographies and a defined list, and that a page's silence about religion is not evidence of a secular home.

I include the negative result because the argument of this essay does not need the bibliometric claim to be true,
and it is better to find that out now. My claim is about *public* understanding, about what almost every child in a
culture has installed, and not about what its professional logicians publish. Professional logicians, on the
evidence of the table, come from every kind of home and build their loops from the mathematics. The question whether
the general public of a loop-primed culture takes to loop-shaped physics more readily is a different question, and it
is the one worth asking.

## 6. Using the priming

Suppose the priming is real: that most Americans, and most people in any culture with a grace-shaped or
covenant-shaped or karma-shaped myth at its center, carry a working self-referential loop from childhood. What
follows for the people whose job is to explain loop-shaped physics?

First, stop building the equipment from scratch. The standard explanation of the measurement problem starts with a
cat, a box, and a wavefunction, and asks the listener to construct the idea of a system that includes its own
observer from nothing. The listener already has one. Say: you know how, in the story, believing is what makes you
able to believe? The observer in quantum mechanics is in that position. Then do the cat. The loop goes in before the
physics, and the physics has something to hang on.

Second, use the discomfort. People who live inside the grace loop know that it is uncomfortable to look at directly,
that it sounds like cheating when you say it plainly, and that it is nonetheless the thing they actually hold. That is
exactly the feeling a physicist has about the participatory universe or the anthropic argument: it sounds like
cheating, it is not quite, and the field cannot leave it alone. Telling the listener that the physics has the same
uncomfortable shape as a loop they already carry is honest, and it is a relief.

Third, be clear about what the analogy is not. A shared shape is not shared truth. The Christian loop being
installed in a child says nothing about whether grace exists; Wheeler's U says nothing about whether the universe is
participatory. The analogy is a scaffold, and the physics still has to stand on its own measurements. In my own
programme the rule is that every sentence says which kind of statement it is: exact, measured, interpretation, or the
hypothesis itself. The myth-to-physics bridge is interpretation, every time, and should be labeled.

Fourth, expect the bridge to run both ways, and be careful. A culture primed on one loop will find the physics loop
that matches it most natural, and may find it too natural: an American audience may accept the observer-selects-the
world argument more easily than it should, because grace taught them that belief changes what is real. Priming is a
resource and a bias in the same object. A good explainer uses the first and names the second.

## 7. What this essay cannot show

I have claimed that children grasp the grace loop before they can state it. I know this from watching children and
from having been one, and from nothing else. It is an observation and a plausible one; it is not a measurement, and
I have not found or read a developmental study that tests it. I have claimed that this early loop is a piece of
cognitive equipment reusable for physics. That is a proposal for how to teach, and the test of it is whether
explanations built on it work better, which nobody has run. I have asked whether loop-primed cultures publish more
about loops, and the one bounded look I could make says no, with confounds large enough that the answer could be
either. And I have described my own loop-shaped hypothesis about spacetime, which is a hypothesis, with a long record
of toy predictions that did not hold, and no claim to be more.

What I am confident of is smaller and, I think, still worth saying. The hardest shape in foundational physics is
already in circulation as a story, told to children, in every major religious culture on earth. Physicists who want
to be understood have been treating their audiences as blank slates on the one point where the slate is already
written on. The writing is in another vocabulary, and it may carry assumptions the physics does not want. But it is
there, it is deep, and it is free.
