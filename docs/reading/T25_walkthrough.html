The six claims this is measured against

  1. Spacetime is a stable phase of something deeper, called X.
  2. Any candidate for X must end up reproducing relativity and quantum mechanics.
  3. X and spacetime are two arrangements of one system with one relational quantity.
  4. A phase change in X produced our phase. It was first order, releasing a bounded lump of energy that became matter and radiation.
  5. What did not convert remained, as hotter X or as another arrangement, X2.
  6. An energy-like quantity is conserved across the change.

4.1

How geometry condenses out of a tangle

IV.1 · Cycle condensation

The network is rewarded for containing short loops. Triangles pay best, then squares, then pentagons, in the ratio 9/8 : 1 : 5/8. But the no-sharing rule means a triangle can only sit beside a pentagon, and a triangle plus a pentagon earns 9/8 + 5/8, which is less than the 2 you get from two squares. So squares win, and triangles and pentagons survive only as scattered defects.

Squares then pile onto a single street until it carries two, one on each side. Past that the local term takes back exactly what a further square earns, so the network starts filling a different street instead. Keep going and every street ends up with two squares. In two dimensions that is a flat torus.

Then comes the sentence this whole project turns on:

If only the global term is retained, the model undergoes a first-order phase transition in which the graph decomposes into isolated, weakly interacting hypercubic complexes. If the full Hamiltonian is used, instead the model undergoes a continuous phase transition from a random graph phase to a geometric phase.

Figure 3 is the evidence: squares per point against the coupling, cooled and heated, rising from 0.126 in the random phase to 1 for a torus. Its caption argues the transition is continuous because the two directions show no hysteresis.

How we compare

This is the section we have spent the project reproducing.

  • The knob has three published points on it, and the line between them is ours. λ = 0 is “global term only”; λ = 1 is the full energy of this paper; λ → ∞ is the hard cap, which is what Kelly et al. 2019 actually simulated, since an infinite penalty is a prohibition. Each paper treats its own point as a separate model.
  • Our design work sits between λ = 1 and about 1.6 — between the full curvature and the cap, not between the first two. That is the only stretch where a flat sheet is the lowest state and other specific arrangements still sit in dips above it. Below λ = 1 the knots win and the flat sheet is what gives way, which is claim 4 wired backwards.
  • At λ = 0 we reproduce what it claims: a jump, a wide gap between cooling and heating, and a cold phase shattered into small closed pieces.
  • At λ = 1 we reproduce the other published figure (Kelly et al. 2019, Fig. 8a) to half a per cent, but not this one. We agree with Figure 3 at both ends and differ only between g = 2 and g = 6.3, where its points drop almost vertically and ours rise smoothly. That is unexplained, and it is the question for the authors.
  • The argument that drops triangles is a ground-state argument. The paper excludes them because they are “excluded for an homogenous ground state”. Figure 3 is not about the ground state but about the transition, where the paper's own words leave them alive as defects. We tested whether allowing them explains the gap; the hot end of Figure 3 sits where a triangle-free run sits, so it does not.
  • The claim that it is continuous rests on no hysteresis at 160 points. Our runs show none either. But a weak first-order transition looks exactly the same at that size, because the barrier between the two states grows with the system and a small one hops across it freely. Telling them apart needs the entropy curve.
  • And the decisive test has not been attempted by anyone. The paper's other support is “several other diagnostics for a second order transition have been tested [29], with positive results”. Reference [29] is the 2019 paper, which says of its own work that “at present even such an analysis is precluded” of finite-size scaling, and that “we are some way off the asymptotic regime”. Finite-size scaling is exactly what separates a weak first-order transition from a continuous one. The 2025 review cites the 2019 paper without repeating its caveat.

4.2

Where the curvature actually comes from

IV.2 · Geometry from combinatorics

This is where a network stops being a diagram and becomes a surface. Give every street the same length. Every loop becomes a polygon, and the network becomes a tiling.

At each crossing four polygons meet. Call the number of sides of those four polygons k₁ to k₄. The sum of (kᵢ − 2)/kᵢ decides the shape: below 2 the surface curves like a ball, exactly 2 is flat, above 2 it curves like a saddle.

In the geometric phase every crossing has at least one square and the rest are hexagons or larger. The further you cool, the more squares per crossing and the flatter the surface, until four squares at every crossing makes it exactly flat.

So the number of squares around a crossing is a quantised curvature. It can only be 1, 2, 3 or 4, and each value gives its own fixed curvature.

How we compare

  • Their curvature and our order parameter are the same quantity from two ends. Squares per crossing is four times our φ, so φ = 1 means four squares and flat, φ = 0.75 means three and saddle-shaped.
  • Our ladder of dimensions is a different cut through the same structure. We count the pairs of streets at a crossing that close no square: two such pairs means two large directions (a sheet), one means one (a tube), none means a closed knot. They count how many squares surround a crossing. Both read geometry off the counting, and neither appears in the other's work.
  • We measure no curvature yet. The project's scorecard asks for it and this section is the recipe.

5

Putting the network in motion

V · Dynamics

Everything so far was about which arrangements are favoured. To watch a network change, the paper introduces a universal time and a rule for accepting a change: p = 1 / (1 + exp(ΔH/g)). It gives one elementary move, a “neighbourhood swap” that on a square lattice replaces three squares with two pentagons.

Then an observation that matters for the hypothesis: a bubble of random phase can be moved elsewhere at no energy cost at all, because its energy depends only on how many points are inside it and on its boundary. Free motion at no cost means such bubbles drift, and there is a maximum speed set by the street length and the number of moves per unit time. A speed limit falls out of the model rather than being put into it.

How we compare

  • We implement exactly this acceptance rule, and our tests check it agrees with the more usual Metropolis rule.
  • We do not implement their move. We use a bipartite edge switch instead. We then listed every arrangement up to 18 points and proved our move reaches all of them, which is more than either paper establishes for theirs. At the sizes actually simulated, neither is proved.
  • Their time is Monte Carlo steps, and so is ours. Claim 6 says X has its own ordering and that our time is emergent. That is the same choice the paper makes.

5.1

Three dimensions and quantum mechanics from a two-dimensional screen

V.1 · Hyperbolic holography

This is the most speculative part of the paper, and the part that tries to deliver claim 2.

On a saddle-shaped surface a random walk does not wander the way it does on a flat one. The space opens out so fast that a walker is carried away in what amounts to a straight line. Working this through, the paper arrives at a striking claim: a random process on a two-dimensional saddle-shaped surface, seen by an observer carried along with the flow, looks exactly like quantum mechanics in three-dimensional space.

So the two-dimensional network is a holographic screen, and the three large dimensions we live in, along with quantum behaviour and the arrow of time, are what that screen looks like from inside it.

How we compare

  • We have nothing here, and we say so. Our model has no time and no quantum behaviour, and both are listed as out of reach.
  • This is his answer to the hardest of the six claims, and it is where his programme is least tested. The paper states elsewhere that general relativity has not yet been derived in the large-scale regime.
  • It is worth setting beside your own picture of dimensions. Yours has a fourth large dimension curling up, leaving three. His has three large dimensions appearing as the view from a two-dimensional surface. Both change the number of large dimensions, in opposite directions. Neither is tested.

6.1

Matter as leftover randomness

VI.1 · Matter, the Einstein equations and the cosmological constant

This is the section that competes with claim 4 most directly.

Take a bubble of random phase sitting inside the geometric phase. Its points carry almost no squares, and its boundary points carry fewer squares and longer loops than the space around them. Two things follow together: the bubble has higher free energy than its surroundings, and it is more sharply curved.

Because those two rise together you can write one as proportional to the other. Demand that the relation be a proper tensor equation that conserves what it should, and there is exactly one possibility: Einstein's equations with a cosmological constant.

Matter, in this model is simply the random phase of the microscopic network.
at large scales, it is “geometry that defines matter”, not the other way around.

One thing worth noticing: his bubbles are the unconverted remainder. That is claim 5, doing the work claim 4 assigns to the released energy.

6.3

Allotropes, and the closest published thing to X2

VI.3 · Dark matter

As the coupling falls, not everything reaches the best arrangement. Domains get stuck with a different number of squares per crossing than their surroundings: higher energy, more sharply curved, and long-lived because there is a barrier around them.

He calls these allotropes, after the word for one substance in two crystal forms. Carbon is the example: graphite is the settled form, diamond the higher-energy one that survives anyway. Such a domain is neither empty space nor ordinary matter, but it does gravitate, which makes it a candidate for dark matter.

How we compare

  • This is the closest published thing to X2, and the resemblance is close enough to take seriously.
  • The difference: his allotropes are less stable than the space around them and are left behind by the change. X2 was proposed as more stable than X, and as a product of the change.
  • A note of ours, unverified. His picture has the favoured arrangement stepping through discrete levels as the coupling falls: four squares per crossing, then three, then two. A system that switches between discrete levels, with long-lived stuck domains and a barrier in between, is the usual description of first-order behaviour, and he attributes it to a continuous transition. If those switches are real, each would be a small first-order step with its own latent heat, which would give the released energy a home even if the main transition is continuous.
  • What we have seen ourselves. At λ = 0 our runs shatter into small closed pieces, and one of them is a 14-point piece nobody has described, tying exactly with the 4-cube in energy. So alternative arrangements at equal energy do turn up by themselves in this family, which is the ingredient X2 needs.

Where the hypothesis is on its own

What the paper never addresses

  • A sealed system. Every calculation in the paper is at a fixed coupling, which means an unlimited bath. Claims 5 and 6 are about what happens when the released energy has nowhere to go. We built that ourselves; it is not in the paper.

    Why a bath is assumed at all: it is far easier, it usually agrees with the fixed-total-energy treatment, and cosmological expansion is a reasonable stand-in for a falling temperature. But that agreement is known to fail at exactly one place: a first-order transition. At fixed total energy a system can sit in the coexistence region with the released energy visibly heating what is left; the canonical ensemble jumps across that region and shows a latent heat only as a discontinuity. So the standard setting is blind precisely where claims 4, 5 and 6 live. Ours, unverified.

  • A specific starting arrangement. His “before” is the random phase throughout. X is specific and stable for now. The paper has no counterpart.
  • Energy conservation across the change. Not discussed, because at a fixed coupling it never arises.
  • Whether the transition survives being tested. The decisive measurement, how the barrier between two states grows with the system, is named as out of reach in the one paper that carries the evidence. It is the measurement this project is now set up to make.
