## The eras of the standard universe [search summary; Adams and Laughlin, "A dying universe", Reviews of Modern Physics 1997; their book *The Five Ages of the Universe*, 1999]

| Era | When (years after the beginning) | What is there |
|---|---|---|
| Starlight (stelliferous) | now to about 10^14 | stars; star formation past its peak (about 10^10 years ago, general knowledge) and nearly over by about 10^11, the last small red stars burning to about 10^14 |
| Degenerate | about 10^15 to 10^39 | white dwarfs, brown dwarfs, neutron stars; protons decaying if they do |
| Black hole | to about 10^100 | black holes evaporating by Hawking radiation, the largest last |
| Dark | after | electrons, neutrinos, low-energy light |

**When mass falls into black holes [search summary of the same paper; general knowledge, to verify].** The paper is
reported to say that a galaxy gradually disperses by ejecting the majority of its stellar remnants into intergalactic space
and driving a minority onto its central black hole, over about 10^20 years; proton decay (if it occurs) begins near
cosmological decade 37; black holes then lose mass by Hawking radiation and are gone by about 10^100 years (a solar-mass
hole in about 10^67, general knowledge). Nearly all the mass that ever falls into black holes therefore falls in during the
starlight era (stars collapsing; the central holes growing, mostly in the first few billion years), with one late minority
tranche at about 10^20 years and nothing after. The 10^19 figure sometimes quoted for the central-hole capture was not
confirmed by the search; the text itself has not been read. *What this is for:* piece 12's birth-time distribution
(`docs/design/loop_measure_brief.md`, section 4): under the converter rule a hole births daughters only while fed, so the
births follow this infall history.

*Ours:* the owner's "no more mass, no more time" end of a turn (VISION Update 44, worded in Update 59 as the last black
hole dissolving) sits at the end of the black hole era, about 10^100 years. No published milestone sits near 10^1000.

## How long observation stays possible [search summary; Krauss and Scherrer, "The return of a static universe and the end of cosmology", General Relativity and Gravitation 2007, arXiv:0704.0221; Gravity Research Foundation prize 2007]

In the standard model with a constant dark energy, galaxies outside the Local Group pass beyond the horizon, the cosmic
microwave background redshifts below detectability and the light elements' abundances are polluted by stars, so a future
observer sees one island of stars (the merged Milky Way and Andromeda), infers a static universe, and cannot infer a Big
Bang. The abstract's timescale is of order 100 billion years; one secondary source says trillions. Not read in full.

*Ours:* this is the published form of the owner's "only 10 to 15 billion years left of good observational science"; her
number is about ten times earlier than theirs.

## Endings, and what the data say now

- **Heat death** (a constant dark energy, w = −1) [general knowledge]: expansion forever, the eras above.
- **Big rip** (phantom dark energy, w < −1) [general knowledge; Caldwell, Kamionkowski and Weinberg 2003, not read]:
  the dark energy density grows, and bound structures (clusters, galaxies, solar systems, atoms) are torn apart in a
  finite time.
- **Vacuum decay** [general knowledge; the [C77] picture]: a lower state of the Higgs field reached at a random time, a
  bubble spreading at nearly light speed; nothing measured dates it.
- **What is measured** [search summary; analyses of DESI DR2 baryon acoustic oscillations with supernova samples,
  2025 to 2026]: a weak preference, one to two sigma, for an evolving dark energy with w0 > −1 and wa < 0, phantom in the
  past and weakening now (a "phantom crossing" near redshift 0.4 to 0.5); several papers warn it rests on particular
  tracers and on extrapolating a two-parameter form; DESI DR3 is expected in 2027. A weakening dark energy avoids a rip.

## Is the expansion still speeding up? The supernova age-bias debate [search summaries, 19 October 2026; nothing read]

- **Son, Lee et al. (Yonsei), MNRAS, November 2025** ("Strong progenitor age bias in supernova cosmology II"): a 5.5σ
  correlation between the standardised brightness of type Ia supernovae and the age of their progenitors; corrected, the
  supernova data align with DESI's evolving dark energy and the authors say the universe has already entered a decelerating
  phase. Covered by Smithsonian (the owner's link, 19 October) and a Royal Astronomical Society release; both refused by the
  proxy.
- **Wiseman, Riess, Schmidt et al., "Still accelerating", arXiv:2601.13785, MNRAS 2026**: the standard host-mass correction
  captures the environmental dependence, and with it no age dependence remains; a progenitor is far younger than its host
  galaxy (the delay-time distribution). Secondary coverage speaks of two errors; only the omitted correction is confirmed
  from the abstract.
- **Yonsei group, "Still non-accelerating", arXiv:2605.21586, May 2026**: defends the bias (5.5σ in one sample, 4.8σ in
  another by a remeasurement) and its robustness to the host-progenitor age mapping.
- *Ours:* unresolved; the mainstream reading is still acceleration. If the fading holds, the observation window above
  (Krauss and Scherrer) no longer closes at about 10^11 years, since it relies on a constant dark energy carrying galaxies
  beyond the horizon; that window is the load-bearing term of piece 12's function (VISION Update 68). Both arXiv papers
  are to be read before any of this is cited.
