## 1. Carter 1983, "The anthropic principle and its implications for biological evolution"

**Bibliography.** B. Carter (Observatoire de Paris-Meudon), Phil. Trans. R. Soc. Lond. A 310, 347–363 (1983); DOI 10.1098/rsta.1983.0096 (DOI from a later paper's reference list; to verify). Read in full from a scanned copy with an OCR layer (Sec. 0).

**(i) Earliest time observers like us could exist, and why.** The paper says (Sec. 1, p. 350) that the selection effect first pointed out by Dicke (1961) is "the condition that biological systems based on the same principles as our own can hardly come into existence except when the age of the Universe is of the same order as the well known main-sequence (hydrogen burning) lifetime ... of a typical ordinary star", because "some stars must already have burned out to provide the medium elements that are essential for our chemical constitution, while the maintenance of a suitable continuous energy supply requires that some stars ... must still be burning." No number in years is given for the start. *Ours:* this is the only statement in the paper about the start of the window, and it is an order-of-magnitude statement (one stellar lifetime), not a date.

**(ii) Latest time, and why.** The paper uses the Sun's main-sequence lifetime as the external cut-off on Earth's habitability: "the main sequence lifetime, τ₀ say, of the Sun, during which the energy output from steady hydrogen burning can maintain favourable conditions for life on Earth, is estimated to be given ... by τ₀ ≈ 10¹⁰ years" (Eq. 3.2, p. 353, citing Hoyle 1955). It also allows a shorter geophysical cut-off τₑ "only very slightly greater than the present age, tₑ, of the Earth" as one way out of the dilemma of Sec. 6 (p. 362), and in the discussion with McCrea speaks of "the certainty of a long term astrophysical cut-off (and the conceivable possibility of a geophysical cut-off in a less distant future)" (p. 363). For the Universe as a whole, no end is stated; in the McCrea discussion Carter says that in an imaginary universe with the cut-offs suspended "most of the life systems ... would not produce anything describable as a scientific civilization until after the universe had reached such an advanced state of expansion that the average number of civilizations per unit volume of intergalactic space might actually be lower than in our own Universe now" (p. 363).

**(iii) Delay from a habitable planet to an observer, and the reasoning.** This is the paper's main point (Secs. 3, 5, 6).
- The coincidence (Sec. 3, Eqs. 3.1, 3.2): the time taken by evolution on Earth so far, tₑ ≈ 0.4 × 10¹⁰ yr, against the Sun's lifetime τ₀ ≈ 10¹⁰ yr; "the Sun is now just about halfway through its expected life".
- Three cases for the intrinsically expected time l̄ for intelligent observers to evolve: much less than τ₀, comparable, or much more. The paper sets the middle case aside a priori as an unlikely coincidence of unrelated quantities, rejects "much less" because "it is hard to think of any particular reason why our arrival should have been greatly delayed relative to the intrinsically expected time", and concludes for "much more": "self-selection ensures that ours must be one of the exceptional cases in which evolution has proceeded much faster than usual; on this basis it is to be expected that tₑ should be comparable with τ₀" (pp. 353–354). The corollary stated there: "civilizations comparable with our own are likely to be exceedingly rare".
- The model (Sec. 5): n "critical" steps, each with a rate λ per unit time, λ⁻¹ ≳ τ₀ (Eq. 5.3); probability that all n are done by time t, p = (1 − e^(−λt))ⁿ (Eq. 5.4); mean completion time l̄ = λ⁻¹ hₙ with hₙ the harmonic number (Eq. 5.6 in the OCR, written "(6.6)"); for a random planet with the same conditions the probability of completing all n steps within τ₀ is p ≈ (τ₀/l̄)ⁿ (Eq. 5.12), and "quite moderate lower bounds" give "p < 10⁻²⁰, which is more than sufficient to ensure that our stage of advancement is unique in the visible Universe" (p. 360; the OCR of the bounds is garbled, to verify).
- The timing prediction (Sec. 6): in the limit t ≪ l̄, p ≈ α tⁿ (Eq. 6.1), so the completion of the n steps within 0 < t < τ₀ "will occur near the end of this range to within a fraction of the order of magnitude of 1/n", giving the prediction τ₀ − tₑ ≈ n⁻¹ τ₀ (Eq. 6.2). Against the observation τ₀ − tₑ ≈ tₑ "to within an uncertainty factor hardly exceeding 100 %" (Eq. 6.3), "the values n = 1 and n = 2 are quite consistent, but values from n = 3 onwards become rapidly more difficult to reconcile", so "1 ≤ n ≤ 2" (Eq. 6.4; pp. 361). The abstract: "at least one but probably not more than two links that were highly improbable (a priori) in the available time interval."
- The dilemma and its two resolutions (Sec. 6, p. 362): either n is large and the available time has been overestimated (a geophysical cut-off τₑ "only very slightly greater than" the present age), or n really is one or two (candidates: "the original establishment of the genetic code, and the final breakthrough in cerebral development") and most of the apparent evolutionary ladder is "an illusion". Carter's "present inclination" is the second.

**(iv) Numerical values.** tₑ ≈ 0.4 × 10¹⁰ yr (Eq. 3.1); τ₀ ≈ 10¹⁰ yr (Eq. 3.2); τ₀ − tₑ ≈ tₑ (Eq. 6.3); n between 1 and 2 (Eq. 6.4); p < 10⁻²⁰ for moderate bounds (Eq. 5.12 and text); the expected remaining time after the last step is of order τ₀/n (Eq. 6.2). *Ours, exact from Eq. 6.2 with τ₀ = 10 Gyr:* the remaining time is about 10 Gyr for n = 1 and 5 Gyr for n = 2. *Ours:* Carter's later paper (arXiv:0711.1985, Sec. 3 of the ar5iv extraction) replaces this with "τₑ is only about 6 Gyr" and a best fit of 5 or 6 steps, once the biosphere's remaining time is taken from Caldeira & Kasting (1992) as about 1 Gyr rather than the Sun's 5 Gyr; that revision is the basis of items 2 and 3 below.

**(v) The paper's own caveats.** The argument "is essentially probabilistic, and hence cannot be absolutely watertight" (p. 354). The model treats the steps as independent and of equal rate, "sufficient for our present very broad and general concerns" (p. 359); Sec. 6 says the timing argument "remains valid even when one allows for ordering and unequal probabilities". The available time may have been overestimated (the geophysical cut-off, p. 362). Carter's alternative life forms caveat (Sec. 2) is set aside because we are known to be star-planet based.

## 2. Hanson 1998, "Must early life be easy? The rhythm of major evolutionary transitions"

**Bibliography.** Robin Hanson (Department of Economics, George Mason University), preprint dated 23 September 1998; unpublished as far as the text says (to verify). Read in full.

**(i) Earliest time.** For Earth, the paper says the window opens when "large impacts made Earth uninhabitable by familiar forms of life until roughly 4.2 to 3.8 billion years ago [11]" (Introduction, p. 2; [11] is Orgel 1998). No cosmic start is given.

**(ii) Latest time.** For Earth: "stellar evolution will make it uninhabitable another 1.1 billion years from now [1]" (Introduction, p. 2; [1] is Adams & Laughlin, Sky and Telescope, August 1998). No cosmic end is given. The paper also allows "a destructive process which shortens the expected window of life on Earth" (nearby supernovae, gamma-ray bursts, dense interstellar clouds), needed "if one is to hypothesize much more than about seven critical steps" (p. 9).

**(iii) Delay and reasoning.** The paper's results, in its words:
- The selection effect: "the fact that four billion years elapsed before high intelligence appeared on Earth seems compatible with any expected time longer than a few billion years [4]" (p. 2). Conclusion: Earth's one data point "is consistent with any expected time for high intelligence to arise beyond about a billion years" (p. 10).
- Conditioning on early success splits steps into "easy" and "hard": "the durations of easy steps reflect their difficulty, but the durations of critical steps all are drawn from roughly the same distribution, as is the time remaining after the last step" (Conclusion, p. 10). Theorem 1: easy steps keep their unconditional mean. Theorem 2: for two trial-and-error steps both harder than the window (τ₁, τ₂ ≥ T), "the ratio of their expected values is within [0.7844, 1]" (p. 6; Eq. 1 gives the bound r).
- The remaining-time result: "the expected time window remaining during which simple life can survive on Earth is about the same as the average spacing between hard steps" (p. 4), attributed to Carter [4], with the caveat that the simulations "suggest (but do not prove)" it for independent trial-and-error steps (p. 6). Tables 1 and 2 (10,000 simulated successes) show the "Left" time with the same mean and spread as each hard step (five steps with ×10 difficulty increments: hard-step and leftover means 0.20 to 0.24 of the window; eight steps with ×2 increments: 0.16).
- Early life: with exactly three very hard steps, "the probability of the first transition happening within the first 16% (8%) of the total window would be 42% (22%). (For four such steps, the probability would be 50% (29%).)" (p. 8). So "the early appearance of life on Earth is consistent with at least three to four critical steps alone, or with one initial critical step plus a subsequent random walk in organism sizes" (p. 10).
- Upper limit on n from the remaining time: "The 1.1 billion year estimate ... implies that intelligent life appeared with about 20% of the window remaining. For seven (five) hard critical steps, there is only a 21% (33%) probability of having a remaining window this large or larger" (p. 9). So "the estimated remaining time for life on Earth is consistent with up to five to seven critical steps" (p. 10). Schopf's five transitions (3.5, 1.8, 1.1, 0.6 Gyr ago, plus primates), with the gaps 0.0–0.7, 0.5, 0.6, 0.7, 1.1 and 1.7–2.4 Gyr, "appears to pass our timing tests" (p. 10); Barrow & Tipler's ten steps are rejected because three fall near the Cambrian explosion (p. 9).

**(iv) Numerical values.** Window on Earth: from 4.2–3.8 Gya to 1.1 Gyr from now (Introduction). Life within the first 16 %, perhaps 8 %, of that window (p. 2). Intelligence with about 20 % of the window remaining (p. 9). Hard-step count: at least 3 to 4 (from early life), at most about 5 to 7 (from the remaining time) (Conclusion). Theorem 2 bound 0.7844 (Eq. 1). *Ours, exact:* 4.0 Gya to 1.1 Gyr ahead is a window of about 5.1 Gyr, and 1.1/5.1 ≈ 0.22, which is the "about 20 %".

**(v) The paper's caveats.** The model assumes a fixed sequence, independence, and constant probability per unit time, though "we can even relax the first and last assumptions" (p. 3). "Timing tests seem insufficient by themselves to uniquely identify Earth's critical transitions" (p. 11). The remaining-time result is shown by simulation, not proved (p. 6). Panspermia or a hot-early-Earth requirement are named as other reasons for caution, "based on some relatively contested hypotheses" (p. 3).

## 3. Snyder-Beattie, Sandberg, Drexler & Bonsall 2021, "The timing of evolutionary transitions suggests intelligent life is rare"

**Bibliography.** Astrobiology 21(3), 265–278 (2021), DOI 10.1089/ast.2019.2149; open copy PMC7997718. No arXiv version found (search of 19 October 2026). Read in part (Sec. 0).

**(i) Earliest time.** For Earth: "Earth's oceans are thought to have formed between 4.4 and 4.2 billion years ago (Gya)" (Sec. 4, citing Peck et al. 2001 and Cavosie et al. 2005); earliest evidence of life "between 4.1 and 3.5 Gya" (Sec. 4; Table 1 gives > 4.10 Ga from Bell et al. 2015 and 3.9 Ga from Betts et al. 2018). No cosmic start.

**(ii) Latest time.** For Earth: the authors "assume the habitability of Earth will end in 0.8–1.3 Ga due to the increasing luminosity of the Sun" (Sec. 4), citing Caldeira & Kasting (1992) and Franck et al. (2006); Table 1's ranges differ slightly from the text (0.9–1.5 Ga for Caldeira & Kasting; 0.8–1.2 Ga for Franck et al.; to verify against the PDF). Sec. 2.1 gives "in perhaps less than 1 Ga" (Franck et al. 2006). No cosmic end.

**(iii) Delay and reasoning.** The model (Secs. 3.1–3.3): n sequential transitions, each with an exponentially distributed waiting time of mean βᵢ = 1/λᵢ; the likelihood of the observed transition times is divided by the probability that all transitions finish before the end of Earth's habitability, since only successful runs are observed (Sec. 3.2); "our first key result" is that this corrected likelihood does not fall to zero as the expected transition times go to infinity (Sec. 3.3). Data (Sec. 4, Table 1): abiogenesis (> 4.10 or 3.9 Ga), eukaryogenesis (< 1.84 Ga; 1.87–1.68 Ga), sex (1.2 Ga), intelligence (about now). Results (Sec. 5, Table 2): for the two-step model "over 90% of the posterior probability is assigned to rate parameters" that give "less than a 1% chance" of the final transition within Earth's lifetime; for three- and four-step models this "increases to 99%". Table 2 (as read from the flattened HTML; to verify): for each choice of transition times, with L = 5.2 or 5.8 Gyr the habitable window and N the number of transitions, the posterior weight on the probability of success being above 10⁻², 10⁻¹² and 10⁻²⁴; the summary rows read N = 2: 0.42, 0.66, 0.94; N = 3: 0.27, 0.51, 0.84; N = 4: 0.18, 0.39, 0.71. Stated conclusions: "expected evolutionary transition times likely exceed the lifetime of Earth, perhaps by many orders of magnitude" (abstract); "Intelligent life therefore emerged on a timescale within an order of magnitude of our star's lifetime" (Sec. 2.1); "this suggests that intelligent life is likely to be exceptionally rare" (Sec. 7); "we may possibly be the only intelligent civilization within the observable universe" (Sec. 6); "Our results corroborate the original argument suggested by Brandon Carter" (abstract). On the size of the expected times: "expected transition times exceeding 10⁶ Ga are compatible with the data" (Fig. 1 caption); evidence of eukaryotes 3 Ga ago "would still be insufficient to rule out expected transition times exceeding 1000 Ga" (Sec. 5.2).

On M dwarfs (Sec. 6.2): the chance that the final transition occurs within a 5 Ga window against a longer window is "heavily skewed in favor of longer lasting environments, by between 4 and 10 orders of magnitude depending on the number of transitions" (Fig. 5); so if M-dwarf planets had Earth's transition parameters, our existence around the Sun predicts that "some other factor is reducing the habitability of dwarf star environments by a factor of over 10,000", framed as a testable prediction; the abstract puts it as "planets orbiting M dwarf stars are uninhabitable" (to verify the exact wording against the PDF).

**(iv) Numerical values.** Window: oceans 4.4–4.2 Gya to an end 0.8–1.3 Ga ahead (Sec. 4), L = 5.2 or 5.8 Gyr (Table 2). Transition dates as above. Posterior weights as above. The M-dwarf factor "over 10,000" (Sec. 6.2).

**(v) The paper's caveats (Sec. 5.3 and 5.1).** Constant rates are "crude" but "the best model because it requires the fewest biological assumptions". Environmental gating (oxygen) shifts the posterior toward faster rates, but "arbitrarily slow rates are still not excluded" (Fig. 4 caption). "Any alternative model still needs to explain why certain evolutionary transitions took such a long period of time." The Self-Indication Assumption "will undermine our results" and is "poorly understood", outside the paper's scope. The results depend on the upper bound of the log-uniform prior, which the authors argue would have to be "unrealistically small" to change the conclusion.

## 4. Behroozi & Peeples 2015, "On the history and future of cosmic planet formation"

**Bibliography.** P. S. Behroozi and M. Peeples (STScI), MNRAS 454, 1811 (2015), arXiv:1508.01202 (v1, 5 August 2015), DOI 10.1093/mnras/stv1817. Read in full.

**(i) Earliest time.** The paper says Earth-like planet formation "tracks the galaxy/cosmic star formation rates" (Fig. 2 caption), with the star formation histories of Behroozi et al. (2013e) covering galaxies out to z ≈ 8, "> 90% of all star formation" (Sec. 2). A metallicity threshold of [Fe/H] = −1.5 (Johnson & Li 2012) "has very limited effects on total planet counts" (Conclusion iii) but "would strongly diminish the number of Earth-like planets formed around stars older than 12 billion years in the Milky Way", consistent with the oldest star known to host Earth-like companions, 11.2 ± 1.0 Gyr (Sec. 3). *Ours:* read from Fig. 2 (top panels), Earth-like planet formation in the Universe is already appreciable by cosmic time about 2 Gyr and its 50 % contour sits before the Solar System's formation; no start date in Gyr is printed in the text (to verify from the figure).

**(ii) Latest time.** The paper says that if the gas now in dark-matter haloes keeps cooling, the remaining star formation happens "over the next trillion years" (Sec. 4.1, citing Adams & Laughlin 1997; Tutukov et al. 2000; Nagamine & Loeb 2004), and "most of the additional planets formed in both the Universe and the Milky Way will be in the very far future (100 Gyr to 1 Tyr from now)" (Sec. 4.1). "Only 8% of the currently available gas around galaxies ... had been converted into stars at the Earth's formation time" (Sec. 4.1). So the planet-formation window closes on a 100 Gyr to 1 Tyr scale, and the paper gives no habitability end beyond that.

**(iii) Delay.** Not modelled as a per-planet lag. The paper uses "the time tc that it took our own species and civilisation to evolve", tc = 4.6 Gyr, against the stellar-mass doubling time td ≈ 20 Gyr, to explain the observer bias in the "late" formation of Earth: "the 'late' formation time of our own planet speaks more to the ratio of tc to td than to conditions for habitability" (Sec. 4.1, Fig. 3). The reference class for habitable zones is "0.6-1.4 M⊙ (K to F5-class) stars", "14.8% of stars by number" (Sec. 2), so nE = 0.015 (Sec. 2) with Lissauer et al.'s 0.1 Earth-like planets per Sun-like star; Sec. 3 says the Earth-like rate is star formation "scaled by nE = 0.0073", a different number (ours: unresolved in the text; to verify).

**(iv) Numerical values.** About 10²⁰ Earth-like and 10²⁰ giant planets in the Hubble volume (10¹³ Mpc³), 10⁹ and 10¹⁰ in the Milky Way (Sec. 3, Conclusions i, ii). The Solar System formed after 80 % of existing Earth-like planets (Universe and Milky Way), after 50 % of existing giant planets in the Milky Way and 70 % in the Universe (Conclusion vi; Fig. 2). Earth formed before about 61 % of all planets the Milky Way–M31 group will ever form (Sec. 4.1) and before 92 % of all Earth-like planets the Universe will ever form, "even discounting any future gas accretion" (Sec. 4.1; f = 0.92 in Eq. 2); hence "at most an 8% chance of us being the only civilisation the Universe will ever have", with ⟨N⟩ = 12.5 civilisations if Earth is the first, scaling as 12.5E if Earth is the E-th (Sec. 4.2, Eqs. 2, 3, Fig. 4). Giant planets' median age is about 2.5 Gyr younger than Earth-like planets' (Conclusion v). Uncertainties "∼1 dex" from Kepler incidence rates (Fig. 2 caption).

**(v) The paper's caveats.** Planet incidence uncertain by 0.5–1 dex, IMF and stellar-population systematics 0.2–0.3 dex (Sec. 3). The PFR is "best interpreted as the bound planet formation rate"; scattering, flybys and eccentricity changes are not modelled, and "larger stellar densities in the past would lead to more interactions and therefore less planetary stability", which would raise the share of future planets (Appendix A). Earth being the first civilisation is "a very conservative assumption" (Sec. 4.2). The future is conditional on gas cooling continuing (Conclusion vii). Most future planets "will not be visible from the Milky Way" because of the accelerating expansion (Sec. 4.1).

## 5. Lineweaver 2001, "An estimate of the age distribution of terrestrial planets in the Universe: quantifying metallicity as a selection effect"

**Bibliography.** C. H. Lineweaver (UNSW), Icarus 151, 307 (2001), arXiv:astro-ph/0012399 (v2, 9 March 2001), DOI 10.1006/icar.2001.6607. Read in full.

**(i) Earliest time.** The paper says Earth-like planet formation lags star formation because metals must first build up: "The time delay between the onset of star formation and the onset of terrestrial planet formation ... is ∼1.5 ± 0.3 Gyr and has an important dependence on the low metallicity tail of P_HE" (Sec. 5; Fig. 2C). The production of earths is taken to be zero at Z/Z⊙ ≈ 1/10 ([Fe/H] = −1.0), with 1/20 to 1/5 as the uncertainty range (Sec. 2.2). *Ours:* the paper's time axis starts at the onset of star formation in its Fig. 2 and does not print an absolute cosmic time for the onset of earths in the text; "1.5 Gyr after the first stars" is the published number, and the conversion to Gyr after the Big Bang depends on when star formation is taken to begin (to verify from Fig. 2).

**(ii) Latest time.** None for the Universe. The high-metallicity side is limited by hot Jupiters destroying earths: P_HE "gets cut off sharply at M ≳ 0.3" (Sec. 2.3), so at late times, as metallicity rises, fewer systems can keep earths. The age distribution's tail: "95% [of earths] are between 0.6 and 10.5 Gyr old" (Sec. 5). *Ours:* a late cosmic cut-off from metallicity is implicit in the method but not stated as a date.

**(iii) Delay.** Not modelled. The paper assumes life forms promptly: "life on Earth seems to have arisen as soon as temperatures permitted" (Sec. 5, citing Lazcano & Miller 1994), with heavy bombardment to about 3.8 Gya and the earliest isotopic evidence at about 3.9 Gya (Sec. 5). "If life exists on some of these earths, it will have evolved, on average, 1.8 billion years longer than we have on Earth" (Sec. 5). Reference class: "sun-like stars (spectral types F7 - K1, in the mass range 0.8 ≲ m/m⊙ ≲ 1.2)", about 5 % of the mass that forms stars (Sec. 3).

**(iv) Numerical values.** Average age of earths around sun-like stars 6.4 ± 0.9 Gyr, so 1.8 ± 0.9 Gyr older than Earth; 74 ± 9 % older than Earth, 26 ± 9 % younger; 68 % between 3.3 and 9.3 Gyr old, 95 % between 0.6 and 10.5 Gyr (Sec. 5). Delay of earths behind stars 1.5 ± 0.3 Gyr (Sec. 5). P_HE peaks at [Fe/H] = 0.135, mean −0.063, median −0.036, 68 % range −0.38 to 0.21 (Sec. 2.3). P_DE: more than 95 % of stars with [Fe/H] > 0.4 host a hot Jupiter, about 20 % at 0.2, about 5 % at solar (Sec. 2.1). Present mean metallicity of star-forming regions M(t₀) = 0.63 with σ = 0.3 (Sec. 4).

**(v) The paper's caveats.** The linear-in-metallicity production of earths is an assumption (Sec. 2.2) and a threshold instead "would shift to the right or left" (Sec. 5). The SFR estimates of 2000 are uncertain and set the error bars (Fig. 2A). Four assumptions needed to read Fig. 2C as an age distribution for life (Sec. 5): molecular-chemistry life; surfaces of earths in classical habitable zones as the dominant harbours; other time-dependent selection effects (supernovae, gamma-ray bursts, clusters) less important than metallicity; and "life is long-lived", otherwise the oldest tail must be chopped off. Infall of metal-rich material may make a star's observed metallicity unfaithful (Sec. 6).

## 6. Haqq-Misra, Kopparapu & Wolf 2018, "Why do we find ourselves around a yellow star instead of a red star?"

**Bibliography.** J. Haqq-Misra, R. K. Kopparapu, E. T. Wolf, Int. J. Astrobiology 17, 77–86 (2018), DOI 10.1017/S1473550417000118, arXiv:1705.07813. Read in full. (The 12 October note read this paper in part; this section supersedes it on the points below and agrees with it where they overlap.)

**(i) Earliest time.** The paper says it considers "the distribution of habitable planets across the entire history of the universe from time t = 0 to t = t_M, which is the timeframe when conscious observers could arise" (Sec. 6.1). No start date beyond t = 0 is used; the model is a likelihood over stellar types weighted by lifetime fractions, not a time series.

**(ii) Latest time.** The end is the longest M-dwarf lifetime: "habitability is limited to the main sequence lifetime of a star, with a typical lifetime of t_G = 10¹⁰ yr for G-dwarf stars, t_K = 10¹¹ yr for K-dwarf stars, and up to t_M = 10¹³ yr for small M-dwarf stars" (Sec. 6.1), giving f_G = 10⁻³, f_K = 10⁻², f_M = 1. Alternatively the window ends when the reference class does: "If observers in our operational reference class do not exist in the future, then our existence around a G-dwarf star at the present era is to be expected" (C5, Sec. 6.2), with the discussion (Sec. 7) suggesting the class may be "a function of time" and "short-lived".

**(iii) Delay.** The paper says "Conscious life on Earth took about 4.5 billion years to develop after the planet formed, but with a sample size of one we cannot predict how typical this timescale may be for other stellar environments" (Sec. 6); F-dwarfs and hotter are excluded because "biological evolution requires at least a few billion years of stable main sequence conditions" (Sec. 5). A speculative alternative (Sec. 7): "the evolution of life on planetary systems develops on a timescale proportional to the lifetime of the star itself, so that M-dwarfs today should not expect to develop any form of advanced life until much later in the future." The operational reference class (Premise P2, Sec. 3): "observers ... possess the cognitive ability to meaningfully comprehend modern scholarly information."

**(iv) Numerical values.** Stellar fractions N_G/N⋆ = 0.07, N_K/N⋆ = 0.12, N_M/N⋆ = 0.77 (Sec. 5.1). Habitable-zone widths HZ_G = 0.74 AU, HZ_K = 0.30 AU, HZ_M = 0.12 AU from flux limits 1.1–0.35 S₀ (G), 1.0–0.26 S₀ (K), 1.5–0.25 S₀ (M, synchronous rotation) with l_K = 0.1 l_G, l_M = 0.01 l_G (Sec. 5.1, Eq. 9). Likelihoods at present: P(G|L) ≈ 0.29, P(K|L) ≈ 0.20, P(M|L) ≈ 0.51; posterior odds K/G ≈ 0.40, M/G ≈ 0.16 (Eqs. 10, 11) → C1 ("about a ninety percent lower likelihood of instead existing around an M-dwarf star today"). With M dwarfs uninhabitable: P(G|L) ≈ 0.59, P(K|L) ≈ 0.41, K/G ≈ 0.41 (Eqs. 12, 13) → C2. Across all time (Eq. 14): P(G|L) ≈ 5.6 × 10⁻⁴, P(K|L) ≈ 3.9 × 10⁻³, P(M|L) ≈ 0.99; K/G ≈ 4.0, M/G ≈ 160 (Eqs. 15, 16) → C3 ("over a hundred times greater likelihood of existing around an M-dwarf star in the future"). Across time with M dwarfs out: P(G|L) ≈ 0.13, P(K|L) ≈ 0.87, K/G ≈ 3.9 (Eqs. 17, 18) → C4 ("about a four times greater likelihood of existing around a K-dwarf star in the future").

**(v) The paper's caveats.** Bayesian methods "implicitly assume that the number of planets inhabited by conscious observers over the history of the universe is a statistically significant quantity" (footnote 4). The habitable-zone census is "illustrative" and will be updated (Sec. 5). M-dwarf habitability is undecided in print, with the pre-main-sequence runaway greenhouse, flares and volatile deficiency on one side and thick substellar clouds, water worlds and ozone shielding on the other (Sec. 5.2). Late K dwarfs behave like M dwarfs (Sec. 5). A 1-in-100 fluke "remains within the realm of possibility" (Sec. 7). The reference class may change through evolution or technology (Sec. 7; the doomsday-argument discussion).

## 7. Lingam & Loeb 2018, "Is life most likely around Sun-like stars?"

**Bibliography.** M. Lingam and A. Loeb (Harvard CfA/ITC), JCAP 05 (2018) 020, arXiv:1710.11134 (v2, 7 May 2018). Read in full.

**(i) Earliest time.** Not addressed; the paper works per star, not in cosmic time. Table 1: t_SW = 1.7 × 10⁷ yr at 0.1 M⊙, 3.2 × 10⁹ at 0.3, 8.8 × 10¹⁰ at 0.6, 10¹² at 1.0, 6.9 × 10¹² at 1.5. *Ours:* so for stars below about 0.55 M⊙ this paper's effective window is far shorter than the main-sequence lifetime, which is the mechanism behind "most M-dwarfs are not likely to host life"; the cosmic end implied is the lifetime of the lowest-mass star that still passes, roughly a 0.5 M⊙ star (Loeb et al.'s τ(0.49) ≈ 9 × 10¹⁰ yr; 12 October note), rather than 10¹³ yr.

**(iii) Delay.** Abiogenesis time t₀ ≈ t⊕ (M/M⊙)⁻³ for M ≲ M⊙ and t⊕ (M/M⊙)⁻¹ above, with t⊕ ≳ 200 Myr (Eq. 2.2), from bioactive UV flux F ∝ M³ (M ≲ M⊙) and F ∝ M (M ≳ M⊙) (Eq. 2.1). For M dwarfs "the timescale for the origin of life would be ∼10 Gyr if the associated timescale on Earth is ∼100 Myr" (Sec. 2). Oxygenation time t_O₂ ≈ 0.22 t⊙ (M/M⊙)²·³ for M ≲ M⊙ with chromospheric emission (Eq. 3.1), or 0.22 t⊙ (M/M⊙)⁻⁶·⁶ without (Eq. 3.2); the GOE at about 2.4 Gyr ago anchors it (Sec. 3). Table 1: t_ℓ = 2 × 10¹¹ yr (0.1 M⊙), 7.5 × 10⁹ (0.3), 1.6 × 10⁹ (0.6), 2.4 × 10⁹ (1.0), 7.1 × 10⁸ (1.5). ## 8. Kipping 2025, "Solar hegemony: M-dwarfs are unlikely to host observers such as ourselves"

**Bibliography.** D. Kipping (Columbia), arXiv:2510.01215 v1, 19 September 2025, "Submitted to AAS Journals"; draft dated 3 October 2025. Read in full.

**(i) Earliest time.** The paper says a star can host observers only once t > t_birth + t_ZAMS + T_delay (Sec. 2.4, criterion 1), with stellar births drawn from the Madau & Dickinson (2014) star-formation rate (Eq. 5) and t_ZAMS interpolated from MIST tracks. No cosmic start is stated beyond what that implies. *Ours:* the earliest observers in this model are at about the first stars plus 3 Gyr, i.e. of order 3 Gyr after the Big Bang (to verify against Fig. 1).

**(ii) Latest time.** Two ends. (a) Stellar: "even with M_crit = 0.08 M⊙, zero stars (out of 10⁶) are left by 10.7 Tyr and thus we set T_win,max = 10 Tyr" (Sec. 2.4); the abstract: "the stelliferous period spans ∼10 Tyr - yet here we are living in the first 0.1% of that volume." (b) Planetary: a per-planet observer window T_win after the star's birth, "essentially a death-date", "most plausibly geophysics" (Secs. 2.1, 2.3); "most geological estimates far smaller values of order 5-10 Gyr (O'Neill et al. 2016; Cheng 2018)" (Sec. 3.1). With T_win = 10 Gyr the track "hits zero by t ∼ 70 Gyr", because "the last [star] is born t ∼ 60 Gyr" (Sec. 2.6). A third option, a Universe-wide cataclysm with cut-off t_max, is tried and set aside: "we constrain t_max < 310 Gyr (2 σ)" but its evidence is "about 2:1 disfavored over even the truncated window hypothesis" (Sec. 3.5).

**(iii) Delay.** A single fixed delay: "T_delay is a universal, fixed parameter defining some delay time for observers to develop after their star is born. We adopt 3 Gyr for this parameter - the time it took for the first multicellular life to emerge on Earth" (Sec. 2.4). Consequence stated: "stars M⋆ > M⊙ will not live long enough to ever produce observers" under the TAMS formula (Sec. 2.4). The TAMS lifetimes are Loeb et al. 2016's (Eq. 6; same as the 12 October note's Eq. 2.3). IMF: Kroupa et al. (2024), static (Sec. 2.4).

**(iv) Numerical values.** t₀ = 13.789 Gyr (Sec. 2.1). Priors: M_crit uniform on 0.08 to 1 M⊙ (Eq. 3); T_win log-uniform from the Earth's age to 10 Tyr (Eq. 4). Joint model: M_crit > 0.45 M⊙ and T_win < 7.6 Tyr at 2σ (Sec. 3.1). Model comparison (Table 1): desolate-M-dwarf alone M_crit > 0.59 M⊙; truncated window alone T_win < 210 Gyr, log Z = −2.9305 against −1.4158 for the joint model, Bayes factor 32.7 (Sec. 3.3); hybrid with T_win fixed at 10 Gyr: M_crit > 0.34 M⊙ at 2σ, > 0.74 M⊙ at 1σ, log Z = −1.3070, favored by 1.28 over the joint model (Secs. 3.2, 3.3). Luck against the desolate-M-dwarf model: Bayes factor 1597 (Eq. 9, Sec. 3.4). The 2σ cut at 0.34 M⊙ "kills some two-thirds of all stars in the Universe"; 0.45 M⊙ excludes "∼75% of all stars" (Secs. 3.1, 4). Ideal hosts for SETI "0.74 - 1.6 Solar masses" (Sec. 4).

**(v) The paper's caveats.** "Just two data points" (Sec. 2.1), so the simplest models are used (a sharp cut-off; a universal T_win). The hybrid model "to some degree ... was engineered to win" and its Bayes factor over the joint model "is marginal", justified only by O'Neill et al.'s geophysical lifetime (Sec. 3.3). The definition of "observer" is "deliberately avoided"; the work "only formally applies to self-aware, reasoning observers such as ourselves, as not life in general" (Sec. 4). A Bayes factor is not an odds ratio; a prior that favors universes with more observers "could overwhelm the large Bayes factor" (Sec. 4). Solar metallicity is assumed in the stellar tracks (Sec. 2.4).

## D. Where they disagree

**The red-dwarf question (do small stars host observers?).** Haqq-Misra et al. (2018) keep both answers on the table: if M dwarfs are as good as G dwarfs, the future M-dwarf era is favored "over a hundred times" (C3), and our existence is a 1-in-100 fluke they call acceptable; if M dwarfs are poor, the present G-dwarf era is "expected" (C2, C4, C5). Lingam & Loeb (2018) give a mechanism for "poor": stellar winds strip Earth-analog atmospheres in 10–100 Myr around low-mass M dwarfs while UV-limited abiogenesis takes about 10 Gyr there, so N⋆ < 1 below 0.3 M⊙ and complex biospheres peak for 0.5–1 M⊙. Snyder-Beattie et al. (2021) reach the same side from the hard-steps direction: longer windows are favored by 4 to 10 orders of magnitude, so our existence around the Sun implies a suppression of M-dwarf habitability "over 10,000". Kipping (2025) makes it a Bayesian verdict: luck disfavored by about 1600, a shortened planetary window alone disfavored by about 33, M_crit > 0.34 M⊙ at 2σ. Loeb et al. (2016), the starting point, kept the full 0.08 M⊙ range and so put the peak at 10¹³ yr. *Ours:* the four later papers all land on "small stars are suppressed", but by different mechanisms (atmospheric loss and UV; a hard-steps timing argument; a mass cut inferred from two data points) and with different cuts (0.3, 0.5, 0.34–0.74 M⊙); none of them is a measurement of an M-dwarf biosphere, and Haqq-Misra et al.'s Sec. 5.2 lists the published reasons the suppression may not be real.

**The hard-steps timescale.** Carter 1983 reads the 4 Gyr-against-10 Gyr coincidence as evidence that the intrinsic time for intelligence greatly exceeds a stellar lifetime, with only 1 or 2 hard steps because 5 Gyr seemed to remain. Hanson 1998 and Carter 2008 use the shorter biosphere lifetime (about 1 Gyr left) to allow 5 to 7 steps, and Hanson shows the early appearance of life is itself explained by 3 or more hard steps. Snyder-Beattie et al. 2021 formalize the selection correction and find the data cannot bound the expected times from above at all ("exceeding 10⁶ Ga are compatible"). Against all of these, Mills et al. (arXiv:2408.10293; searched, not read) argue that the steps came late because Earth's environment (oxygen) only recently permitted them, "without invoking the existence of hard steps", and Snyder-Beattie et al.'s own Sec. 5.3 concedes that oxygen gating shifts the posterior toward faster rates while not excluding slow ones. Kipping 2025 sidesteps the question with a fixed 3 Gyr delay; Haqq-Misra et al. use "about 4.5 billion years" and "at least a few billion years"; Lingam & Loeb give 2.4 Gyr to complex life for the Sun and nothing for intelligence; Lineweaver and Behroozi & Peeples assume life is prompt or do not model it. *Ours:* the delay is the least constrained input. Published choices for the time from a habitable planet to an observer run from about 3 Gyr (Kipping) through 4.5–4.6 Gyr (Haqq-Misra; Behroozi & Peeples' t_c) to "longer than the host star lives, selected" (Carter; Snyder-Beattie), and the last choice changes the shape of the observer distribution within each planet's window (pushed toward the end, by Carter's Eq. 6.2 and Hanson's leftover-time result).

**The planet-formation future.** Behroozi & Peeples (2015) say the Universe "will form over 10 times more planets than currently exist", mostly 100 Gyr to 1 Tyr from now, if halo gas keeps cooling; Earth formed before 92 % of all Earth-like planets ever. Loeb et al. (2016) extrapolate the Madau & Dickinson rate with an e^(−t/50 Gyr) cut-off from a closed-box gas reservoir (12 October note), which puts almost all star formation within the next 100–200 Gyr. Kipping (2025) draws births from the Madau & Dickinson shape with no future tail at all ("the last one is born t ∼ 60 Gyr" among 10⁶ stars, Sec. 2.6). Lineweaver (2001) looks only backward and finds most earths already older than Earth. *Ours:* these are three different futures for the same input (star formation after today): a trillion-year tail, a 50 Gyr e-fold, and an effective stop near 60 Gyr. Which is used moves the end of the planet-supply window by one to two orders of magnitude and so moves any observer peak that is not already killed by the stellar-mass cut.

## E. For the pre-registration (ours)

These are candidate reference classes, each fully specified by published start, end and delay choices with their sources. Nothing is computed; where published work says where the observer peak falls relative to the present, that is reported qualitatively, with the source. No class is claimed to be right. The owner chooses which to pre-register and what to predict.

**Class (a): Sun-like stars only (G-type and near).**
- Stars: 0.8–1.2 M⊙ (Lineweaver 2001, Sec. 3, "F7–K1"); or Kipping's 1σ cut, 0.74–1.6 M⊙ (Sec. 4), noting that in his model stars above 1 M⊙ never reach T_delay = 3 Gyr before TAMS.
- Start: first Earth-like planets 1.5 ± 0.3 Gyr after star formation begins (Lineweaver 2001, Sec. 5), with star formation beginning at about 30 Myr (Loeb et al. 2016, 12 October note) or as the Madau & Dickinson fit has it (Kipping 2025, Eq. 5).
- End: each planet's window closes at the host's main-sequence end, τ(m) = 10 Gyr (m/M⊙)⁻²·⁵ (Loeb et al. Eq. 2.3; Kipping Eq. 6), or at T_win = 10 Gyr if shorter (Kipping 2025, hybrid model, after O'Neill et al. 2016); the planet supply ends with star formation under one of the three futures of Sec. D.
- Delay: 3 Gyr (Kipping), or 4.5 Gyr (Haqq-Misra et al., Sec. 6), or hard-steps-shaped (observer time pushed toward the planet's end, Carter Eq. 6.2; Hanson 1998).
- Published position of the peak: Loeb et al. 2016 say "for mass ranges centered narrowly around a solar mass, we find that the current time indeed represents the peak" (12 October note, Sec. 1.4); Haqq-Misra et al.'s C5 says the present is "to be expected" if the class has no future; Kipping's abstract says we live "in the first 0.1%" of the stelliferous period but his favored model removes most of that period. *Ours:* in this class the published expectation is a peak near the present, within a few Gyr either way, set mainly by the star-formation history and the 10 Gyr lifetime.

**Class (b): K and G stars.**
- Stars: 0.5–1.2 M⊙ (Lingam & Loeb 2018's lower bound of about 0.5 M⊙ for complex biospheres, Sec. 2; Haqq-Misra et al.'s early-to-mid K plus G, Sec. 5); or Kipping's 2σ hybrid cut, > 0.34 M⊙ (Sec. 3.3).
- Start: as (a).
- End: per planet, the stellar lifetime, up to τ(0.49 M⊙) ≈ 9 × 10¹⁰ yr (Loeb et al. Eq. 2.3; ours, 12 October note) or τ(0.34) longer still; or T_win = 10 Gyr if the planetary window is adopted (Kipping), which makes (b) end like (a).
- Delay: Lingam & Loeb's t_ℓ(M) (Eqs. 2.2, 3.1: 1.5 Gyr at 0.67 M⊙, 2.4 Gyr at 1 M⊙) plus an unspecified time to intelligence; or the fixed and hard-steps options of (a).
- Published position of the peak: Haqq-Misra et al.'s C4 gives "about a four times greater likelihood of existing around a K-dwarf star in the future" when M dwarfs are excluded; Loeb et al. 2016 say "a rather small extension of the mass range down to a quarter of a solar mass shifts the peak by two orders of magnitude in time into the future" (12 October note), and 0.5 M⊙ lies between their 0.49 and 0.90 curves; Lingam & Loeb put the present at about 10 % probability for this class. *Ours:* in this class the published expectation is a peak in the future by a factor of a few to about ten in cosmic time if the stellar lifetime sets the end, and near the present if a 10 Gyr planetary window does; the two end rules disagree most here.

**Class (c): all stars down to 0.08 M⊙.**
- Stars: 0.08 to about 3 M⊙ (Loeb et al. 2016, Sec. 2.2; Kipping's M_crit = 0.08 M⊙ "luck" model).
- Start: as (a); or Loeb et al.'s first stars at 30 Myr with life immediate.
- End: the longest M-dwarf lifetime, about 10¹³ yr (Loeb et al. Eq. 2.3 at 0.08 M⊙, 1.07 × 10¹³ yr by the 12 October note's arithmetic; Haqq-Misra et al.'s t_M = 10¹³ yr; Kipping's 10.7 Tyr).
- Delay: Loeb et al.'s none; Kipping's 3 Gyr; or Lingam & Loeb's t₀ ∝ M⁻³ (10 Gyr for M dwarfs if 0.1 Gyr on Earth), which by itself removes most of the M-dwarf advantage.
- Published position of the peak: Loeb et al. 2016's headline, a peak at t ≈ 10¹³ yr "a thousand times larger than for the Sun", with the present at the 0.1 % level; Haqq-Misra et al.'s C3, 160 to 1 for the M-dwarf future; Kipping's Fig. 1 darkest track. *Ours:* in this class every paper read puts the peak far in the future, by about three orders of magnitude in time, and the present at or below the 0.1 % level; this is the class the later papers were written to reject.

**Class (d), suggested by the reading: a planet-limited class, any host star but a fixed planetary observer window.**
- Stars: any star above the hydrogen-burning limit whose lifetime exceeds the delay, or Kipping's M_crit scan.
- Start: as (a).
- End: per planet, T_win ≈ 10 Gyr after the star's birth from the end of active geology (Kipping 2025, Secs. 2.1, 2.3, 3.1–3.3, citing O'Neill et al. 2016 and Cheng 2018), the planetary analogue of the Earth-only ends used by Hanson (1.1 Gyr ahead), Snyder-Beattie et al. (0.8–1.3 Gyr ahead) and Carter 2008 (about 1 Gyr ahead), all of which make Earth's window about 5–6 Gyr long, shorter than the Sun's life.
- Delay: 3 Gyr (Kipping) or hard-steps-shaped within the planetary window (Hanson; Snyder-Beattie).
- Published position of the peak: Kipping's Fig. 2 shows the T_win = 10 Gyr track ending by about 70 Gyr with the curve's bulk set by the star-formation history; he finds this hypothesis alone "unable to well-explain the data" because it says nothing about why the Sun (Sec. 3.3). *Ours:* in this class the observer peak follows the star-formation history delayed by a few Gyr, so it lies in the past-to-present range (the cosmic star-formation peak is at about 3.5 Gyr, Madau & Dickinson Eq. 15, 12 October note), and the class trades the red-dwarf question for a geophysics assumption. It is the natural fourth class because it is the only one in which the end is set by the planet rather than the star, which is what every Earth-only hard-steps paper assumes.

**What every class needs stated (ours).** Which of the three futures of Sec. D is used for star formation after today; whether the observer count is per planet, per planet-year, or per observer-moment (the 12 October note, Sec. 5, says the published object is a planet rate); and whether the delay is a fixed shift, a stellar-mass-dependent shift (Lingam & Loeb), or a hard-steps distribution that pushes observers toward each planet's end (Carter; Hanson; Snyder-Beattie). The last choice is the one the published literature disagrees about most and the one none of the cosmic-time papers (Loeb et al.; Behroozi & Peeples; Kipping) has combined with a cosmic-time model.
