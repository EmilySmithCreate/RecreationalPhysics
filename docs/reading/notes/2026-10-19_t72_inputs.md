## 1. Madau and Dickinson 2014, the cosmic star-formation history

**Bibliography (from the arXiv abs page).** Piero Madau and Mark Dickinson, "Cosmic Star-Formation History", *Annual Review of Astronomy and Astrophysics* 52 (2014); arXiv:1403.0007 [astro-ph.CO], v1 28 Feb 2014, v3 13 Jun 2014; DOI 10.1146/annurev-astro-081811-125615. The abs page does not list the page numbers; the pre-registration's "52, 415" is **to verify** against the journal.

**How much was read.** The whole ar5iv text in three passes (sections 1 to 3, 5.1 to 5.2 with Figures 8 and 9, and 5.3 to 6), searched for the fitting function, the peak, the IMF, the dust correction and the uncertainty statements. Tables and figures were read through their captions only.

### 1.1 The fitting function (needed for T72's n_pl)

- **The paper says (Section 5.1, Equation 15, Figure 9 caption):** the best-fit star-formation rate density is

      ψ(z) = 0.015 (1+z)^2.7 / {1 + [(1+z)/2.9]^5.6}   M⊙ yr⁻¹ Mpc⁻³.

  The sentence introducing it: "Figure 9 shows the cosmic SFH from UV and IR data following the above prescriptions, as well as the best-fitting function." The Figure 9 caption says the solid curve "plots the best-fit SFRD in Equation 15."
- **The paper says (Figure 8 and 9 captions):** the luminosity-to-SFR conversion factors behind the data points are K_FUV = 1.15 × 10⁻²⁸ and K_IR = 4.5 × 10⁻⁴⁴ (cgs), "valid for a Salpeter IMF".
- **The paper says (Section 1, final paragraph):** the adopted cosmology is Ω_M = 0.3, Ω_Λ = 0.7, Ω_b = 0.045, h = 0.7.
- **Ours:** Equation 15 is in units of M⊙ per year per comoving Mpc³ and is for a Salpeter IMF over 0.1 to 100 M⊙ (Section 2, below). For T72 the overall normalization cancels in p_R(t), which is normalized over t; only the shape against redshift matters, together with the conversion from z to cosmic age under the paper's own cosmology (h = 0.7, Ω_M = 0.3) or Planck's; which cosmology T72 uses for z → t is to be declared in the script and the difference reported (ours: small beside the other uncertainties, but to be checked, not assumed).

### 1.2 The peak and its cosmic age

- **The paper says (Abstract):** the star-formation rate density "peaked approximately 3.5 Gyr after the Big Bang, at z ≈ 1.9" and "declined exponentially at later times, with an e-folding timescale of 3.9 Gyr."
- **The paper says (Section 5.1):** the SFRD is "slowing and peaking at some point probably between z = 2 and 1.5, when the Universe was ∼3.5 Gyr old"; for z < 1 the data "can also be fit quite well by an exponential decline with cosmic time and an e-folding timescale of 3.9 Gyr."
- **The paper says (Section 6):** "the SFRD peaked ∼3.5 Gyr after the Big Bang"; the fit "places this peak at z ≈ 1.85, which is plausible but still uncertain"; and "it is still hard to accurately pinpoint the redshift of maximum SFRD within a range Δz = 1."
- **Ours:** the abstract's z ≈ 1.9 and Section 6's z ≈ 1.85 are the same number at the precision the paper claims (Δz = 1). For T72: peak at z ≈ 1.9 (range about 1.5 to 2.5), cosmic age about 3.5 Gyr.

### 1.3 Stated uncertainty

- **The paper says (Section 5.1):** the authors "simply retained the fractional errors on the SFRD measurements published by each author" and caution that these errors "should not be taken too literally", especially where faint-end slopes differ. Robust far-infrared luminosity density measurements are not yet available at z > 2.5.
- **The paper says (Section 6):** "Uncertainties in the faint-end slope of the IRLF and in extinction corrections for the UVLF ... still dominate at this peak era." "At z > 4, our galaxy surveys have been strongly biased toward UV-bright galaxies and may underestimate both SFRDs and SMDs."
- **The paper says (Section 5.3):** integrating ψ(z) predicts a stellar mass density "somewhat high (∼0.2 dex on average, or 60%)" against the measured one; "a ∼60% effect should not be disregarded"; the offset "can be reduced further to only ∼0.1 dex" (under the adjustments described there).
- **Ours:** the paper gives **no single uncertainty on Equation 15** (no "± x dex" on the fit). What it gives is: the peak redshift uncertain by about Δz = 1; a 0.1 to 0.2 dex tension in the integral; and a warning that the high-redshift tail (z > 4) may be underestimated. For T72, the sensible sensitivity test is to shift the peak redshift within 1.5 to 2.5 and the high-z tail by a factor of about 2, and report both. A quoted "±0.2 dex" would be our reading, not the paper's number, and must be labelled so.

### 1.4 The IMF, for a user

- **The paper says (Section 2):** "Although disfavored by many observations, a Salpeter IMF" is used as the reference throughout the review, over 0.1 to 100 M⊙.
- **The paper says (Section 3.1.1):** to convert SFRs quoted for other IMFs to Salpeter, "we divide by constant factors of 0.63 (Chabrier) or 0.67 (Kroupa)"; for stellar masses the factors are 0.61 (Chabrier) and 0.66 (Kroupa). **Section 5.3:** "When needed, we have scaled from a Chabrier IMF to a Salpeter IMF by multiplying the stellar masses by a factor of 1.64"; "it seems premature to tinker further with the IMF."
- **Ours:** for T72 the IMF matters in two places. (a) The normalization of ψ(z), which cancels. (b) The share of stars that fall in each reference class's mass range, which does **not** cancel: the number of 0.8 to 1.2 M⊙ stars formed per unit of ψ is set by the IMF's shape, and Salpeter over 0.1 to 100 M⊙ puts more mass into low-mass stars than Chabrier or Kroupa. T72's script must declare which IMF converts ψ (mass formed) into stars of each mass, and class C's reproduction gate must use the 2016 paper's IMF, whatever it is (to be read from that paper; not covered by this note).

### 1.5 Dust corrections, for a user

- **The paper says (Section 3.1.3):** on average "80% of the FUV emission from typical" Lyman-break galaxies at z ≈ 2 is absorbed by dust (citing Reddy et al. 2012); the Calzetti and Meurer UV extinction laws are "broadly appropriate" for most L* LBGs at z ≈ 2 but "may fail for other galaxies including the most IR-luminous objects"; dust-obscured galaxies contribute "5 to 10% of the SFRD at z ≈ 2".
- **The paper says (Section 5.1):** "Clearly, a robust determination of dust attenuation is essential to transform FUV luminosity densities into total SFRDs"; "the gap between the UV and IR measurements increases with redshift out to at least" z ≈ 1 and may narrow between z = 1 and 2; the star formation missed by UV surveys at high redshift "remains uncertain."
- **Ours:** Equation 15 is fit to **dust-corrected** UV plus IR data; a user must not apply a further dust correction to it, and must know that above z ≈ 2.5 the IR side of the correction is missing, so the tail is UV-based and the paper itself calls it uncertain.

## 2. Bryson and others 2021, rocky habitable-zone planets around solar-like stars

**Bibliography (from the arXiv abs page).** Steve Bryson, Michelle Kunimoto, Ravi K. Kopparapu, and 79 others (82 authors), "The Occurrence of Rocky Habitable Zone Planets Around Solar-Like Stars from Kepler Data", *The Astronomical Journal*; arXiv:2010.14812 [astro-ph.EP], v1 28 Oct 2020, v2 3 Nov 2020; DOI 10.3847/1538-3881/abc418. The abs page gives no volume or article number; the pre-registration's "161, 36" is **to verify** against the journal.

**How much was read.** The ar5iv text in two passes (abstract through Section III; Sections IV and V with Tables 5 to 7); the appendices were not read. Searched for the headline rates, the G and K split, the temperature ranges, the Teff dependence and the caveats.

### 2.1 Headline occurrence rates

- **The paper says (Abstract):** η⊕ is defined as the habitable-zone occurrence of planets "with radius between 0.5 and 1.5 R⊕" around main-sequence stars "with effective temperatures between 4800 K and 6300 K". Conservative habitable zone: η⊕ "between 0.37 (−0.21, +0.48) and 0.60 (−0.36, +0.90) planets per star"; optimistic habitable zone: "between 0.58 (−0.33, +0.73) and 0.88 (−0.51, +1.28) planets per star" (68 % credible intervals). The two values in each pair are the two extreme assumptions for extrapolating completeness beyond the data (constant and zero; Section III.3.2). The large uncertainties come from "the small number of detected small HZ planets."
- **The paper says (Section II.1):** "We consider planets in the 0.5 to 1.5 R⊕ size range to calculate rocky planet occurrence rates", noting that some studies put the rocky limit as high as 2.5 R⊕ but that many such planets are highly irradiated.

### 2.2 G and K stars separately

- **The paper says (Section IV.2, Table 5; Model 1, hab2 population, with input uncertainties, 0.5 to 1.5 R⊕; low = constant extrapolation, high = zero extrapolation; 68 % intervals):**

  | Habitable zone | G stars (5300 to 6000 K) | K stars (3900 to 5300 K) |
  |---|---|---|
  | Conservative | 0.38 (−0.22, +0.50) to 0.63 (−0.38, +0.94) | 0.32 (−0.17, +0.35) to 0.42 (−0.23, +0.50) |
  | Optimistic | 0.60 (−0.34, +0.75) to 0.93 (−0.55, +1.32) | 0.49 (−0.26, +0.51) to 0.60 (−0.33, +0.69) |

- **The paper says (Section IV.2, Table 6):** 95 % and 99 % credible intervals; for example the 95 % interval for the conservative K-star rate is [0.07, 1.34] (constant extrapolation) and [0.10, 3.77] (zero extrapolation).
- **The paper says (Section V.9, Table 7):** for the combined GK population (3900 to 6000 K), conservative 0.32 (−0.18, +0.41) to 0.40 (−0.23, +0.54), optimistic 0.48 (−0.27, +0.59) to 0.59 (−0.33, +0.76); an FGK population (3900 to 7300 K) is also given but "extends well into" temperatures with no detections and very low or zero completeness, so it requires extrapolation from cooler stars.
- **Ours:** the paper's temperature bands are in effective temperature, not mass. T72's classes are in mass (A and D: 0.8 to 1.2 M⊙; B: 0.5 to 1.4 M⊙). The mapping from mass to Teff on the main sequence (roughly: 0.8 M⊙ ≈ 5000 K, 1.0 M⊙ ≈ 5800 K, 1.2 M⊙ ≈ 6200 K, 0.5 M⊙ ≈ 3800 K, 1.4 M⊙ ≈ 6600 K; **general knowledge, to verify** against a stellar-parameter table before use) puts class A/D inside the paper's 4800 to 6300 K headline band, and class B slightly beyond it at both ends. Since the paper finds no compelling Teff dependence (2.3 below), the simplest declared choice for T72 is one rate for all of classes A, B and D, the headline conservative pair 0.37 to 0.60, with the optimistic pair as the sensitivity case. As with ψ(z), a constant occurrence cancels in the normalized p_R(t); it matters only if it differs between classes or changes with time (metallicity), which the pre-registration handles separately (Lineweaver 2001; Behroozi and Peeples 2015).

### 2.3 Dependence on stellar type, and extrapolation

- **The paper says (Section II.1):** the sample is limited to 4800 to 6300 K, "covering mid K to late F"; the lower cut is motivated by direct-imaging inner working angles and by likely tidal locking below 4800 K; the upper cut because the habitable zones of early F stars have orbital periods Kepler cannot detect.
- **The paper says (Section II.2, Equation 4):** hotter stars have wider habitable zones, a "geometric effect" modelled by a function g(Teff); whether HZ occurrence depends on Teff depends on how planets are spaced in semi-major axis; if spacing scales as Δa/a the geometric dependence cancels.
- **The paper says (Sections IV.1, IV.2, V.2.1, V.6):** the models detect a weak increase of HZ occurrence with Teff that is "not compelling"; overlapping 68 % intervals would allow a Teff-independent rate; models 1 and 2 detect a weaker Teff dependence than expected from the larger HZ of hotter stars; an information-criterion comparison did not significantly favour any of models 1 to 3.
- **The paper says (Section V.4):** the expected nearest HZ rocky planet, assuming a random distribution, is about 6 pc for G dwarfs (5.5 to 5.9 pc) and about 4 pc for K dwarfs (3.6 to 4.1 pc); using the 95 % lower bound (0.07), the nearest is within about 6 pc at 95 % confidence and 7.5 pc at 99 %; about 7 HZ planets around G dwarfs and 14 around K dwarfs within 10 pc, or about 4 using the 95 % bound.
- **M dwarfs:** **not found** in the text read (abstract through Section V); the paper's sample starts at 3900 K (hab2) and its headline at 4800 K, so it makes no M-dwarf statement that we found. **To verify** in the appendices and Section V before any M-dwarf number is attributed to this paper. T72's class C takes its M-dwarf occurrence from the 12 October reading note, not from here.
- **Ours:** for T72 this paper covers classes A, B and D (K to late F) and says nothing about class C's M dwarfs. Extrapolation of its rate upward in Teff (F stars) is warned against in the paper's own words (Section V.9).

### 2.4 Caveats a user must know

- **The paper says (Section V.8):** ignoring the reliability correction (against false alarms and astrophysical false positives) changes the conservative η⊕ by about a factor of two.
- **The paper says (Sections IV.2, V.1, III.3.2):** DR25 completeness data end at 500 days; completeness beyond is bracketed by the constant and zero extrapolations, which is why every rate is a pair.
- **The paper says (Section V.10):** the 1.5 R⊕ rocky limit may be too high; the HZ limits are model-dependent; stellar multiplicity may affect results at about the 20 % level and is only partly mitigated; power-law population models may not describe the population well at high instellation.
- **The paper says (Section V.11):** Poisson uncertainty from the few small HZ detections and low completeness dominates.
- **Ours:** the honest range for "Earth-size planets in the habitable zone per sun-like star" from this paper is 0.1 to 1 at 68 % and 0.07 to several at 95 %. T72 should carry the 68 % pair and state that the number is uncertain by a factor of about three either way.

## 3. Krauss and Scherrer 2007, the end of cosmology

**Bibliography (from the arXiv abs page).** Lawrence M. Krauss and Robert J. Scherrer, "The Return of a Static Universe and the End of Cosmology", *General Relativity and Gravitation* 39, 1545 to 1550 (2007), DOI 10.1007/s10714-007-0472-9; also *Int. J. Mod. Phys. D* 17, 685 to 690 (2008); arXiv:0704.0221 [astro-ph], v1 2 Apr 2007, v3 27 Jun 2007. Fifth prize, 2007 Gravity Research Foundation essay competition.

**How much was read.** The whole ar5iv text (a six-page essay: introduction and five short sections). Its equations are unnumbered in the conversion.

### 3.1 The timescale

- **The paper says (Introduction):** all cosmological models with a non-zero cosmological constant approach a de Sitter universe in the far future, with scale factor R(t) = exp(Ht); the present energy budget is "roughly 30% matter (both dark matter and baryonic matter) and 70% dark energy".
- **The paper says (Section 1, "The disappearance of the Hubble expansion"):** galaxies outside the Local Group leave the observable universe "in a timescale on the order of 100 billion years" (attributed to Krauss and Starkman); light from distant objects is "exponentially redshifted"; the Local Group "remains gravitationally bound in the face of the accelerated Hubble expansion" (citing Loeb; Nagamine and Loeb), and the future observer lives in "the merger product of the Milky Way and Andromeda". The far-future matter-to-dark-energy ratio inside the horizon is ρ_M/ρ_Λ ∼ 10⁻¹² (citing Busha et al.).
- **The paper says (Section 2, "Vanishing CMB"):** at t ≈ 100 Gyr "the peak wavelength of the cosmic microwave background will be redshifted to roughly" 1 m (about 300 MHz) and "the intensity of the CMB will also be redshifted by about 12 orders of magnitude"; the CMB becomes unobservable once its peak wavelength exceeds the plasma wavelength of the interstellar medium, "which corresponds to a plasma frequency of" about 1 kHz (n_e ≈ 0.03 cm⁻³, λ_p ≈ 3 × 10⁷ cm); "this corresponds to an expansion factor" of about 10⁸ relative to today, which "will be reached when the universe is less than 50 times its present age", assuming exponential expansion dominated by dark energy.
- **The paper says (Section 3):** the inference that the universe expands or contracts "is dependent upon the cosmological hypothesis that we live in an isotropic and homogeneous universe", which the island observer has no way to test.
- **The paper says (Section 4, "Polluted elemental abundances"):** helium-4 "has been enhanced by only a few percent" since Big Bang nucleosynthesis; "extrapolating forward by 100 Gyr" the authors expect "significantly more contamination of the helium-4 abundance, and concomitant destruction of the relic deuterium"; the ultimate helium mass fraction is Y = 0.6 (citing Adams and Laughlin), so "the primordial helium mass fraction of Y = 0.25 will be a relatively small fraction of this abundance."
- **The paper says (Introduction, Sections 1 and 5):** these timescales are "comparable to the longest lived main sequence stars"; no numerical stellar lifetime is given.

### 3.2 Caveats, the paper's and ours

- **The paper says:** the argument assumes a cosmological constant (not evolving dark energy); a bound observer in the Local Group; and that the island observer cannot test homogeneity. H₀ and ρ_Λ are not given numerically; the 100 Gyr is an order-of-magnitude statement, and the CMB figure is a bound ("less than 50 times its present age", that is, under about 700 Gyr by our arithmetic from 13.8 Gyr, **ours**).
- **Ours:** the three thresholds the pre-registration asked for are, in the paper: galaxies beyond the Local Group gone, of order 100 Gyr (Section 1); the CMB undetectable through the interstellar plasma, within 50 present ages, with 100 Gyr the epoch at which it has already dimmed by 10¹² (Section 2); the light-element record swamped, over the same 100 Gyr (Section 4). None is a sharp number; the paper's own phrase is "on the order of 100 billion years". T72's cap W(t) at 10¹¹ yr is therefore the paper's order of magnitude, and the sensitivity test should move it by a factor of a few either way (say 5 × 10¹⁰ to 5 × 10¹¹ yr), not treat it as 100.0 Gyr. As the pre-registration already expects, the cap bites only where class D's planets are long gone, so its exact value should not matter for D; that expectation is to be checked, not assumed.

## 4. The main-sequence lifetime against stellar mass

**What was sought:** a citable statement of τ_MS ≈ 10 Gyr × (M/M⊙)⁻²·⁵ and the mass range in which it holds. A textbook was not reachable directly from here; what follows is from a reference work that cites textbooks, and from a code documentation page that compares published fits. Both are second-hand for the textbook statements and marked so.

- **The source says (Wikipedia, "Main sequence", section "Lifetime", read 19 October 2026):** "τ_MS ≈ 10¹⁰ years [M/M⊙][L⊙/L] = 10¹⁰ years [M/M⊙]⁻²·⁵", citing Hansen and Kawaler (1994), *Stellar Interiors: Physical Principles, Structure, and Evolution*, Birkhäuser, p. 28, for the formula; the mass-luminosity power law L ∝ M³·⁵ and its range, "This relationship applies to main-sequence stars in the range 0.1 to 50 M☉", citing Rolfs and Rodney (1988), *Cauldrons in the Cosmos*, University of Chicago Press; and, citing Laughlin, Bodenheimer and Adams (1997), *ApJ* 482, 420, that "stars with less than a tenth of a solar mass may last for over a trillion years" while "the most massive stars may remain on the main sequence for only a few million years." **The textbook pages themselves were not read; to verify** against Hansen and Kawaler p. 28 and Rolfs and Rodney.
- **The source says (VICE documentation, "Stellar lifetimes", read 19 October 2026):** the single power law τ_MS = τ⊙ m⁻^α with τ⊙ = 10 Gyr and α = 3.5 (that is, L ∝ M^(1+α) = M⁴·⁵; note their exponent differs from the 2.5 above) "fails for higher mass stars (≳ 4 M⊙)" and is of limited accuracy below about 0.5 M⊙; it compares published fits with explicit forms: Larson (1974), *MNRAS* 166, 585, log τ = α + β log m + γ (log m)² with (α, β, γ) = (1.02, −3.57, 0.90) originally, (1.0, −3.42, 0.88) as used by Kobayashi 2004 (τ in Gyr); Maeder and Meynet (1989), *A&A* 210, 155, a broken power law whose lowest segment, m ≤ 1.3 M⊙, is τ = 10^(−0.6545 log m + 1) Gyr; Padovani and Matteucci (1993); Hurley, Pols and Tout (2000), *MNRAS* 315, 543, metallicity-dependent.
- **Ours, arithmetic on the sources above.** At the two ends of class A/D (0.8 and 1.2 M⊙) the simple relation gives 17.5 Gyr and 6.3 Gyr; Maeder and Meynet's low-mass segment gives 10 × 0.8⁻⁰·⁶⁵⁴⁵ = 11.6 Gyr and 10 × 1.2⁻⁰·⁶⁵⁴⁵ = 8.9 Gyr (**ours**; the segment's exponent is far shallower than 2.5, so the two prescriptions differ by 50 % at 0.8 M⊙; this is to be checked against a modern evolutionary-track table, for example Hurley, Pols and Tout 2000, before class A's window is fixed). For class B's lower edge, 0.5 M⊙, the simple relation gives 57 Gyr and the VICE page warns it is inaccurate there; for class C's top, 3 M⊙, it gives 0.64 Gyr, inside the range where the single power law is said to fail (≳ 4 M⊙) only just. For class C's 0.08 M⊙ end the relation gives 5.5 × 10¹² yr, consistent with Laughlin, Bodenheimer and Adams' "over a trillion years" and with the pre-registration's "to 10¹³ yr".
- **Ours, recommendation for T72.** State the relation as τ_MS ≈ 10 Gyr (M/M⊙)⁻²·⁵, source Hansen and Kawaler 1994 p. 28 as cited by Wikipedia (to verify), valid roughly for 0.1 to 50 M⊙ as a mass-luminosity law and least accurate below 0.5 M⊙ and above 4 M⊙; and report class A and D with the Maeder and Meynet low-mass segment as the sensitivity case, since the two differ most exactly where those classes live. Class C's reproduction gate should use whatever lifetime law the 2016 paper used (not covered here).

**Ours, closing.** Three of the four inputs are order-of-magnitude or factor-of-two quantities by their own authors' account (the SFRD tail, η⊕, the 10¹¹ yr cap); only the SFRD peak epoch (3.5 Gyr, z ≈ 1.9 ± 0.5) is tight. T72's gate 3 sensitivity tests should therefore vary η⊕ (cancels unless class-dependent), the cap (×5 either way), the lifetime law (two prescriptions) and the SFRD peak (±0.5 in z), and report which, if any, moves a class across a verdict boundary.
