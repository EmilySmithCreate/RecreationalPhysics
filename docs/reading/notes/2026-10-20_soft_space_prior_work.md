## 1. Massless (Gaussian) fields on dynamical triangulations

**What exists.** Coupling a massless field to a random lattice is exactly what the soft-space kernel would do, and it is a
mature, thirty-year-old line of work. [AGJZ12] is the closest in form. Its geometry is two-dimensional causal dynamical
triangulations on a torus; it puts d massless fields on the triangles, which are the points of the dual graph, a
three-regular graph; the field energy is Σ over neighbors Σ_μ (x_i − x_j)² (Eq. 2), with the constant mode fixed; the
geometry moves by adding and deleting links, the field by heat bath (Sec. 2). It is the brief's kernel with three links
per point instead of four, and with d fields instead of one. They state (Sec. 4, Eqs. 9 and 10) that integrating the
fields out gives the weight det′(D)^(−d/2) per geometry, with D the graph Laplacian, and that computing it is practical
only below a few hundred points. That is the same N² cost the brief found for integrating out (O61), and the same reason
to keep the field explicit.

**What they found.** For d ≤ 1 the torus stays uniform in time, its shape set by fluctuations. For d > 1 the geometry
changes: almost all the volume gathers into a "blob" of fixed extent with Hausdorff dimension 3, whose volume profile is
V(t) ∝ cos²(α t / N^(1/3)) (Eqs. 5 and 6), at N = 8,000 to 128,000 triangles; α grows with d (their Fig. 4). This is the
causal version of the c = 1 barrier of two-dimensional quantum gravity, where matter with central charge above 1 destroys
the surface (Sec. 1). A single massless field is c = 1, exactly at the barrier.

**What is close to the toy.** *Ours, unverified.* The toy with one field (the brief's choice) sits where [AGJZ12] saw
nothing happen to the geometry, which agrees with O61's finding that the field's own fluctuations barely move the ladder
(about 0.004 g in λ). It also says that if the toy ever carried several soft fields (for instance a length per link,
item 3, which is several numbers per point), their combined fluctuations could act on the wiring strongly, and that is a
published, measurable effect rather than a surprise.

**Fields as coordinates, and so as rows.** [ADGGJN21a] (p. 2, Eqs. 2 and 3) and [ABB12] (Sec. 2, Eq. 22) build
coordinates on a random torus from the same Laplacian: solve Laplace's equation with a jump of one unit across a cut, so
each coordinate is a harmonic map to a circle. On a toroidal triangulation the two harmonic one-forms give the torus's
shape parameter τ = τ₁ + iτ₂, whose real part is its twist (Sec. 2, Eq. 22 and the five steps of Sec. 3), and an embedding
in the plane in which "any vertex is located at the center of mass of its neighbors" (Sec. 2, Fig. 2, 150,000 triangles).
For the pure-geometry ensemble the shape's continuum distribution is τ₂⁻² F(τ)^(c−1) with F(τ) = τ₂^(−1/2) e^(πτ₂/6)
Π_n |1 − e^(2πinτ)|^(−2) (Eqs. 4 and 5), tested at c = 0 and c = −2. [ADGGJN21b] adds a back-reaction: when the jump δ is
made large (δ = 1.0 against 0.1, in four-dimensional causal triangulations on a three-torus), the field's energy changes
the geometry and the topology can change.

**Which published measurement could the toy's graph-plus-field kernel reproduce first?** *Ours.* In order of cost:
(a) none of these ensembles first, but the exact lattice numbers of section 8 below, which test the field part alone;
(b) the harmonic coordinates and τ of [ABB12] on the toy's built flat tori, where the answer is known by construction;
(c) the d-dependence of [AGJZ12]: whether the toy's wiring responds when d copies of the field are carried, and whether
the onset is above d = 1. Matching their numbers would need their lattice (dual graphs of causal triangulations, not the
toy's square-counting graphs); in the toy it would be a new question, not a reproduction. (d) The shape distribution of
[ABB12] Eqs. (4) and (5) is for an ensemble of floppy triangulations; the toy's flat space is rigid, so it does not apply.

## 2. A Newtonian pull measured in discrete quantum gravity

**Already recorded** (gravity note of 25 September, topic 2): [dBS97] found positive binding energies, 0.02 to 0.08 in
lattice units, between two scalar test particles in four-dimensional dynamical triangulations, near the transition; and
[DLSU21] found the binding energy growing as m^α with α = 4.6(9), against Newton's 5 (their Eq. 22), after tuning and
extrapolation to the continuum and infinite volume. A search on 20 October found nothing later on the same question.

**New here: [HW95]** (abstract and Sec. 2 opening read). In Regge's lattice gravity, with edge lengths as the variables
on a hypercubic lattice made rigid by diagonals, they compute the potential between two heavy masses from correlations of
the lines they trace, check that the continuum analog gives Newton's potential at weak field, and find near the critical
point that "non-linear graviton interactions give rise to a potential which is Yukawa-like, with a mass parameter that
decreases towards the critical point". *Ours:* a lattice gravity model measured at finite size and near, not at, its
critical point gave a screened pull, which is what the gap argument of O60 (a) predicts and what soft space is meant to
remove by putting the massless field in.

**Distance from the toy.** Both of these use a geometry that fluctuates at every scale and is four-dimensional over a
range of scales; the toy's space does not fluctuate (its flat space is gapped). Neither is a first replication target.

## 3. Regge calculus: do lengths on links give Einstein's waves?

**Yes, with a condition the brief does not yet state.** [Ham09] (pp. 135 to 143) summarizes Roček and Williams (1981):
expanding Regge's action (curvature carried by deficit angles at hinges, Eq. 518) to second order about flat space on a
hypercubic lattice gives "the lattice graviton propagator in a De Donder-like gauge ... precisely analogous to the
continuum expression". The condition is the lattice: the hypercubic lattice is "made rigid by introducing face diagonals,
body diagonals and hyperbody diagonals", which gives 2^d − 1 = 15 edge lengths per point in four dimensions against the
metric's 10 components. Of the 15, four are zero modes (translations), one more is the hyperdiagonal, and after
block-diagonalization "the remaining degrees of freedom are 10, as in the continuum"; the transverse-traceless part, the
waves, appears explicitly (Eqs. 546 and 547). The redundant lattice variables "are non-propagating". [DFS07] (abstract)
recovers "the complete free graviton propagator in the continuum limit" in a three-dimensional lattice model and reads the
action's single zero mode per simplex as a remnant of coordinate freedom. [Gen99] (abstract) argues "that only a
tetrahedral lattice can successfully reproduce the continuum solution" for gravitational-wave initial data.

**Numerical Regge evolution.** [GM01] and [BG01]: full 3+1 Regge evolutions exist (the Kasner cosmology), the solutions
converge to Einstein's at second order in the lattice spacing, and a controversy about convergence was resolved in that
sense ([BG01], abstract). [Gen99] built Brill-wave initial data, second-order accurate. [Bre15] (abstract) finds Regge
evolution about 110 times slower than the "smooth lattice" method. We found no published time evolution of a
gravitational wave by Regge calculus; the search was two queries and is not a rule-15 search.

**What this means for "a length per link" in the toy.** *Ours, unverified, counting from [Ham09].* In three space
directions a metric has 6 components per point, and a cubic lattice offers 3 edges per point (one per direction), so
edge lengths on the toy's six-link graph alone cannot hold the metric: they can stretch the three directions but not
shear them, and a cube with only its edges given is not rigid. The analog of the Roček–Williams lattice adds the
diagonals: three face diagonals and one body diagonal per point, 7 lengths against 6 components. In the toy a face
diagonal would be a length assigned to each square (a square has two), not to a link. So the brief's sentence that waves
"need a quantity on the links (a length per link)" should read: a length per link **and per square diagonal**, or some
other set rich enough to fix the shape of each cube. A plus-polarized wave along an axis changes only lengths along axes
and would survive on links alone; the cross polarization is a shear and would not.

## 4. Gravity as time dilation and as refraction

**Refraction.** For a static weak field with negligible stresses, light moves as if in a medium of refractive index
n = (1 − 2Φ) (in units with c = 1, Φ negative near a mass), from h_tt = −2Φ and h_ij = −2Φ δ_ij ([BCFVW05] Eq. 29,
citing the lensing textbooks for the "well-known standard results"). With isotropic pressure a second potential Ψ₀
enters, n = 1 − 2Φ − 2Ψ₀ with ∇²Ψ₀ = 4πG p (Eqs. 24 to 28): in Einstein's gravity stress also bends light, which a
single φ sourced by curling energy would not capture (*ours*; small for slow matter). The optical analogy itself is old:
Gordon (1923) wrote an effective metric for a dielectric, g_eff = η + (1 − n⁻²) V V ([BLV] Sec. 3.1.1, Eq. 146).

**What the analogues can and cannot do.** In analogue gravity the medium gives the metric and "the underlying classical
equations of motion have nothing to do with Einstein equations" ([BLV], Sec. on semiclassical back-reaction); Volovik
writes that in superfluid helium "the 'gravity' itself is not determined by Einstein equations" ([Vol01] p. 16). That is
the soft-space situation too: the kinematics (how clocks and rulers respond) can be made right, while the dynamics (what
sources the field and how) is put in.

**Slow matter falls because clocks run at different rates.** [Unr93] (pp. 5 to 11) argues exactly this with his
"beach ball" picture: "one can alternatively explain this essential attribute of gravity by assuming that time flows
unequably from place to place, without calling into play any 'force of gravity' at all." [OST00] (abstract) defends the
reading of the gravitational redshift through clocks that "run the faster the higher they are located".

**Walkers whose rate is set by their departure point.** This is published, and in the right setting. [Sme11] Secs. 3.1 to
3.3 takes a random walk whose jumps happen at fixed rates in the walker's own proper time; converting to the common time
multiplies the rate by the lapse N at the point the walker leaves, so the probability flux is j = N(σ) p(σ) Γ(σ → σ′)
(Eq. 21: "where proper time runs faster (high N), the walker jumps more frequently"). In a static spacetime, with N = χ
the redshift factor, the equilibrium condition is that χ p* satisfies ordinary detailed balance (Eq. 26), so for symmetric
jumps p* ∝ 1/χ: walkers collect where clocks run slow. Smerlak calls this "the stochastic counterpart of the
Tolman–Ehrenfest relation". The same rule appears in biology and soft matter as "self-propelled particles tend to
accumulate where they move more slowly", and when they also slow down where crowded this feedback produces clumping that
maps, at leading order, onto "passive particles with attractions" ([CT15], abstract; [TC08]; Schnitzer 1993, not read).
[LSRK21] (abstract) is a toy model "for gravity" built on walkers that move faster where the medium has been visited,
giving an effective attraction; abstract only.

*Ours, arithmetic, unverified:* the size of the effect. With χ ≈ e^Φ, p* ∝ e^(−Φ) is a Boltzmann distribution
e^(−mΦ/kT) at temperature kT = mc². At the Earth's surface Φ/c² is about 7 × 10⁻¹⁰, so the excess of walkers lower down
is of that order: the time-dilation drift is real, but for anything colder than its own rest energy it is far weaker than
falling, which in Einstein's account is inertia following the slowed clocks (Unruh's picture), not diffusion. So the
brief's question 4(b) is a published mechanism, and it is the right description of a random walker in a gravitational
field; whether it can stand in for the toy's pull depends on the toy having nothing like inertia, which it does not.

## 5. What creates soft space: induced gravity and media with soft modes

**Sakharov** ([Vis02] Secs. 1 to 3). On a given geometry, one-loop quantum fields automatically produce terms in the
effective action proportional to a cosmological constant, to the Einstein–Hilbert term and to curvature squared (Eq. 1);
gravity's stiffness is "induced", as "hydrodynamics or continuum elasticity theory emerges from molecular physics". Visser
lists four readings (one-loop dominance, finiteness, calculability, renormalizability) and the observational point that
the induced corrections give Yukawa terms of Planck range (Eq. 26). *Ours:* in the toy the analog runs the other way
round: the soft field's own fluctuations induce a weight on the wiring (det^(−1/2), O61's spanning-tree count), which is
Sakharov's mechanism at toy scale, and it was found to be tiny.

**Volovik** ([Vol01], abstract and p. 16): in superfluids the relativistic fields, gauge fields and an effective metric
arise as collective modes; the effective gravity "cannot reproduce in a full scale the Einstein theory", its action being
"contaminated by the noncovariant terms". *Ours:* the lesson for the toy is that a medium can supply soft modes and an
effective metric for free; it does not supply Einstein's dynamics.

**A crystal whose defects carry gravity** ([KZ04], abstract and Sec. I). Kleinert's 1987 "world crystal" models
spacetime as a crystal in which disclinations are curvature and dislocations torsion; with ordinary elasticity the force
between curvature sources "grow[s] linearly with the distance R" and is confining, and the correct 1/R appears "by
assuming that the dislocations have proliferated" (a nematic phase), which also explains why no torsion is seen. *Ours,
unverified:* this is the nearest published answer to "what creates soft space" that lives inside the medium rather than
being added: a rigid crystal confines, and a crystal whose slip defects have condensed carries a long-range pull. The toy's
flat space is the rigid case; its dislocations would be shifted seams, the twisted rows of section 7. Whether the toy has
a phase in which such seams are cheap and proliferate without melting the sheet is a question the toy could ask with no
new field; T76's clusters of missing squares (O141) are the nearest thing measured, and they melted.

## 7. Twisted grids

**Every flat four-link torus is a twisted grid, and the twisted grids are classified.** "4-regular quadrangulations of
the torus (bipartite or not) can be described (not necessarily uniquely) by three integer parameters m ≥ 1 (width),
n ≥ 1 (height) and q (shift)", Q(m, n; q): take an m-by-n grid, close the vertical direction as a cycle, and join the
right-hand column to the left-hand one with a shift of q, (m − 1, j) to (0, j + q) ([EM13] Sec. 2, citing Altshuler and
others). Thomassen (1991) classified all quadrangulated tori and Klein bottles into eight families, and Li reduced the
torus families to one, T(n, m, r), "an n × m chessboard by sticking the left and right sides together and then
identifying the top and bottom sides with a torsion of r squares" ([LZZ23] Theorems 2.1 and 2.2). **T(n, m, r) is
bipartite if and only if both m and n + r are even** ([LZZ23] Theorem 2.4). [EM13] adds that by Euler's formula every
4-regular bipartite graph on the torus is a quadrangulation.

*Ours, to verify against the toy's code:* a flat state of the four-link toy (every link in exactly two squares) is a
4-regular bipartite quadrangulation of a closed surface, so if that surface is a torus it is one of these Q(m, n; q), and
the shifted seam of the opened tube (O116) is the shift q. Two consequences for growth by rows (T76): a row can be inserted
along either direction and the shift stays an integer; but by Theorem 2.4 a single inserted row can break bipartiteness
when it changes the parity of n + r, so on a twisted torus rows may have to be added in pairs, or the shift adjusted.

**How rows and coordinates are defined.** On a built twisted grid the rows are the grid's own cycles. On any wiring,
including a damaged or partly opened one, the published way is the harmonic coordinates of section 1 ([ABB12] Eq. 22;
[ADGGJN21a] Eqs. 2 and 3): one Laplace solve per direction, with a unit jump across a cut, gives a coordinate whose level
sets are the rows, and the torus's twist is the real part of τ. It uses the same graph Laplacian the soft field needs, so
it costs nothing extra in the kernel.

**Twisted tori elsewhere.** Lattice Monte Carlo uses helical (screw) boundary conditions, which number the sites with a
single index so that the last site of one row is a neighbor of the first site of the next (Newman and Barkema 1999, not
read). Parallel computers use twisted tori because a twist restores edge symmetry to a rectangular torus and shortens
distances: the ILLIAC IV "employed a twist of one unit in the wraparound links" to embed a ring, and twisted 2a × a and
2a × a × a tori gain "up to an increase of 74% in accepted load" ([Cam10] abstract and Sec. 2). Twisted boundary
conditions in spin models ([search summary] of arXiv:2312.04468) twist the spins, not the lattice. 't Hooft's twisted
boundary conditions twist a gauge field and do not bear on the toy.

## 8. Exact lattice numbers for the field alone

For a field with energy (κ/2) Σ over links (φ_p − φ_q)² at coupling g, the covariance is (g/κ) L⁺ and so
⟨(φ_p − φ_q)²⟩ = (g/κ) R_pq, where R_pq is the resistance between p and q when every link is a unit resistor (*ours*,
standard Gaussian algebra, the brief's g L⁺ check written in another form). The resistances of infinite lattices are
published exactly ([Cse00]): square lattice, neighbors R/2 and across a square 2R/π, and at large separation
(R/π)(ln r + γ_E + ½ ln 8) with γ_E = 0.5772 (Eq. 33); simple cubic lattice, neighbors R/3, and 2G(0) = 0.505462 for the
resistance to infinity (Eq. 37), which is twice the potential at a unit point source, G(0) = 0.252731, with G(r) falling
as 1/(4πr). These give the toy's flat sheet and flat cube (large tori approach them) a check of the field kernel that is
independent of the toy's own Green's function code, and the 1/r shape of a sourced dip in one stroke.

## What this note contradicts or qualifies in the brief (ours)

1. **Option 1(b) is a stratified scalar theory, a published class with a theorem against it.** It does reproduce light
   bending and delay (γ = 1) and, with exponential clock and ruler factors, Mercury's perihelion (β = 1) ([BL18]), but
   only in its own rest frame; every such theory has α₁ = −8 against a measured |α₁| below about 4 × 10⁻⁵ ([WN72],
   [SW12]). The brief's sentence "reproduces the static measurements" needs "for bodies at rest in the toy's frame", and
   the preferred frame becomes a named risk.
2. **With waves (her answer to question 3), option 1(b) carries a scalar, breathing wave.** Pure scalar polarization is
   disfavored by a log₁₀ Bayes factor of −4.24 ± 0.30 ([LVC21]); orbital decay agrees with Einstein's quadrupole formula
   to about 6 × 10⁻⁵ ([Kra21]), not "a fraction of a percent"; dipole radiation is tightly bounded ([Fre12]). So the toy's
   scalar waves are a feature for piece 10 (a signal with one speed) and a liability for any claim about gravitational
   waves; the brief already says it cannot give the two polarizations, and this puts numbers on it.
3. **"A length per link" is not enough for gravitational waves on the toy's graph.** Linearized Regge calculus recovers
   Einstein's waves on a hypercubic lattice only with its diagonals ([Ham09], citing Roček and Williams); a cube's three
   edges per point cannot hold six metric components, and the cross polarization is a shear. The six-link version needs
   lengths on square diagonals (or an equivalent), which VISION Update 76's sentence on links should also reflect.
4. **Question 4(b) is published physics**, the stochastic Tolman–Ehrenfest relation ([Sme11] Eq. 26), and is correct for
   random walkers in a gravitational field; its strength corresponds to a temperature equal to the walker's rest energy,
   so it is weak compared with falling, unless the toy's walkers are meant to have no inertia.
5. **Isotropy of the wave speed** (the brief's "how it fails" for the signal job): on any regular lattice a discrete wave
   is isotropic only at long wavelengths; short pulses travel at direction-dependent speeds (general knowledge, the
   lattice dispersion; to verify). The test should be scored at wavelengths of several links, or the brief's failure
   condition will trip on the lattice rather than on the physics.
6. **Stress gravitates in Einstein's gravity** ([BCFVW05] Eqs. 24 to 28): a φ sourced only by curling energy leaves out the
   pressure potential. Small for slow, dilute relics; worth one line in the brief.
