# Where does "where" come from?

*A plain-language introduction. Draft 1. It describes what we are testing, not what we have found: no results exist yet.*

## The stage nobody questions

Almost every picture of the universe starts with a stage. Things happen *somewhere*. One place is near another place and far from a third. Even Einstein's general relativity, which lets the stage bend and stretch, still begins with a stage: space and time are the raw material, and physics describes what they do.

The standard history of the universe is told on that stage. About 13.8 billion years ago space was hot and dense, it expanded, and it cooled. Most cosmologists add an earlier chapter called inflation, a burst of extremely fast expansion that smoothed everything out. That story is well supported by evidence and nothing in this project disputes it.

What the standard story leaves alone is the stage itself. Why is there such a thing as "near" and "far" at all?

## A different starting point

A number of physicists take seriously the idea that space is not a basic ingredient. It may be something that *forms*, the way ice forms from water. This is a research direction, not an accepted fact, and nobody has made it work completely. Our project sits inside one small, concrete corner of it.

Here is the picture. Imagine a very large crowd of people, each holding a phone. At the start anyone can call anyone. There is no such thing as a neighbour, because everybody is one call away from everybody else. Asking "how far apart are these two people?" has no useful answer. That is the world before space: lots of relationships, no geometry.

Now suppose calls cost effort, and small closed loops of friends (you call her, she calls him, he calls you) are rewarding. As the crowd calms down, people keep only a few contacts, and the rewarding loops knit together into something like a net. In the net, some people are two steps away and others are two hundred. Distance has appeared. Nobody put it in. It came from the pattern of who is connected to whom.

Physicists who proposed this kind of model in 2006 gave the moment of knitting-together a name: **geometrogenesis**, the birth of geometry. In this picture the Big Bang is less like an explosion in space and more like a change of state, as when steam becomes water. It is the moment "where" started to mean something.

## The problem with the simple version

The model is attractive, and it has been simulated on computers. It runs into a stubborn difficulty that the original researchers found themselves.

Think about seating a party. With ten guests there are a manageable number of ways to be disorganised. With ten thousand guests who may each talk to *anyone*, the number of messy arrangements explodes far faster than the crowd grows. Disorder wins by sheer weight of numbers. Translated back into the model: the bigger the universe, the colder it must get before space can form, and for an endlessly large universe it never forms at all.

Researchers have suggested ways out. The universe might be finite. The rules might get stronger as the universe gets bigger. There might be long-range forces that discourage mess. Each has a cost.

## Our question

We ask about a fix that, as far as we have been able to find, has not been tried. What if the "before" was never a free-for-all? Suppose each person's phone only ever held a short contact list. Call that list their **menu**. Everything else in the model stays the same.

Working through the mathematics, we expect this to cut both ways, and that is the interesting part.

- If the contact lists are handed out **at random**, the mess is tamed, but so is everything else. Random short lists almost never contain the little closed loops that make the net worth forming. There is nothing to knit with. We expect no space to appear, however cold it gets.
- If the contact lists already have **local pattern** (my contacts tend to know each other, as in a real neighbourhood), then the net can form at an ordinary, fixed temperature, however large the crowd.

If that is right, it says something plain about this family of models: **you cannot get geometry from nothing. You can get it from a richer, more tangled geometry that settles into a simpler one.** The birth of space would be less like conjuring a statue out of mist and more like carving one from a block.

## What this is and is not

This is a toy model on a computer, with a few hundred to a few thousand points. It does not contain time as we experience it, or gravity, or matter. It cannot tell us whether the real universe began this way. What it can do is close one door cleanly and show what it costs to open another, in a setting small enough that anyone can rerun every number.

That is why all of the code, every assumption and its source, and the predictions we wrote down *before* running the experiment are published alongside this text. If the predictions fail, that will be in the paper too.
